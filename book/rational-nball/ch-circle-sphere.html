CHAPTER 02

The Circle, the Sphere, and the Cylinder

A polygon has an area we can calculate by finite arithmetic. A circle has a curved boundary, so a single polygon leaves something unaccounted for. Inscribed chords give a lower estimate; tangents give an upper one. The useful question is how to make the gap as small as we choose.

This turns the circle constant into a geometric computation. The same habit of enclosing a shape by simpler pieces will let us compare the sphere, cone and cylinder before we have a general integration theory.

Earlier context: Computable Numbers and Functions.

Area, length, and volume can be computed before defining an integral of a function. We begin with a concrete rational interval computation. We then establish the elementary rules of area and identify the computation with the quarter-circle. Throughout this chapter, the order of vertices determines orientation; area and volume retain that orientation.

2.1 A quarter-circle computation

Put

\[ P(u)=\left(\frac{1-u^2}{1+u^2},\frac{2u}{1+u^2}\right), \qquad u_j=\frac{j}{2^n}\quad(0\le j\le2^n). \]

All these coordinates are rational, and expansion gives \(P_1(u)^2+P_2(u)^2=1\). The endpoints are \((1,0)\) and \((0,1)\). The chords and endpoint tangents in the picture motivate the two expressions

\[ \ell(u,v)=\frac{(v-u)(1+uv)}{(1+u^2)(1+v^2)}, \qquad h(u,v)=\frac{v-u}{1+uv}. \]

For now, regard them simply as rational formulas. Define a raw computation by

\[ Q_n=\left[ \sum_{j=0}^{2^n-1}\ell(u_j,u_{j+1}), \sum_{j=0}^{2^n-1}h(u_j,u_{j+1}) \right]. \]

Its first stages are \(Q_0=[1/2,1]\) and \(Q_1=[7/10,5/6]\). The existing midpoint-update program computes exactly these sums; no new schedule or numerical definition of \(\pi\) is being substituted.

Validity of the computation.

Finite refinement increases the lower sum and decreases the upper sum. Furthermore

\[ h(u,v)-\ell(u,v)= \frac{(v-u)^3}{(1+uv)(1+u^2)(1+v^2)}. \]

On the chosen mesh each denominator is at least one. Summing the cell gaps therefore gives

\[ 0\le Q_n^+-Q_n^-\le2^n(2^{-n})^3=4^{-n}. \]

Thus the intervals are ordered, nested, and arbitrarily narrow: \(Q\) is a valid raw computable number. This conclusion uses finite rational formulas, not an area or integral theorem.

2.2 Area from ordered rational vectors

A point or vector is a pair of rational numbers. The area of the parallelogram spanned by the ordered vectors \(u,v\) is

\[ \omega(u,v)=u_1v_2-u_2v_1. \]

This is the normalized alternating bilinear rule: it is additive and rationally homogeneous in each input, vanishes when the two inputs agree, and has \(\omega((1,0),(0,1))=1\). Conversely those rules determine the formula uniquely by expansion in the coordinate basis. We construct this rule and prove its characterization; no new global axiom is introduced.

The area of the ordered triangle is

\[ A[p,q,r]=\frac12\omega(q-p,r-p). \]

A degenerate triangle has area zero. Exchanging two vertices reverses area; a cyclic change of its three vertices preserves it. For every rational point \(o\), expansion gives the subdivision identity

\[ A[p,q,r]=A[o,q,r]+A[p,o,r]+A[p,q,o]. \]

No condition that \(o\) lies inside the triangle is needed: orientation handles the cancellations.

For a rational affine map \(x\mapsto Mx+b\), the transformation law is

\[ A[Mp+b,Mq+b,Mr+b]=\det(M)A[p,q,r]. \]

Translations and proper rigid motions preserve area; reflections reverse it. A homothety of ratio \(s\) multiplies area by \(s^2\). These assertions follow from finite algebra, without lengths, angles, square roots, or trigonometry.

2.3 Polygons and the choice of starting vertex

A rational polygon is a cyclic sequence of rational points. In a stored list \(P=(p_0,\ldots,p_{N-1})\), the last vertex is joined back to the first; there is no preferred first vertex and no assumption of simplicity or convexity. Define

\[ A(P)=\frac12\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\omega(p_j,p_{j+1}), \qquad p_N=p_0. \]

The empty list has area zero. Reversing the cycle reverses its area.

Theorem: a fan from any starting vertex.

Bundled proof routes and LOC →

For any rational point \(o\),

\[ \boxed{A(P)=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}A[o,p_j,p_{j+1}].} \]

In particular, take \(o=p_0\). The first and last terms vanish, leaving

\[ A(P)=\sum_{j=1}^{N-2}A[p_0,p_j,p_{j+1}]. \]

Choosing any other starting vertex gives the same result. More generally, both the fan point and the place where the cyclic list starts may be changed independently, without changing the computed rational number.

The proof is cancellation of the radial edges: each \(\omega(o,p_j)\) occurs once with each sign. The boundary terms remain. For a convex polygon this is an ordinary triangulation. For other cycles it is an oriented triangle sum; the triangles need not be disjoint or remain inside the polygon. Self-crossing cycles therefore retain multiplicity rather than measuring the union of the regions they trace. This distinction requires no change to the formula or to the starting-vertex theorem.

The same convention for volume

For rational vectors in three dimensions put \(\Omega(u,v,w)=\det(u,v,w)\). The volume of the ordered tetrahedron is

\[ V[p,q,r,s]=\frac16\Omega(q-p,r-p,s-p). \]

Exchanging two vertices reverses volume, subdivision through any point is additive with the displayed orientations, and a homothety of ratio \(c\) multiplies volume by \(c^3\). These computations precede the solid comparisons later in this chapter. Spatial face areas and segment lengths may require square roots; planar polygon area and tetrahedral volume do not.

2.4 The geometric meaning of the computation

Return to the opening picture. The inner polygon has the origin followed by the consecutive points \(P(u_0),\ldots,P(u_{2^n})\). The outer polygon inserts between consecutive circle points the intersection of their tangent lines \(P(u_j)\cdot z=1\) and \(P(u_{j+1})\cdot z=1\). These intersections are rational. Both boundary cycles have the positive orientation shown in the picture.

Theorem: the interval endpoints are polygon areas.

Bundled proof routes and LOC →

At every stage, not merely in the limit,

\[ \boxed{Q_n=[A(P_n^-),A(P_n^+)].} \]

Either polygon area can be computed from a fan at any starting vertex, or indeed from any rational fan point. Its rational value is unchanged. The proof identifies the triangle and tangent-cell formulas with the already defined boundary-area computation.

The chords lie inside the quarter-disk and the tangent half-planes contain it. Thus these are inscribed and circumscribed polygons. Their proved area gap tends to zero. This is the area computation of the quarter-circle: we have first constructed a number and then justified what its bounds measure. No general area function on arbitrary regions is required.

Define \(\pi=4Q\). The same construction on \([0,t]\) gives sector area \(A(t)\), so \(Q=A(1)\). Finite polygon bounds give \(2<\pi<4\), and rational coordinate scaling gives the disk-area computation \(\pi R^2\). For later use,

\[ \frac{v-u}{1+v^2}\le A(v)-A(u)\le\frac{v-u}{1+u^2} \qquad(0\le u<v\le1). \]

In particular \((v-u)/2\le A(v)-A(u)\le v-u\). These are geometric bounds; the later integral constructions are unchanged and are not being redefined as areas in this revision.

The sphere and cylinder arguments below are coordinate reconstructions in the spirit of Archimedes' exhaustion, not transcriptions of his proofs. The historical references remain at the end of the chapter.

2.5 Circumference and sector length

Area and circumference are initially independent computations. Approximate the boundary by inscribed chords and circumscribed tangent segments. For a unit-circle chord of length \(c\), its midpoint is at distance \(d=\sqrt{1-c^2/4}\) from the origin. Twice its radial triangle area is \(cd\), and

\[ 0\le c-cd\le c^3/4. \]

If \(c_*\) is the largest chord, the difference between the inner perimeter and twice the inner area is at most \(c_*^2\) times the inner perimeter divided by \(4\). The perimeters have a fixed rational bound supplied by an outer square. The difference therefore shrinks.

For a polygon tangent to the unit circle, twice its area equals its perimeter, triangle by triangle. The inner and outer areas have already been proved to agree. The polygonal length computations consequently agree as well, and give circumference \(2\pi \). Scaling gives circumference \(2\pi R\) at radius \(R\).

The same comparison on a sector says that its arc length is twice its area divided by its radius. Additivity and invariance under rotation follow by finite polygonal refinements and the determinant identities. Computed coordinates are handled by rational enclosures and their finite error bounds. This proves the facts about arcs that the next chapter will use to introduce angle parameters.

2.6 Cylinders and cones by finite solid comparisons

Extruding the circle’s inner and outer polygons through a height \(H\) gives inner and outer prisms. Their volume gap is \(H\) times the planar area gap. Thus a circular cylinder has volume computation

\[ V_{\mathrm{cyl}}=\pi r^2H. \]

Extruding boundary polygons instead gives lateral-area computation \(2\pi rH\). Including both end disks gives total area \(2\pi rH+2\pi r^2\).

To compute a cone with apex at height zero, base radius \(r\), and height \(H\), cut its axis at \(z_k=kH/N\). In slab \(k\), the cone’s radius lies between \(rk/N\) and \(r(k+1)/N\). Inscribed and circumscribed cylinder stacks therefore have volumes

\[ C_N^- =\frac{\pi r^2H}{N^3}\sum _{k=0}^{N-1}k^2, \qquad C_N^+ =\frac{\pi r^2H}{N^3}\sum _{k=1}^{N}k^2. \]

These are finite solid containments, not a theorem about infinitesimal slices. Each circular cylinder can itself be replaced by polygonal prisms with an arbitrarily small additional error.

The identity \(\sum _{k=1}^{N}k^2=N(N+1)(2N+1)/6\) follows by induction (or by summing successive cubes). Consequently

\[ C_N^\pm =\pi r^2H\left(\frac13\mathbin {\pm }\frac1{2N} +\frac1{6N^2}\right). \]

Here the minus sign gives \(C_N^-\) and the plus sign gives \(C_N^+\). Their gap is \(\pi r^2H/N\). Refining, for example with \(N=2^n\), gives nested solid bounds, and proves

\[ \boxed {V_{\mathrm{cone}}=\frac13\pi r^2H.} \]

All occurrences of \(\pi \) in finite bounds are evaluated by its existing rational interval computation. The displayed expressions abbreviate finite arithmetic on these enclosures.

2.7 The hemisphere and the cone fill the cylinder in the bounds

Fix a positive rational radius \(R\). Use height \(z\) above the equatorial plane. The upper hemisphere has squared sectional radius \(R^2-z^2\). Compare it with the cone whose sectional radius is \(z\), both inside the cylinder of radius and height \(R\).

For a rational partition \(0=z_0{\lt}\cdots {\lt}z_N=R\), put \(\Delta z_i=z_{i+1}-z_i\). Cylinder stacks bounding the hemisphere have volumes

\[ H_P^-=\pi \sum _i(R^2-z_{i+1}^2)\Delta z_i, \qquad H_P^+=\pi \sum _i(R^2-z_i^2)\Delta z_i. \]

The corresponding cone stacks have volumes

\[ C_P^-=\pi \sum _i z_i^2\Delta z_i, \qquad C_P^+=\pi \sum _i z_{i+1}^2\Delta z_i. \]

At every finite partition there are exact identities

\[ \boxed {H_P^-+C_P^+=\pi R^3, \qquad H_P^++C_P^-=\pi R^3.} \]

Moreover their common gap is at most

\[ \pi \max _i\Delta z_i\sum _i(z_{i+1}^2-z_i^2) =\pi R^2\max _i\Delta z_i. \]

Thus the independently bounded hemisphere and cone volumes add to the cylinder volume. The cone calculation gives the hemisphere volume \(2\pi R^3/3\), and reflection gives

Theorem 2.1 Sphere volume

The sphere’s solid-volume computation is

\[ \boxed {V_R=\frac43\pi R^3.} \]

It is two-thirds of the volume of the circumscribed cylinder of radius \(R\) and height \(2R\).

2.8 A conical frustum’s area is another polygonal computation

Rotate a segment with endpoint distances \(r,r'\) from the axis and axial height \(h\). Its length is \(\ell =\sqrt{h^2+(r-r')^2}\). We compute the lateral area of the resulting frustum from planar facets, before measuring the sphere’s surface.

Choose an inner polygon of the unit circle and the outer polygon given by its endpoint tangents. Let their areas be \(a^-,a^+\), their perimeters \(p^-,p^+\), and let the inner chord lengths be \(b_j\) with midpoint distances \(d_j=\sqrt{1-b_j^2/4}\). Scale these two polygons by \(r\) and \(r'\) in the two endpoint planes and join corresponding edges.

An inner trapezoid has area

\[ T_j=\frac{r+r'}2 b_j\sqrt{h^2+d_j^2(r-r')^2}. \]

Its slant height lies between \(d_j\ell \) and \(\ell \). Thus its total area \(F^-\) satisfies

\[ (r+r')\ell a^- =\frac{r+r'}2\ell \sum _j b_jd_j \le F^-\le \frac{r+r'}2\ell p^-. \]

Every outer polygon edge has distance \(1\) from the axis in its base plane. The transverse slant height of the associated outer trapezoid is therefore exactly \(\ell \). Its total area is

\[ F^+=\frac{r+r'}2\ell p^+=(r+r')\ell a^+. \]

The planar comparisons \(p^-\le 2\pi \) and \(a^-\le \pi \le a^+\) imply

\[ F^-\le \pi (r+r')\ell \le F^+, \qquad F^+-F^-\le (r+r')\ell (a^+-a^-). \]

Refinement constructs a unique lateral-area computation and proves

\[ \boxed {\operatorname {Area}(\text{frustum})=\pi (r+r’)\ell .} \]

The case \(r'=0\) is the cone’s lateral area \(\pi r\ell \); the case \(r=r'\) is the cylinder’s lateral area. Degenerate zero-area cases are read directly. This proof used only planar trapezoids and the circle’s exhaustion, not an unproved formula for a surface of revolution.

2.9 The finite chord identity behind the sphere’s surface

Use coordinates \((r,z)\) in a meridian half-plane, with \(r\ge 0\). Take consecutive points

\[ p=(r,z),\qquad q=(r',z'),\qquad r^2+z^2=(r')^2+(z')^2=R^2,\qquad z{\gt}z'. \]

Exclude the single diameter joining both poles. Set

\[ \Delta z=z-z',\quad \ell =|p-q|,\quad m=(p+q)/2,\quad d=|m|=\sqrt{R^2-\ell ^2/4}{\gt}0. \]

The midpoint vector is perpendicular to the chord. Its radial coordinate is \((r+r')/2\), so elementary similar right triangles give

\[ \frac{r+r'}2\ell =d\Delta z. \]

Rotating the chord gives an inner frustum of area

\[ L(p,q)=\pi (r+r')\ell =2\pi d\Delta z. \]

For an outer comparison, take the parallel tangent to the circle at \(Rm/d\) and truncate it by the same two horizontal planes. Its endpoint radii exceed \(r,r'\) by the same amount

\[ k=(R-d)\frac{\ell }{\Delta z}. \]

The slant length is still \(\ell \), so the tangent frustum has area

\[ U(p,q)=2\pi \left(d\Delta z+(R-d)\frac{\ell ^2}{\Delta z}\right). \]

These are actual finite frustum computations. Since \(d\le R\) and \(\ell \ge \Delta z\), their comparison is

\[ \boxed {L(p,q)\le 2\pi R\Delta z\le U(p,q).} \]

The middle quantity is the independently computed area of the cylinder band of radius \(R\) between the same planes.

There is also a uniform error bound, including near the poles. The nonnegative radii give

\[ (r-r')^2\le |r^2-(r')^2|\le 2R\Delta z, \qquad \ell ^2\le 4R\Delta z. \]

Using \(R-d=\ell ^2/(4(R+d))\) therefore yields

\[ \boxed {U(p,q)-L(p,q) =\frac{\pi \ell ^4}{2(R+d)\Delta z}\le 2\pi \ell ^2.} \]

This explicit estimate, not an assertion about arbitrary triangulations of a surface, will prove convergence.

2.10 Sphere and zone surface areas by exhaustion

For a full meridian take, on its northern half,

\[ (r(t),z(t))= R\left(\frac{2t}{1+t^2},\frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2}\right), \qquad t=j/N,\quad 0\le j\le N, \]

and reflect in the equatorial plane for the southern half. These are rational points. Adjacent northern chord lengths satisfy the exact identity

\[ \ell (u,v)^2= \frac{4R^2(v-u)^2}{(1+u^2)(1+v^2)}\le \frac{4R^2}{N^2}. \]

The reflected chords have the same bound. Write \(\ell _*\) for the largest chord, so \(\ell _*\le 2R/N\). Their total length is at most the meridian semicircle length \(\pi R\), already computed from polygons.

Let \(L_P\) be the sum of inner frustum areas and \(U_P\) the sum of the separate tangent-frustum areas. The latter need not join along a common parallel; its validity is the finite comparison below, not a general claim that containment of surfaces orders their areas. Adding the chord identities and the error estimates gives

\[ L_P\le 4\pi R^2\le U_P, \qquad U_P-L_P\le 2\pi \ell _*\sum _i\ell _i \le 2\pi ^2R\ell _*\le \frac{4\pi ^2R^2}{N}. \]

Thus \(64R^2/N\) is a rational upper bound for the geometric gap. All these intervals overlap, independently of the chosen meridian subdivisions. Compute the finitely many lengths, tangents, and areas to any further prescribed rational error and take finite-prefix intersections. This constructs the sphere’s surface-area computation, independently of its volume, and proves

Theorem 2.2 Sphere area

The sphere’s surface-area computation is

\[ \boxed {\mathcal S_R=4\pi R^2.} \]

It equals the lateral area of the circumscribed cylinder, and equals four times the area of a great-circle disk.

At the coarsest stage, the meridian consists of the two chords from the north pole to the equator and then to the south pole. Its bounds are

\[ 2\sqrt2\, \pi R^2\le \mathcal S_R \le (8-2\sqrt2)\pi R^2. \]

Further rational subdivisions improve these bounds by the proved schedule.

The same proof works between any two fixed parallel planes with heights \(z_0{\lt}z_1\). The chord heights telescope to \(H=z_1-z_0\) instead of \(2R\). One may take rational heights and compute the endpoint radii by square roots. With height mesh \(\delta \), the earlier bound gives \(\ell _*\le \sqrt{4R\delta }\), so refinement terminates even at a pole. The construction proves

\[ \boxed {\operatorname {Area}(\text{spherical zone of height }H) =2\pi RH.} \]

In particular a cap of height \(h\) has area \(2\pi Rh\). The squared straight-line distance from its pole to a point of its rim is \(2Rh\). The cap therefore has the area of the planar disk with that chord as radius, another of Archimedes’ comparisons.

Adding the two end disks to the circumscribed cylinder gives total area \(6\pi R^2\). Thus both the sphere’s volume and its surface area are two-thirds of those of the cylinder including its bases. Lateral area alone instead equals the sphere’s area. These are distinct statements.

2.11 Caps and solid sectors

The same finite sum identities also compute a spherical cap’s volume. Let \(0\le h\le 2R\) be rational and measure depth \(t\) from the north pole. The squared section radius is \(q(t)=2Rt-t^2\). For \(t,u\in [0,2R]\),

\[ |q(t)-q(u)|=|t-u|\, |2R-t-u|\le 2R|t-u|. \]

On \(N\) equal depth slabs of thickness \(\delta =h/N\), enclose squared radii between \(\max (0,q(j\delta )-2R\delta )\) and \(q(j\delta )+2R\delta \). The associated cylinder stacks give volume bounds whose gap is at most \(4\pi R h\delta \).

Their reference sum, obtained using \(q(j\delta )\), is exactly

\[ \pi \delta \sum _{j=0}^{N-1}q(j\delta ) =\pi \left[R h^2\left(1-\frac1N\right) -h^3\left(\frac13-\frac1{2N}+\frac1{6N^2}\right)\right]. \]

This uses only the finite sums of integers and squares. Its difference from \(\pi (Rh^2-h^3/3)\) is bounded by \(\pi (Rh^2/N+h^3/(2N)+h^3/(6N^2))\), while either bounding stack differs from the reference sum by at most \(2\pi R h\delta \). These bounds construct the cap volume and prove

\[ \boxed {V_{\mathrm{cap}}=\pi h^2\left(R-\frac h3\right).} \]

For \(h=0\) use the zero computation. For \(h=2R\) it agrees with the earlier, independent full-sphere computation.

For \(0\le h\le R\), the solid sector joining the cap to the center is the cap together with a cone of height \(R-h\) and squared base radius \(2Rh-h^2\). The cone formula and finite additivity give

\[ V_{\mathrm{sector}} =\pi h^2(R-h/3)+\frac\pi 3(2Rh-h^2)(R-h) =\frac23\pi R^2h =\frac R3\operatorname {Area}(\text{cap}). \]

For \(R{\lt}h\le 2R\), subtract the opposite smaller sector from the sphere; the same formula results. Taking the whole sphere gives \(V_R=R\mathcal S_R/3\), now as a consequence of two independent geometric computations rather than a definition of either quantity.

2.12 A sphere computation by rational polytope refinement

Write \(B_n^+(R)=\{x:x_i\ge0,\ \sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2\le R^2\}\). We construct a particular nested rational interval computation from finite convex polytopes in the positive orthant. Its stage values come from refining the preceding stage. The symbol \(v_n\) will name the value produced by this computation after multiplying the orthant bounds by \(2^n\). No sphere volume is assumed in advance, and no general definition of volume for regions bounded by surfaces is introduced. Only finite rational polytopes have a volume function here; the formula-agreement argument is a separate theorem.

The finite volume axioms come first

We keep the chapter’s orientation convention: reversing orientation negates volume. In each positive dimension, volume is finitely additive under oriented dissections, invariant under translations and linear maps of determinant one, and normalized on the standard oriented cube by

\[ \operatorname{Vol}_n([0,1]^n)=1. \]

Dissection additivity applies when intersections have dimension below the ambient dimension. It follows that lower-dimensional pieces have zero volume: applying this additivity to a lower-dimensional piece and itself gives \(V=V+V\), hence \(V=0\). Nonnegativity and containment monotonicity are derived below from rational simplex dissections; they are not additional volume axioms. All balls and enclosing polytopes below use that orientation.

Rational boxes. Partition the unit cube into congruent boxes using a common positive integer denominator. Translation invariance and finite additivity give every small box its rational product of side lengths. Cutting and assembling these boxes proves

\[ \operatorname{Vol}_n\!\left(\prod_{i=1}^n[a_i,b_i]\right) =\prod_{i=1}^n(b_i-a_i),\qquad a_i,b_i\in\mathbb Q,\quad a_i\le b_i. \]

Rational stretching is a consequence. For \(q\in\mathbb Q_{>0}\), let \(D_q=\operatorname{diag}(q,1,\ldots,1)\). Then

\[ \operatorname{Vol}_n(D_qP)=q\operatorname{Vol}_n(P). \]

Here is the justification without assuming a determinant transformation law. Enclose a rational polytope \(P\subset[-C,C]^n\) by the rational grid boxes of side \(1/N\) that meet it, and inscribe the boxes wholly inside it. The remaining boxes meet its finitely many facet hyperplanes. For a hyperplane \(a\cdot x=b\), choose a coordinate with maximal \(|a_i|\). Over each grid column in the other coordinates the hyperplane meets at most \(n+2\) boxes. If there are \(F\) facets and \(C\ge1\) is rational, the total volume gap is at most

\[ \frac{F(n+2)(2C+2)^{n-1}}{N}. \]

The stretched boxes have exactly \(q\) times the original box volumes, with the same finite incidence relations. Their gap is at most \(q\) times this bound. Both sides of the claimed identity therefore agree to every prescribed rational error. All box classification is finite rational linear feasibility; there is no existence claim based on a completed scalar field.

The simplex formula is proved, not stipulated

Let \(\Delta_n=\{x_i\ge0:\sum_i x_i\le1\}\). The unit cube is dissected into the \(n!\) ordered-coordinate simplices

\[ 0\le x_{\sigma(1)}\le\cdots\le x_{\sigma(n)}\le1, \qquad \sigma\in S_n. \]

Each has vertices given by the successive terminal sums of coordinate basis vectors. Order these vertices to give the orientation inherited from the cube. The resulting edge matrix has determinant one: when the first ordering has determinant negative one, exchange two vertices. Thus every oriented piece is a determinant-one affine image of the standard oriented simplex. The stated invariances give equal volumes to all pieces, so additivity and cube normalization imply

\[ \operatorname{Vol}_n(\Delta_n)=\frac1{n!}. \]

For rational vertices \(p_0,\ldots,p_n\), put \(A=[p_1-p_0\ \cdots\ p_n-p_0]\). If \(\det A>0\), put \(q=\det A\). Then \(A=S D_q\) with \(\det S=1\), so rational stretching and the given invariances give volume \(\det A/n!\). If \(\det A<0\), exchange two vertices: both the volume and determinant change sign, reducing to the positive case. Consequently, with orientation given by the displayed vertex order,

\[ \boxed{\operatorname{Vol}_n\bigl(\operatorname{conv}(p_0,\ldots,p_n)\bigr) =\frac{\det(p_1-p_0,\ldots,p_n-p_0)}{n!}.} \]

If the determinant vanishes, the simplex is lower-dimensional and has volume zero. Thus the same formula covers degenerate simplices. A positively oriented rational convex polytope is triangulated by triangulating its faces and coning them to the rational mean of its vertices. Recursion in dimension gives finitely many consistently oriented rational simplices. The axioms prove that the sum of their determinant volumes is independent of the triangulation. This establishes exact rational polytope volume before we use it to bound a ball.

What is the polytope here?

For this construction, a finite rational vertex set and its convex hull suffice for the inner body. The outer body is a bounded intersection of finitely many rational tangent and coordinate halfspaces. These describe the geometric bodies; a triangulation is a way to compute their volumes, rather than the definition of the bodies. Both carry the standard ambient orientation. Finite differences and unions used in the proof are polytopal regions, dissected into consistently oriented convex pieces.

Mathlib’s generic convexHull can describe the hull on rational coordinates. We do not need a new general polytope type for this example. Rational coordinates provide the exact finite geometry and its dense input domain; interpreting the same inequalities on valid represented coordinates is a separate bridge. Mathlib’s set description supplies no volume theorem for our foundation. In the checked rational layer, points have type Fin n → ℚ, the inner body uses convexHull ℚ, and a finite halfspace presentation uses rational normals and rational offsets. The sphere’s outer body is the special case with offset one. In the positive orthant, retaining all positive coordinate-axis tangents and the coordinate planes bounds every outer coordinate between \(0\) and \(1\).

One rational chart in the positive orthant

For \(n\ge2\), take \(t\in\mathbb Q^{n-1}\), put \(s=\sum_i t_i^2\), and use

\[ \sigma(t)=\frac{(2t_1,\ldots,2t_{n-1},\ 1-s)}{1+s}, \qquad t_i\ge0,\quad s\le1. \]

The chart covers the whole spherical boundary in the positive orthant. Expansion gives \(\|\sigma(t)\|^2=1\) exactly, and every coordinate is nonnegative. The parameter domain is bounded. At stage \(k\), every parameter coordinate uses the same subdivision denominator \(M_k=2^k\): take \(t_i=j_i/M_k\), with \(0\le j_i\le M_k\), retaining tuples with \(\sum_i j_i^2\le M_k^2\).

Include the corresponding samples on every coordinate face recursively. In dimension one the sample is the unit endpoint. These face samples matter: in the 3D case, a dyadic parameter grid has only axis endpoints on its circular parameter boundary. Without the separate face charts, the inscribed polygon on that coordinate face would never improve. Write \(S_k\) for the resulting finite set, including all positive axis points. The origin is added to the inner hull, not to the spherical sample set.

Refinement evaluates only new chart-grid tuples—those with at least one odd numerator at the doubled denominator—and the new face-grid tuples. It retains all preceding samples and removes duplicate points. Thus \(S_{k-1}\subset S_k\) by construction, rather than by rebuilding a new approximation independently.

The two finite convex polytopes

\[ P_k=\operatorname{conv}(\{0\}\cup S_k),\qquad Q_k=\{x:x_i\ge0\}\cap\bigcap_{p\in S_k}\{x:p\cdot x\le1\}. \]

Every sampled point supplies its rational tangent plane, including the axis points. The coordinate planes through the origin supply the flat faces. Consequently \(P_k\subset B_n^+(1)\subset Q_k\). Axis tangents give \(x_i\le1\), so the outer polytope is bounded at every stage. At stage zero, \(P_0\) is the axis simplex and \(Q_0\) is the unit cube in the positive orthant:

\[ \ell_0=\operatorname{Vol}_n(P_0)=\frac1{n!},\qquad u_0=\operatorname{Vol}_n(Q_0)=1. \]

The reusable ConvexPolytope.volume() function accepts rational vertices, or a bounded rational halfspace presentation with a supplied strict rational interior point. It triangulates into rational simplices in the standard ambient orientation and adds their determinant volumes. Each simplex is ordered to have positive determinant; reversing the body orientation negates the answer. The caller does not track the individual orientation signs. The function is implemented for finite dimensions; facet enumeration in higher dimensions is exact but can be expensive.

A stage extends the preceding stage

The evaluator retains the current vertices, facets, sample set, and rational endpoints. Process each new sample \(p\) once. For the inner hull, find the old facets visible from \(p\). The added region consists of the pyramids from those facets to \(p\); triangulate these pyramids and add their volumes. Update the hull along its horizon. For the outer body, clip the current polytope by \(p\cdot x\le1\). Triangulate the removed cap and subtract its volume. The coordinate and axis planes remain in place.

\[ \ell_k=\ell_{k-1}+\sum_{p\in S_k\setminus S_{k-1}}\delta_p^{\mathrm{in}},\qquad u_k=u_{k-1}-\sum_{p\in S_k\setminus S_{k-1}}\delta_p^{\mathrm{out}}, \qquad \delta_p^{\mathrm{in}},\delta_p^{\mathrm{out}}\ge0. \]

Each increment or cap is taken relative to the body after processing the preceding new point. This makes the pieces disjoint in their interiors and avoids counting any region twice. In the 3D implementation, the facet polygons themselves are updated and clipped. The stage evaluator never recomputes either whole-body volume after initialization. The independent certificate checker does compare the results with fresh polytope triangulations; that is validation, not the stage algorithm.

The particular interval computation is

\[ I_k=[\,2^n\ell_k,\ 2^n u_k\,],\qquad I_k\subset I_{k-1}. \]

Reflections of the orthant pieces are given the standard ambient orientation before their contributions are added. Thus their contributions add with factor \(2^n\); there is no cancellation from the determinant signs of the reflection maps. No volume is assigned to a curved region in order to run this computation.

Why this particular computation converges

The finite chart identity

\[ \|\sigma(t)-\sigma(u)\|^2 =\frac{4\|t-u\|^2}{(1+\|t\|^2)(1+\|u\|^2)} \le4\|t-u\|^2 \]

gives the precision schedule. Rounding each nonnegative parameter down to the grid preserves \(\sum_i t_i^2\le1\) and gives squared sampling distance at most \(4(n-1)/M_k^2\). The recursive face samples give the same estimate on every coordinate face. Put \(\eta=2(n-1)/M_k^2\) and \(a=1-\eta>0\).

For the inner containment, consider a facet normal and the coordinate face supported by its positive components. The nearby sample on that same face has no contribution from the negative components. The identity \(p\cdot w=1-\|p-w\|^2/2\) for unit directions then gives \(aB_n^+(1)\subset P_k\). For the outer containment, apply the same identity to the nonnegative direction of a point of \(Q_k\); a sampled tangent gives \(Q_k\subset a^{-1}B_n^+(1)\). Hence

\[ Q_k\subset a^{-2}P_k,\qquad 0\le u_k-\ell_k\le a^{-2n}-1. \]

Only the polytope scaling law and \(P_k\subset[0,1]^n\) are used to obtain this gap. There is no sphere-volume formula in the estimate. The finite Bernoulli inequality gives, whenever \(\eta\le1/(4n)\),

\[ \boxed{|I_k|\le\frac{2^{n+3}n(n-1)}{M_k^2}.} \]

The nested rational intervals therefore produce a valid computable number \(v_n\), with an explicit stopping rule. This is a sphere-specific computation, not a general extension of the polytope volume function to arbitrary surfaces. Agreement with the formula below is proved separately by finite geometric comparisons. Dimension one already gives the exact interval \([2,2]\); the zero-dimensional computation is the constant \(1\).

The general-dimensional formula

The value of the particular interval computation just constructed satisfies

\[ \boxed{v_{n+2}=\frac{2\pi}{n+2}\,v_n,\qquad v_0=1,\quad v_1=2.} \]

Here \(\pi=v_2\) is the disk area already established in this chapter. Induction gives, for every nonnegative integer \(k\) and nonnegative radius \(R\),

\[ \boxed{V_{2k}(R)=\frac{\pi^k}{k!}R^{2k},\qquad V_{2k+1}(R)=\frac{2^{k+1}\pi^k}{1\cdot3\cdot5\cdots(2k+1)}R^{2k+1}.} \]

The odd denominator is one when \(k=0\). In particular, \(V_2(R)=\pi R^2\), \(V_3(R)=4\pi R^3/3\), \(V_4(R)=\pi^2R^4/2\), and \(V_5(R)=8\pi^2R^5/15\). Here \(V_n(R)\) denotes this specific radius-scaled computation, not a general curved-region volume operator. Rational radii scale every vertex and determinant exactly. An arbitrary valid represented radius is handled by its nonnegative rational lower and upper bounds; refine those bounds and the unit-ball polytopes together. The finite power identity below controls the scaling error and proves representation independence.

Proof of the recurrence by finite dissections

Fix \(n\ge1\) and write coordinates in dimension \(n+2\) as \((x,y)\), with \(x\) in dimension \(n\) and \(y\) in dimension two. Set \(t_j=j/N\) and \(h=1/N\). Over the radial shell \(t_j\le\|x\|\le t_{j+1}\), the permitted squared radius of the disk in the last two coordinates lies between \(1-t_{j+1}^2\) and \(1-t_j^2\). Thus finite shell–disk products give

\[ v_n\pi A_N\le v_{n+2}\le v_n\pi B_N, \]
\[ A_N=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}(1-t_{j+1}^2)(t_{j+1}^n-t_j^n),\qquad B_N=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}(1-t_j^2)(t_{j+1}^n-t_j^n). \]

These comparisons between the particular computations are justified by rational polytopal dissections, rather than a general definition of curved-region volume or a general principle about cross-sections. Approximate each nested ball by its inner and outer polytopes, form shell differences by cutting their finitely many rational faces, and triangulate. For the disk radii, use rational lower and upper square-root bounds, then rational disk polygons. Products are rational polytopal regions, whose volumes multiply: prove this first for grid boxes, then squeeze each factor by the boundary-box estimate above. For each fixed finite partition, these constructions make the additional error smaller than any prescribed rational tolerance. Disjoint shell products add, and their inner and outer containment gives the displayed bounds. Thus a curved shell or disk is never assigned an uncomputed exact rational volume.

Finite summation by parts, with zero endpoint terms, gives

\[ A_N=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}t_j^n(t_{j+1}^2-t_j^2) =2h\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}t_j^{n+1}+h^2\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}t_j^n. \]

For \(0\le a\le b\), factor the difference of powers:

\[ b^m-a^m=(b-a)\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}b^{m-1-i}a^i. \]

Every term lies between \(a^{m-1}\) and \(b^{m-1}\). With \(m=n+2\), summing over the partition telescopes to one. The difference between the upper and lower power sums is exactly \(h\), so

\[ \frac1{n+2}-h\le h\sum_j t_j^{n+1}\le\frac1{n+2}. \]

The remaining term of \(A_N\) is between zero and \(h\). Also

\[ 0\le B_N-A_N =\sum_j(t_{j+1}^2-t_j^2)(t_{j+1}^n-t_j^n)\le2h. \]

Consequently both bounds differ from \(2/(n+2)\) by at most \(3/N\). Since \(v_n\pi\le2^{n+2}\), the geometric volume differs from \(2\pi v_n/(n+2)\) by at most \(3\cdot2^{n+2}/N\), in addition to the freely chosen rational polytope approximation error. Refinement to every rational tolerance proves the exact recurrence. For \(n=0\), the same recurrence is simply \(v_2=\pi\), already established. Repeated substitution and finite induction give the two closed formulas.

Only rational determinants, finite dissections, square-root brackets and power identities have been used. No notion of an integral occurs in the argument. In the later Gamma-function chapter we will give a second proof and show that it agrees with this geometric computation.

2.13 What precedes calculus

Only finitely many polygons, prisms, cylinders, and planar frusta occur at any stage. The operations are determinants, square roots, rational sums, and interval arithmetic. Inner–outer gaps supply the needed number computations. The first cone and sphere sums have been evaluated by finite integer identities, not by antiderivatives. Surface area has its own facet construction, not the rule of differentiating volume with respect to radius.

The next chapter introduces trigonometry by using sector area as an angle coordinate. Only afterwards do we define a general rectangle integral and identify these particular geometric exhaustions as examples of it. That later organization explains and reuses the geometry; it is not a prerequisite for the results proved here.
