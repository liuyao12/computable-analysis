Worked strategy test: normalized sine and power-series sine

The requested comparison has different coordinates. The primary normalized sine takes \(r\in\mathbb Q\) and computes \(\sin(\pi r)\). The primary power-series sine takes \(z\in\mathbb Q(i)\) and computes \[ S(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n z^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!}. \] Its implementation pairs the odd terms of exponential prefixes; the checked coefficient identities justify this description. Comparing equal rational parameter numerals would compare different angles. The intended real extension identity is \[ \widehat F(x)\simeq\widehat S(\pi x). \] Here the hats denote derived evaluation, not a change of the primary domains.

ODE uniqueness: the better characterization for independent constructions

For two independently constructed radian sine/cosine pairs, compare actual solutions of \[ \frac{d}{dt}\begin{pmatrix}s\\c\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}s\\c\end{pmatrix}, \qquad (s(0),c(0))=(0,1). \] This first-order system avoids needing a separately packaged second derivative. Equivalently, sine satisfies \(y''=-y\), \(y(0)=0\), \(y'(0)=1\).

In the normalized coordinate, both aligned constructions must instead satisfy \[ f''=-\pi^2 f,\qquad f(0)=0,\qquad f'(0)=\pi. \] The first-order form is \(f'=\pi c\), \(c'=-\pi f\), with the same initial pair. One must justify the derivatives and the coordinate chain rule; initial values alone do not prove agreement.

The complete application is checked in ElementarySineODESolutions. For every valid represented complex frequency \(a\), it constructs two actual solutions of \(s'=ac\), \(c'=-as\), with initial pair \((0,1)\): the paired scalar entire exponentials at \(iaz\) and \(-iaz\), and the independently computed rotation-matrix Taylor sum. Holomorphicity, both derivative laws and both initial values are proved from the constructions. The central computations_agree_global proof compares them using those laws, rather than using an existing sine identity.

EntireConstantODEUniqueness multiplies each solution by the actual inverse matrix exponential and proves zero derivative. Checked local ODE uniqueness gives constancy neighborhoods; the finite rational open-cover argument in HolomorphicPathConstancy propagates constancy along the constructed affine path from the origin to any endpoint. Entire-domain membership holds at every path parameter. No small endpoint restriction, assumed uniqueness provider, caller-supplied cover or assumed final comparison is required.

ElementarySineODEComparison then proves, on the entire represented complex plane, \[ \widehat F(z)\simeq\widehat S(\pi z). \] The coefficient \(\pi\) is the repo's valid computed geometric pi, with no rationality assumption. Matrix Taylor parameter scaling justifies the actual coordinate change. Separate adapters connect the entire computations to the primary normalized function on \(\mathbb Q\) and the primary power-series function on \(\mathbb Q(i)\). For every prescribed positive rational error, powerSeries_primary_converges and sinPi_primary_converges prove eventual closeness of those primary values at computed rational centers/midpoints to the derived value. Holomorphic continuity moduli and shrinking input-box errors supply the schedules, so irrational evaluation is justified from the primary computations.

The primary power-series adapter still uses the established finite odd-prefix identity to identify its exponential formula, then the new ODE proof to identify the matrix Taylor computation. Thus the ODE core is an independent comparison route; this does not turn the existing normalized implementation into an independently constructed geometric sine.

The older ScalarODEUniqueness module is different: its self-derivative uniqueness provider is still a conditional input. It is not a ready-made harmonic-oscillator uniqueness theorem.

Why the identity theorem is less helpful in this test

The identity theorem requires holomorphic extensions in a common coordinate and an exact local seed. Agreement only at the origin is insufficient. The physical sets \(\pi\mathbb Q\) and \(\mathbb Q\) have not supplied an accumulating common set of agreed nonzero points.

An ODE can supply the missing seed through its initial-value uniqueness, or through its recurrence determining every Taylor coefficient. A coefficient argument additionally needs actual local analytic reconstruction. The finite prefix route already proves the present concrete comparison, so adding holomorphic identity propagation would not remove a proof obligation.

For a future independent geometric sine, prefer a shared ODE and initial normalization when its derivative evidence is easiest to construct. Prefer the identity theorem when exact accumulating-set agreement or a local analytic identity is already available. This is a choice based on the constructions, not a permanent ranking of the methods.

Independent test: geometric circle versus power-series sine

The new primary geometric object uses a rational stereographic slope, rather than a rational radian angle: \[ P(t)=\left(\frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2},\frac{2t}{1+t^2}\right),\qquad t\in\mathbb Q. \] Its sine and cosine are these literal rational circle coordinates. The geometric object and angle algorithm contain no exponential or power-series computation in their checked definition closures. Polygonal sector areas compute the coordinate add-on \(\theta(t)=2\operatorname{atan}_{\mathrm{geom}}(t)\) on \(0\le t\le1\). For larger positive slopes the algorithm uses \(\theta(t)=2\theta(1)-\theta(1/t)\); negative slopes use reflection. The geometric identity \(2\theta(1)\simeq\pi\) is the existing area construction of \(\pi\). All rational slopes are covered.

This is an independent construction test: there is no common exponential definition to compare by odd-prefix algebra. The useful equation in its actual coordinate is \[ P'(t)=\frac{2i}{1+t^2}P(t),\qquad P(0)=1. \] The geometric derivative evidence and explicit rational residuals justify the finite Euler recurrence \[ p_{k+1}=p_k+h\frac{2i}{1+(kh)^2}p_k,\qquad p_0=1. \] The geometry computation and the analytic rotation solution each have a constructed error bound against this same mesh. For the analytic solution, local exponential remainders, finite-product stability and converging angular rectangle sums supply the bound. The geometric bound comes from the actual rational circle residual and stability. These are intermediate quantitative solution proofs, not agreement fields assumed in a certificate.

The common-Euler uniqueness law then concludes exact agreement from \[ \|u-p_N\|\le C_u\varepsilon_N,\qquad \|v-p_N\|\le C_v\varepsilon_N,\qquad\varepsilon_N\longrightarrow0. \] This is uniqueness for these supplied, justified calculations. It is not a new abstract variable-coefficient ODE existence theorem. Exponential is an intermediate analytic solution in the proof; it does not define the geometric primary function. The whole-plane oscillator uniqueness theorem separately identifies that solution's sine coordinate with the matrix Taylor computation. The established adapter and convergence theorem connect the matrix computation to the actual primary power-series sine on \(\mathbb Q(i)\).

The checked comparison therefore proves, for every rational slope, \[ \frac{2t}{1+t^2}\simeq\widehat S(\theta(t)). \] For every positive rational tolerance it also proves eventual closeness of \(S(q_n)\) to the geometric sine, where \(q_n\in\mathbb Q(i)\) are the centers of the computed angle boxes. The hat remains a derived evaluation from primary rational inputs. The exact primary-to-primary theorem retains the premise that the two coordinates describe the same angle.

For example, the slope \(t=1/2\) gives the exact value \(\widehat S(2\operatorname{atan}_{\mathrm{geom}}(1/2))\simeq4/5\). The reciprocal slope \(t=2\) gives the same sine coordinate at the reflected angle \(2\theta(1)-2\operatorname{atan}_{\mathrm{geom}}(1/2)\). These are specializations of the checked all-rational theorem, not numerical evaluations.
