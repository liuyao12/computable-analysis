Highlights: near-integer exponentials

Two checked Lean theorems give certified errors for exponential values remarkably close to integers.

The discriminant \(-43\) example

\[\begin{aligned}\exp(\pi\sqrt{43})&=884736744-\delta_{43},\\10^{-4}&\le\delta_{43}\le3\cdot10^{-4}.\end{aligned}\]

The value lies just below the integer, by between one and three ten-thousandths.

The discriminant \(-163\) example

\[\begin{aligned}\exp(\pi\sqrt{163})&=262537412640768744-\delta_{163},\\10^{-14}&\le\delta_{163}\le9\cdot10^{-13}.\end{aligned}\]

The value lies below the integer by less than one trillionth.

Why these integers? Exact modular identities
\[j\!\left(\frac{1+i\sqrt{43}}2\right)=-960^3,\qquad j\!\left(\frac{1+i\sqrt{163}}2\right)=-640320^3.\]

The nearby integers are the positive cubes plus \(744\). The proofs construct the modular function, prove these exact special values, and bound the small Laurent correction. The graph lets you follow this argument from coarse numerical bounds through exact identities to the fine exponential estimates.

The general class-polynomial theorem remains open; these two exact CM values and their exponential corollaries are checked.

All quantities use the project’s computable real and complex foundation: geometric \(\pi\), positive square roots, and the current exponential API. The native proofs use no Mathlib real or complex numbers and respect equivalent valid representations.
