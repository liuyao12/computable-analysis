Arctangent inside a complex logarithm

Use the branch of the logarithm with \(\operatorname{Log}(1)=0\) on the right half-plane. Along the straight segment \(z(t)=1+it\), where \(0\le t\le1\),

\[\frac{dz}{z}=\frac{i\,dt}{1+it}=\frac{t+i}{1+t^2}\,dt.\]

Taking real and imaginary parts of the same integral gives

\[\begin{aligned}\operatorname{Log}(1+i)&=\int_{[1,1+i]}\frac{dz}{z}\\&=\int_0^1\frac{t\,dt}{1+t^2}+i\int_0^1\frac{dt}{1+t^2}\\&=\frac12\log 2+\frac{i\pi}{4}.\end{aligned}\]

Thus the arctangent integral gives \(\pi/4\), and

\[\pi=4\,\operatorname{Im}\operatorname{Log}(1+i).\]

The segment stays in the right half-plane, so its logarithm has no ambiguity from winding around zero. This explains why the logarithm and arctangent computations belong together: the reciprocal kernel on a complex segment contains both the real logarithm and the real arctangent.

A computation using one symmetric series

Move the logarithm argument into a rapidly convergent local series:

\[q=\frac{(1+i)-1}{(1+i)+1}=\frac{1+2i}{5},\qquad |q|^2=\frac15.\]
\[H(q)=2\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{2k+1}}{2k+1}=\operatorname{Log}\frac{1+q}{1-q}=\operatorname{Log}(1+i).\]

The branch is fixed throughout: for \(|q|<1\), the quotient \((1+q)/(1-q)\) lies in the right half-plane, and both expressions vanish at \(q=0\). The identity follows by differentiating there and comparing that initial value.

\[P_N=8\,\operatorname{Im}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{q^{2k+1}}{2k+1}.\]

All terms are Gaussian rationals. Since \(|q|\le1/2\) and \(|q|^2=1/5\), the geometric tail gives, for \(N\ge1\),

\[\begin{aligned}|\pi-P_N|&\le8\sum_{k=N}^{\infty}\frac{|q|^{2k+1}}{2k+1}\\&\le\frac{4}{2N+1}\sum_{k=N}^{\infty}5^{-k}\\&=\frac{5}{(2N+1)5^N}.\end{aligned}\]
