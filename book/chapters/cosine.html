A first meeting of geometry and calculus

A primitive should be a theorem about two independent computations—not a new name for the answer. The cosine integral gives a small, complete example.

An angle is an area computation

We keep the order of the geometric chapters. Rational polygons give the geometric arctangent \(A\); rational lower and upper rectangles give an equivalent computation:

\[ A(u)\simeq\int_0^u\frac{dv}{1+v^2},\qquad 0\le u\le1. \]

These are two ways to compute the same number, not two names for identical finite outputs. We fix the normalization \(\pi=4A(1)\) before introducing the integral below. No cosine integral enters this definition of \(\pi\).

From the clock to a point on the circle

For rational \(0\le x\le\tfrac12\), the closed inverse construction computes \(u(x)\) in the unit interval with

\[ A(u(x))\simeq 2xA(1). \]

The two rational circle coordinates define our sine and cosine:

\[ S(x)=\frac{2u(x)}{1+u(x)^2},\qquad C(x)=\frac{1-u(x)^2}{1+u(x)^2}. \]

The argument is a normalized angle: a half-unit is a quarter-turn. In the comparison with conventional trigonometry, \(S(x)\) corresponds to \(\sin(\pi x)\), not \(\sin x\). The functions in this particular theorem are restricted to the displayed chart; the zero-valued totalizations outside it are not global trigonometric extensions.

An independently computed integral

For our purposes, introduce integration for increasing or decreasing functions. The two endpoint rectangle sums bound the area; their exact gap is \(h|f(b)-f(a)|\). No concavity, derivative or Lipschitz assumption is needed for this construction. Rational evaluation errors are enclosed separately, on a fixed finite schedule of retained meshes and sample refinements.

On the present chart, \(C\) is decreasing. Thus, with \(x_i=ih\) and \(h=t/m\),

\[h\sum_{i=1}^{m} C(x_i)\ \preceq\ \int_0^t C(x)\,dx\ \preceq\ h\sum_{i=0}^{m-1} C(x_i).\]

The existing three Lean proofs use the independently defined quadrature program described next; the monotone-rectangle illustration is not a replacement for its literal output schedule.

Divide \([0,t]\) into \(m\) equal cells and add the left-endpoint cosine rectangles. Evaluate every cosine value by its own rational enclosure. A finite geometric estimate supplies a rational allowance for the error made by replacing the curve with these rectangles.

A finer mesh and a finer function evaluation do different jobs. Retaining a fixed mesh while improving its finitely many samples lets us separate them. Taking finite intersections of the resulting enclosures produces a valid integral computation \(I(t)\). Its numerical instructions read cosine values, never the proposed sine endpoint.

TheoremThe cosine primitive

For every rational \(t\) with \(0\le t\le\tfrac12\), the independent integral and endpoint computations agree:

\[ \boxed{I(t)=\int_0^t C(x)\,dx\;\simeq\;\frac{S(t)}{\pi}.} \]

The equality denotes equivalence of valid rational-enclosure programs. It does not say that their stage outputs or their instructions are identical.

Three arguments, one proposition

Direct finite inequalities

Compare a small circle chord with its angular increment. For a fixed rational step \(h\), the resulting sine increment differs from \(h\pi C(x)\) by at most a constant times \(h^2\), once the finite samples are sufficiently precise. Choose a common stage for a fixed finite mesh and telescope the rational sine increments. The sum of the cell errors tends to zero with the mesh. Nesting then gives overlap at every pair of output stages.

Through concavity and the fundamental theorem

Here concavity is used to construct the derivative, not to define the integral. Put \(P=S/\pi\). The geometric chord inequalities first prove supporting-secant bounds

\[ C(y)\preceq\frac{P(y)-P(x)}{y-x}\preceq C(x),\qquad x<y. \]

They establish concavity without assuming a derivative. Quantitative control of these secants gives the data required by the native fundamental theorem. Applying that theorem identifies the cosine integral with \(P(t)-P(0)\). The independently proved value \(S(0)\simeq0\) removes the lower endpoint.

Through Mathlib, with both bridges

The optional comparison first proves that our values at rational arguments represent Mathlib’s sine, cosine and pi. A separate quadrature estimate identifies our actual cosine-sum program with Mathlib’s interval integral: agreement on rational arguments alone would not suffice. Mathlib’s primitive formula then identifies this integral with the real represented by our endpoint program. Two interval programs containing the same real at every stage have overlapping outputs.

These are three terms of one Lean proposition with identical hypotheses. The native proofs do not import Mathlib. The comparison is an external verification route, not part of the numerical foundation.

What the comparison teaches us

The direct route concentrates on the geometry of this function. The fundamental-theorem route invests in reusable calculus. The Mathlib route uses an existing theory together with explicit representation bridges. A useful comparison therefore keeps the last proof step, the earlier mathematics, and the bridges separate.

The proof map beside the theorem shows the three structures converging on one conclusion. Its bundles open onto mathematical statements first, with the exact checked Lean declarations available underneath. The quantitative comparison is available separately; the size of the supporting library is evidence about this example, not a verdict on all calculus.
