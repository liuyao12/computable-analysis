Two integral families, two foundations

A calculus foundation earns its keep when it can be reused. Cosine powers and beta polynomials lead from independent finite sums to factorial identities and arithmetic bounds.

Each family below has two closed formal derivations: our quantitative FTC and Mathlib's calculus, connected to the very same native computations by separate quadrature bridges. The curves, endpoints, and stage schedules are fixed before their integrals are evaluated. The native development introduces no integrability predicate and obtains no numerical value by noncomputable choice.

Cosine powers and a prescribed joint schedule

Write \(c(t)=C(t/2)\), \(s(t)=S(t/2)\), and \(\lambda=\pi/2\), with the existing geometric \(\pi=4A(1)\). On the rational unit interval, construct

\[W_n=\int_0^1 c(t)^n\,dt.\]

Every integrand is nonnegative and decreasing. At joint stage \(k\), the program uses \(2^k\) equal cells and evaluates each cosine sample at stage \(k\). This particular diagonal is justified by a uniform power-error estimate, not by pointwise convergence alone. The finite sample mean is widened by \((1+112n)2^{-k}\), then intersected with earlier enclosures and with \([0,1]\).

\[\operatorname{width}((W_n)_k)\le2(1+112n)2^{-k}.\]

This validity proof does not use the power-reduction recurrence or the proposed value of \(W_n\). The bound is conservative; it provides a certified construction rather than an optimized integration routine.

The calculus step

The derivatives \(s'=\lambda c\), \(c'=-\lambda s\), and the product rule give

\[\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(s(t)c(t)^{n+1}\bigr)=\lambda\bigl((n+2)c(t)^{n+2}-(n+1)c(t)^n\bigr).\]

The primitive vanishes at both endpoints. FTC therefore yields

\[(n+2)W_{n+2}=(n+1)W_n,\qquad W_0=1,\quad\lambda W_1=1.\]

Our proof certifies the finite product and power remainders and applies the reusable quantitative FTC. It does not assume that the whole primitive is convex or concave. The Mathlib proof uses its cosine-power integration-by-parts theorem and a change of variable; a separate rectangle comparison first identifies the native program with its real interval integral.

The factorial evaluation

The recurrence has rational coefficients. Define \(a_0=a_1=1\), \(a_{n+2}=\frac{n+1}{n+2}a_n\), independently of the integral. Finite induction gives \(W_{2n}=a_{2n}\) and \(\lambda W_{2n+1}=a_{2n+1}\); factorial identities put these in their familiar form.

TheoremWallis integral evaluations

For every natural \(n\), the independently computed integral programs satisfy

\[\boxed{4^n(n!)^2W_{2n}\simeq(2n)!,\qquad(2n+1)!\lambda W_{2n+1}\simeq4^n(n!)^2.}\]

The cleared-denominator form is the common Lean proposition. Both complete proofs have exactly the same hypotheses and conclusion.

A finite rational product encloses geometric pi

Set \(R_n=a_{2n+1}/a_{2n}\). Pure rational algebra proves

\[R_0=1,\qquad R_{n+1}=\frac{(2n+2)^2}{(2n+1)(2n+3)}R_n.\]

This is the finite Wallis product. Its computation does not read \(\pi\), cosine, or an integral. Since \(0\le c\le1\), the samples satisfy \(W_{2n+2}\le W_{2n+1}\le W_{2n}\). Combining these inequalities with the evaluations gives:

TheoremRational Wallis-product bounds
\[\boxed{2R_n\le\pi\le2\frac{a_{2n+1}}{a_{2n+2}}=2R_n\frac{2n+2}{2n+1}.}\]

The Lean statement bounds the original geometric pi computation between the two independently calculated rational endpoints. The order and factorial arguments are shared; the analytic recurrence is supplied by either calculus route.

Thus the middle of the argument is calculus, but the output is a rational product estimate. The familiar central-binomial coefficient enters through \(a_{2n}=(2n)!/(4^n(n!)^2)\); the formalization here proves that factorial identity, not a new theorem about a separate combinatorial counting implementation.

Beta polynomials: a different kind of integrand

For natural \(m,n\), independently compute

\[B_{m,n}=\int_0^1 x^m(1-x)^n\,dx.\]

Unlike cosine, its samples at rational inputs are exact rationals. For positive exponents the integrand rises and falls; it is not globally monotone. The supplied finite estimate \(|f(x)-f(y)|\le(m+n)|x-y|\) controls every cell, so a fixed dyadic schedule gives a valid integral even across its turning point. This is a constructor for the stated polynomial and bound, not an unrestricted existence assertion for integrals.

\[\operatorname{width}((B_{m,n})_k)\le2(m+n)2^{-k}.\]

The native proof obtains the derivative of \(x^{m+1}(1-x)^{n+1}\) by the same finite power/product calculus. Applying FTC to its zero endpoint difference, and using \(x+(1-x)=1\), gives

\[(m+n+2)B_{m,n+1}=(n+1)B_{m,n},\qquad (m+1)B_{m,0}=1.\]

The Mathlib route proves its own derivative and applies its FTC. A separate Lipschitz rectangle bridge identifies the same native quadrature with the real integral without first using a beta or gamma evaluation.

TheoremBeta integrals at integer parameters

Uniformly for all natural \(m,n\),

\[\boxed{(m+n+1)!B_{m,n}\simeq m!n!.}\]

This includes zero exponents and does not assume endpoint vanishing where an exponent is zero. The recurrence endpoint is defined by rational arithmetic; it is not used to define or validate the quadrature.

Normalizing polynomial densities

The reciprocal of the independently defined rational recurrence value is a positive rational coefficient \(d_{m,n}\). The factorial evaluation identifies it with \((m+n+1)!/(m!n!)\). Scaling the already constructed integral is the chosen quadrature for the corresponding polynomial density.

TheoremBeta polynomial normalization
\[\boxed{\frac{(m+n+1)!}{m!n!}\,B_{m,n}\simeq1.}\]

The coefficient is calculated by finite rational recursion, not by taking the reciprocal of the integral program. The proof uses the evaluation; the computation does not.
