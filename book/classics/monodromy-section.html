Monodromy: returning with a different solution

The evolution above takes place on a real interval. In a complex domain, continuation can return to the same point along a loop and produce a different local solution. Monodromy records this change.

Setup: local solutions and loops

Let \(U\) be a connected open complex domain on which the coefficients of

\[y''+p(z)y'+q(z)y=0\]

are holomorphic, and fix an ordinary point \(a\in U\). A solution germ at \(a\) is a local holomorphic solution, with two representatives identified when they agree throughout some neighborhood of \(a\). Agreement means equality of represented values, not equality of their algorithms. A germ contains local function information, not just one value. The local existence and uniqueness theorem identifies these solution germs with the initial data \((y(a),y'(a))\), giving a two-dimensional complex vector space \(V_a\).

Definition — monodromy along a loop

Let \(\gamma\) be a loop in \(U\), starting and ending at \(a\). When continuation of solution germs along this loop is constructed and proved unique, its monodromy operator sends each initial germ to the terminal germ:

\[T_\gamma:V_a\longrightarrow V_a,\qquad [y]_a\longmapsto[y^\gamma]_a.\]

For a linear equation, continuation preserves linear combinations, and reversing the loop reverses continuation. Consequently \(T_\gamma\) is an invertible linear map. These are consequences of continuation and uniqueness; returning to the same point alone does not imply \(T_\gamma=I\).

A matrix in a chosen basis

Choose two independent local solutions and place their state vectors in columns:

\[\Phi(z)=\begin{pmatrix}y_1(z)&y_2(z)\\y_1'(z)&y_2'(z)\end{pmatrix}.\]

The monodromy matrix is the constant invertible matrix characterized, on a neighborhood of the base point, by

\[\Phi^\gamma(z)=\Phi(z)M_\gamma.\]

Thus continuation of a solution with coefficient column \(c\) gives coefficient column \(M_\gamma c\). Replacing the basis by \(\widetilde\Phi=\Phi C\), for a constant invertible \(C\), changes the matrix to

\[\widetilde M_\gamma=C^{-1}M_\gamma C.\]

The operator is intrinsic to the equation, base point, and loop; its displayed matrix depends on the basis. Everything here extends to a system \(Y'=A(z)Y\) of any finite dimension, with a fundamental matrix whose columns are independent vector solutions.

Loops, composition, and the monodromy group

Use \(\eta\star\gamma\) to mean following \(\gamma\) first, then \(\eta\). Unique continuation gives

\[M_{\eta\star\gamma}=M_\eta M_\gamma,\qquad M_{\gamma^{-1}}=M_\gamma^{-1}.\]

The further homotopy-invariance theorem says that deforming a loop within \(U\), with the base point fixed, leaves its monodromy unchanged. Once that theorem and continuation along all loops are available, the operators give the monodromy representation

\[\rho:\pi_1(U,a)\longrightarrow\operatorname{GL}(V_a),\qquad [\gamma]\longmapsto T_\gamma.\]

Here \(\pi_1(U,a)\) is the group of based loops modulo such deformations, with the composition convention above. The image of \(\rho\) is the monodromy group. A single computed loop operator does not establish this whole representation. The related monodromy theorem says that a germ which can be continued along every path in a simply connected domain has a single-valued holomorphic continuation there; that is a theorem, not part of the definition.

The running Bessel example

For \(p(z)=1/z\) and \(q(z)=1\), use the basis \((J_0,Y_*)\), where \(J_0\) is entire and \(Y_*=J_0\operatorname{Log}z+H(z)\), with \(H\) entire. This normalization differs from the standard \(Y_0\). A counterclockwise circuit around the origin gives

\[J_0\longmapsto J_0,\qquad Y_*\longmapsto Y_*+2\pi iJ_0,\] \[M_\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\pi i\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad M_\gamma^k=\begin{pmatrix}1&2k\pi i\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\quad(k\in\mathbb Z).\]

The regular singular point has nontrivial monodromy: regular singularity controls growth and does not require solutions to return unchanged. Conversely, trivial monodromy need not make a singularity removable: \(y=1/z\) solves \(y'=-y/z\) and returns unchanged around its pole.

Definition and classical consequences; not new Lean continuation theorems. In our foundation, first construct the local represented solutions, prove agreement on open overlaps along the chosen route, and identify the terminal basis by exact represented identities. Solution-space dimension, invertibility, independence of chart choices, and homotopy invariance require their corresponding analytic proofs. The continuation skill records those obligations.
