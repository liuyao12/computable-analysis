Euler’s sine-product proof

Euler’s insight survives intact: the quadratic term of the sine product determines the sum of reciprocal squares. A uniform finite-product estimate makes the coefficient comparison rigorous.

\[\boxed{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k^2}=\frac{\pi^2}{6}.}\]

This is a new, checked Lean proof using Mathlib’s sine-product theorem. It is independent of the Bernoulli–Fourier evaluation and of our native finite-rearrangement proof. The proof runs in a separate Mathlib package; it does not yet supply an Euler-product proof in the project’s rational-interval foundation.

What has to be justified?

Knowing the zeros of sine does not by itself determine the function: multiplication by a nonvanishing factor preserves those zeros. Even after the product identity has been proved, an infinite product cannot simply be expanded like a polynomial without controlling its remainder.

\[\frac{\sin(\pi x)}{\pi x}=\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{x^2}{k^2}\right).\]

We justify these two steps separately: use a proved sine-product identity, then extract its quadratic coefficient by an explicit inequality. No differentiation of an infinite product is needed for the Basel case.

Start with a finite product

For a finite family with \(0\le a_k\le1\), set \(A=\sum_k a_k\). Induction proves

\[0\le\prod_k(1-a_k)-(1-A)\le\frac{A^2}{2}.\]

If \(R\) is the current remainder, adjoining a factor gives the exact identity \(R_{\mathrm{new}}=(1-a)R+aA\). Nonnegativity and the upper bound follow using only finite algebra and inequalities.

Now put

\[S_N=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac1{k^2},\qquad P_N(x)=\prod_{k=1}^{N}\left(1-\frac{x^2}{k^2}\right).\]

The telescoping comparison \(1/k^2\le2/k-2/(k+1)\) gives \(0\le S_N\le2\). Consequently, for \(0\le x\le1\),

\[\boxed{0\le P_N(x)-(1-x^2S_N)\le2x^4.}\]

The bound is uniform in \(N\). This is the crucial replacement for discarding unspecified higher-order terms.

Supply the sine-product identity

Mathlib proves the product formula by a recurrence for weighted cosine integrals, followed by a concentration argument related to Wallis’ formula. This supplies the analytic identity without appealing to a previously evaluated reciprocal-square sum. General Weierstrass factorization is not required for this route.

Write \(S=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}1/k^2\); ordinary series convergence does not assume its value. Fix \(0<x\le1\) and pass to the limit in the finite estimate:

\[0\le\frac{\sin(\pi x)}{\pi x}-(1-x^2S)\le2x^4.\]

Compare coefficients with an error bound

The independently proved sine estimate is

\[\left|\sin t-\left(t-\frac{t^3}{6}\right)\right|\le\frac{|t|^5}{100}\qquad(|t|\le1).\]

Combine this with the product estimate and divide by the positive quadratic factor. For \(0<x\le1\) and \(\pi x\le1\), Lean proves

\[\boxed{\left|S-\frac{\pi^2}{6}\right|\le\left(2+\frac{\pi^4}{100}\right)x^2.}\]

Take \(x=1/((\pi+1)(n+1))\) and let \(n\) grow. The right side tends to zero, so \(S=\pi^2/6\). Euler’s coefficient comparison is now a consequence of two controlled remainders.

What is checked?

The final theorem is unconditional. The audit follows actual elaborated proof dependencies and requires the finite-product bound, sine-product theorem, and coefficient-error theorem. It rejects previous zeta evaluations, the native Basel proof, unfinished proofs, and custom axioms. The permitted axioms are Mathlib’s usual propext, Classical.choice, and Quot.sound.

Toward every even zeta value

The natural extension is to control higher coefficients of the same product. With the required convergence and differentiation estimates, its logarithmic derivative would give

\[\pi x\cot(\pi x)=1-2\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\zeta(2m)x^{2m}\qquad(0<|x|<1).\]

Comparison with the Bernoulli expansion then yields

\[\zeta(2m)=\frac{(-1)^{m+1}B_{2m}(2\pi)^{2m}}{2(2m)!}\qquad(m\ge1).\]

This extension is a roadmap, not a theorem of the new package. The checked result here is the Basel case. A native version also needs an interval-level sine-product theorem and its connection to geometric \(\pi\). The separate Mathlib Fourier proof already covers all positive even arguments.
