The Basel problem

Finite rearrangements, Euler’s sine product, and Fourier series: three routes to the sum of reciprocal squares.

\[\boxed{\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}{6}.}\]

The native theorem identifies the independently computed reciprocal-square series with geometric \(\pi^2/6\). It also identifies the value of our real-input zeta function at \(2\). Both results are unconditional.

The native proof: square finite Leibniz sums

For \(N\ge1\), write

\[B_N=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{(-1)^k}{2k+1},\qquad H_N=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac1{2k+1},\qquad M_N=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac1{(2k+1)^2}.\]

Expand \(B_N^2\) as a finite double sum. Partial fractions and alternating-prefix bounds compare the square and triangular regions of summation, giving

\[|2B_N^2-M_N|\le\frac{2H_N}{N}.\]

Next separate the odd and even terms of the reciprocal-square series. The explicit comparison schedule is

\[\left|\sum_{k=1}^{2m^2}\frac1{k^2}-\frac83 B_{2m^2}^{\,2}\right|\le\frac{16}{m}\qquad(m\ge1).\]

The bound tends to zero. The checked Leibniz identity then gives \((8/3)(\pi/4)^2=\pi^2/6\). Every rearrangement is finite; convergence is supplied by the displayed rational error bounds. The proof implements the finite rearrangement approach discussed by Benko and Molokach.

Euler’s product, made rigorous

A separate checked proof now derives the Basel identity from the sine product. Its key estimate is uniform in the number of factors:

\[0\le\prod_{k=1}^{N}\left(1-\frac{x^2}{k^2}\right)-\left(1-x^2\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac1{k^2}\right)\le2x^4\qquad(0\le x\le1).\]

This controls the coefficient comparison after the product limit is taken. The new proof uses Mathlib’s sine-product theorem and avoids its Fourier evaluation. Read Euler’s rigorous proof and its Lean audit.

The Mathlib proof: Bernoulli Fourier series

Mathlib takes a broader route. It computes the Fourier coefficients of the Bernoulli polynomials by a derivative recurrence, proves summability, and reconstructs their periodic extensions. At the endpoint, this gives the general even-zeta formula; specializing to \(2\) yields hasSum_zeta_two.

For the Basel case, the periodic polynomial is \(B_2(x)=x^2-x+1/6\). Its Fourier expansion is

\[B_2(x)=\frac1{\pi^2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\cos(2\pi n x)}{n^2}\qquad(0\le x\le1).\]

Evaluation at \(x=0\) gives the answer. Mathlib’s formal sum is indexed by natural numbers including \(0\); the zero-index term is zero under its total division convention, so it agrees with the series displayed above.

Where irrationality enters

Basel does not need the irrationality of \(\pi^2\). The later Euler-sieve argument uses it to rule out a finite exhaustive list of primes.

Irrationality of geometric \(\pi^2\) is already formalized in the published Cartwright development, with finite, FTC, and Mathlib comparison routes. The newer Basel module currently keeps irrationality as a hypothesis: its source version has not yet incorporated that earlier theorem and the connection between the two implementations. This is an integration gap, not an absent irrationality proof.
