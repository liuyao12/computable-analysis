Function theory · Approximation and exact proof

How can a zeta zero be exactly on the line?

We can compute only enclosures. Yet those enclosures can prove an exact statement: a particular zero has real part precisely \(\tfrac12\). The equality comes from symmetry and uniqueness.

A decimal answer cannot settle equality

Suppose a computation places a suspected zero very close to the critical line \(\Re s=\tfrac12\). Even an enclosure a million digits narrow permits a nonzero displacement. A small value of \(\zeta(s)\) also does not, by itself, prove that a zero exists nearby.

There is a way past this difficulty. We use approximate evaluations to prove strict inequalities and an exact integer count. Then a mathematical symmetry forces the root onto the line. We never need to decide equality by comparing two decimal expansions.

One root, together with its reflection

Suppose a function has a proved reflection symmetry, and a reflection-invariant region contains exactly one zero, counted with multiplicity. The reflected zero belongs to the same region. Uniqueness makes these two zeros equal, so that zero lies exactly on the reflection's fixed line.

\[R(s)=1-\overline s,\qquad f(R(s))=\overline{f(s)},\qquad N_B=1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\rho=R(\rho)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Re\rho=\frac12.\]

The region can have appreciable width. It is the unique-root proof, together with symmetry, that establishes equality.

The symmetry we need for zeta

It is convenient to use the completed function

\[\xi(s)=\frac12s(s-1)\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(s/2)\zeta(s).\]

Its functional equation, together with conjugation, gives

\[\xi(1-s)=\xi(s),\qquad \xi(\overline s)=\overline{\xi(s)},\qquad \xi(1-\overline s)=\overline{\xi(s)}.\]

In the open critical strip the normalizing factors are nonzero, so zeros of \(\xi\) and \(\zeta\) coincide, with their multiplicities. These classical facts require proofs for the constructed functions in our foundation. See the completed function and functional equation.

Write a zero as \(\rho=\beta+i\gamma\). Its reflected companion is \(1-\beta+i\gamma\): the same height, on the opposite side of the middle line. Symmetry alone allows two distinct off-line roots. A proof that there is room for only one root removes that possibility.

An enclosing rectangle turns symmetry into equality

Choose rational bounds \(0< a< \tfrac12\) and \(0< t_-< t_+\), and use the open rectangle

\[B=\left\{s:\frac12-a< \Re s< \frac12+a,\quad t_-< \Im s< t_+\right\}.\]

This rectangle stays inside the strip and is invariant under \(R\). A genuine zero count \(N_B=1\) proves existence and uniqueness, and also simplicity. Take its zero \(\rho\). The symmetry proves that \(R(\rho)\) is another zero in \(B\). Therefore

\[\beta+i\gamma=1-\beta+i\gamma,\qquad 2\beta=1,\qquad \beta=\frac12.\]

Every step after the count is exact algebra. Approximation locates the height and verifies the count; it is never used to replace “very close” with “equal.”

How approximate values prove an exact count

Let \(C\) be the positively oriented boundary of \(B\). For a holomorphic function with no zeros on \(C\), a justified argument-principle identity gives

\[N_B=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_C\frac{\xi'(s)}{\xi(s)}\,ds.\]

The left side is the actual number of zeros, with multiplicity, hence a nonnegative integer. Suppose a certified enclosure of the right side proves

\[\frac34< N_B< \frac54.\]

Only one integer fits. Thus \(N_B=1\) exactly, although the integral was computed approximately. The displayed interval is illustrative; it is not a computation for an actual zeta rectangle.

There are three separate proof obligations. First, compute values and derivatives with justified error bounds. Second, exclude zero on every point of the contour: endpoint samples alone do not suffice. For example, a whole-edge enclosure can separate its image from the origin, or a supplied variation bound can control the gaps between samples. Third, prove that the computed contour value equals the actual multiplicity count. A rounded number near one establishes none of these obligations on its own.

One can instead enclose the contour image by a polygon and prove a deformation that avoids the origin. Its winding number is an exact integer, but its connection to actual zeros still needs proof. The zero-counting skill selects concrete local factorizations, logarithms and cancellation, or a justified comparison for the function and contour at hand. A general Cauchy–Goursat theorem is an optional route.

A simpler way to prove there is a zero on the line

The symmetry makes the following function exactly real-valued for real \(t\):

\[\Xi(t)=\xi\left(\frac12+it\right),\qquad \overline{\Xi(t)}=\Xi(t).\]

Now evaluate it at two rational heights. If rigorous rational enclosures establish, for example,

\[\Xi(t_-)\in[L_-,U_-],\quad U_-< 0,\qquad \Xi(t_+)\in[L_+,U_+],\quad 0< L_+,\]

then the signs are exact. Continuity and a justified root-existence construction give some \(t_*\) strictly between the endpoints with \(\Xi(t_*)=0\). Its corresponding zeta zero is \(\tfrac12+it_*\), on the line by construction. There is no approximate real part to correct.

This establishes a line zero. To identify it with the unique zero in a chosen rectangle, supply the enclosing rectangle's uniqueness proof. To produce a computable number for its height, supply a shrinking isolation schedule and prove convergence and the zero equation. Bisection is a useful strategy when its progress is justified; an undecided midpoint sign cannot silently be treated as a decided equality.

Practical work often uses the real-valued Hardy function \(Z(t)\), which differs from \(\zeta(\tfrac12+it)\) by a nonzero phase factor. The same sign-certificate idea applies. Critical-line functions.

Why an almost-zero and symmetry are insufficient

Here is an elementary model, not a zeta computation. Fix rational \(t_0>0\) and \(\delta>0\), and put

\[c=\frac12+it_0,\qquad F_\delta(s)=(s-c)^2-\delta^2=(s-c-\delta)(s-c+\delta).\]

It has the same reflection-conjugation symmetry: \(R(s)-c=-\overline{s-c}\), so \(F_\delta(R(s))=\overline{F_\delta(s)}\). Its two roots are \(c-\delta\) and \(c+\delta\). Both lie off the line, however small \(\delta\) is. Moreover, \(F_\delta(c)=-\delta^2\) is arbitrarily small although the point on the line is not a root.

A symmetric rectangle enclosing both roots has count two. Reflection exchanges them, so uniqueness is unavailable. If \(\delta=0\), the center is instead a double root on the line, also with count two. The count two alone does not distinguish these possibilities; the count one certificate does.

What a certificate for one particular zero contains

  1. A constructed continuation of zeta on the entire chosen region, and a proved completed-function symmetry and zero correspondence.
  2. A rational symmetric rectangle, with rigorous nonvanishing bounds on its whole boundary.
  3. A convergent contour computation or winding construction, with its proved identity as the actual multiplicity count and an enclosure forcing that count to be one.
  4. The exact reflection-and-uniqueness deduction. A shrinking isolation construction additionally supplies a represented root and its height to arbitrary precision.

These are checkable pieces of mathematical evidence. A table of decimal roots is useful for proposing the rectangles, but the certificate is what proves the exact statement about each root.

One zero and all zeros below a height

Repeating an individual certificate proves the result for each certified rectangle. To assert that every zero below a chosen height lies on the line, also exclude any omitted zeros. Disjoint rectangles whose counts exhaust a justified global count provide one route; critical-line sign certificates combined with a Turing completeness argument provide another. Boundary zeros and multiplicities must be accounted for.

Platt and Trudgian's rigorous finite-height verification illustrates interval arithmetic together with completeness. The extra counting argument is essential to the word “every.” It is unnecessary when the claim concerns only a particular certified zero.
