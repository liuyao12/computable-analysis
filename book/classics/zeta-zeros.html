Function theory · Primes, continuation, and exact zeros

Why the zeros of zeta matter

Start with a sum over all integers. Find the primes inside it. Then follow the same function until its zeros become visible.

The primes are already in the function

For \(\Re s>1\), two descriptions agree:

\[\zeta(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{-s}=\prod_{p\ \mathrm{prime}}(1-p^{-s})^{-1},\qquad n^{-s}=e^{-s\log n}.\]

Expand each factor as a geometric series. Choosing a power of each prime chooses an integer, and unique factorization says each integer appears exactly once. Finite products make this arithmetic visible; absolute convergence justifies the limit. See the Euler product.

Analytic continuation makes the connection less mysterious: the local function already encodes the primes, and any extension on the same connected domain must agree with it. Yet uniqueness does not tell us how to extract the primes, how accurately nearby values determine distant ones, or why zeros are the useful data. Those are the questions this page follows.

The question: exactly on the critical line?

The nontrivial zeros lie in the critical strip. The Riemann hypothesis asserts that every one of them lies on its middle line:

\[\zeta(\rho)=0,\quad 0<\Re\rho<1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Re\rho=\frac12.\]

This is a conjecture about exact equality for every zero. A large finite list, however accurately computed, cannot prove the assertion for all heights. Even a finite-height theorem needs a proof that no zeros were omitted. Classical zero distribution.

Reach the strip by an actual computation

The original series does not converge at the nontrivial zeros. Continuing the function means supplying a new computation and proving agreement where both computations work. One useful entry into the strip is to pair adjacent terms:

\[\eta(s)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\big((2k-1)^{-s}-(2k)^{-s}\big),\qquad\zeta(s)=\frac{\eta(s)}{1-2^{1-s}}.\]

For \(\sigma=\Re s>0\), integrate the derivative of \(t^{-s}\) over each unit interval. The paired tail after \(K\ge1\) terms has the concrete bound

\[\left|\sum_{k>K}\big((2k-1)^{-s}-(2k)^{-s}\big)\right|\le\frac{|s|}{\sigma}(2K)^{-\sigma}.\]

On a chosen parameter rectangle, replace \(\sigma\) by its positive lower bound and \(|s|\) by a computed upper bound. This gives an executable precision schedule. Differentiated terms need their own tails, with logarithmic factors, to establish holomorphicity directly. Rearrangement on \(\Re s>1\) proves overlap agreement. The denominator is nonzero throughout \(0<\Re s<1\); on its other zeros a quotient alone is not a definition. See the alternating continuation formula.

This is a construction recipe to formalize, not a new Lean theorem. It needs specific series and derivative estimates; a general Cauchy–Goursat theorem is not a prerequisite.

Why the middle line is special

Gamma supplies the normalization that makes the functional equation a reflection:

\[\xi(s)=\frac12s(s-1)\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(s/2)\zeta(s),\qquad\xi(s)=\xi(1-s).\]

After the removable values are filled, \(\xi\) is entire. The factors cancel the pole and the negative even zeros of zeta; its zeros are precisely the nontrivial ones. Conjugation gives a second symmetry. Thus a hypothetical off-line zero generally comes with three companions:

\[\rho,\quad\overline\rho,\quad1-\rho,\quad1-\overline\rho.\]

Symmetry permits off-line pairs; it does not force them onto the line. On the line, the two reflections coincide in pairs. The negative even zeros \(-2,-4,\ldots\) are called trivial because the functional equation explains them explicitly. They are outside the Riemann hypothesis. Seeing a zero and counting every zero are different tasks

A small value can suggest where to search. A sign change of a justified real-valued critical-line function can prove a zero there. Neither excludes another zero elsewhere in the strip.

For a positively oriented contour \(C\) on which the constructed \(\xi\) never vanishes, the argument principle relates a boundary computation to the number inside, with multiplicity:

\[N(C)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_C\frac{\xi'(s)}{\xi(s)}\,ds=\frac{\Delta_C\arg\xi}{2\pi}.\]

The image of the contour winds around the origin. Near a zero, a proved local factorization \(f(s)=(s-\rho)^m g(s)\), with \(g\ne0\), gives \(f'/f=m/(s-\rho)+g'/g\). The pole term contributes its multiplicity. Constructing the regular logarithms and cancelling their contributions on the chosen region connects winding to actual roots. A rounded integral near an integer does not establish that connection.

Our zero-counting skill should choose the shortest justified route for the particular function and contour: local factorization and finite cancellation, or a proved zero-free deformation to a function whose roots are already counted. Whole edges need nonvanishing bounds, including the gaps between samples. Every comparison, integral, and multiplicity identity still needs proof.

How a finite computation can prove exact location

Choose a rectangle invariant under \(s\mapsto1-\overline s\). Suppose a genuine count proves it contains exactly one simple root. Reflection gives a root in the same rectangle, so uniqueness forces

\[\rho=1-\overline\rho\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Re\rho=\frac12.\]

This is an exact algebraic conclusion. No decimal approximation to the real part is being upgraded to equality. The repository already proves this conditional reflection-and-uniqueness implication for represented roots. To certify all zeros below a height, disjoint isolating rectangles must exhaust the global count, and boundary exclusions must be proved. Checked implication.

Other rigorous methods combine certified critical-line roots with Turing completeness checks; Platt and Trudgian give a substantial example. Rigorous computation is a proof when its bounds and completeness argument are justified.

Why zeros appear when we recover the primes

Taking a logarithmic derivative of the Euler product turns prime factors into prime-power coefficients:

\[-\frac{\zeta'(s)}{\zeta(s)}=\sum_{p}\sum_{k\ge1}(\log p)p^{-ks}=\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^s},\qquad\Re s>1.\]

Here \(\Lambda(p^k)=\log p\), and all other coefficients vanish. Write \(\psi(x)=\sum_{n\le x}\Lambda(n)\), and let \(\psi_0\) take the average of the two limits at a jump. The inversion kernel \(x^s/s\) converts these coefficients into the staircase. Moving its integration contour encounters a pole at each zeta zero. A zero of multiplicity \(m\) contributes \(-m x^\rho/\rho\).

\[\psi_0(x)=x-\lim_{T\to\infty}\sum_{|\Im\rho|\le T}\frac{x^\rho}{\rho}-\log(2\pi)-\frac12\log(1-x^{-2}),\qquad x>1.\]

The sum counts multiplicities and is ordered symmetrically by height; it is not absolutely convergent. The pole at \(s=1\) supplies \(x\); the other terms come from nontrivial zeros, the origin, and trivial zeros. This is the classical explicit formula, with contour limits and tails to justify. One zero pair: frequency and amplitude

For \(\rho=\beta+i\gamma\), combine its term with the conjugate term. Ordinary complex arithmetic gives

\[-\frac{x^\rho}{\rho}-\frac{x^{\overline\rho}}{\overline\rho}=-\frac{2x^\beta}{\beta^2+\gamma^2}\big(\beta\cos(\gamma\log x)+\gamma\sin(\gamma\log x)\big).\]

The height \(\gamma\) sets the frequency in \(\log x\); the real part \(\beta\) sets the growth scale \(x^\beta\). This is the quantitative content missing from uniqueness alone. Under the Riemann hypothesis, each pair has the square-root scale. Controlling the entire sum still requires bounds for all heights. Classically, the hypothesis is equivalent to \(\psi(x)=x+O(x^{1/2+\varepsilon})\) for every \(\varepsilon>0\). Prime-counting estimates.

Our next constructions

The checked foundation supplies quotient derivatives and polynomial calculus, integer-zeta comparison laws, positive-integer Gamma Gauss limits, and the exact reflection implication. Complex zeta, general-parameter Gamma, their symmetry, actual contour counts, and the explicit formula above remain construction tasks. No finite-height critical-line theorem is claimed here.

For each task, the skill selects a computation, proves its local derivative or tail bounds, establishes overlap agreement, and proves the needed contour identity. General Cauchy–Goursat is an optional route, not a gate every application must pass.
