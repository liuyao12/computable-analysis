Function theory · A Taylor series with a boundary

Arctangent: where Taylor stops

A function can be smooth along the entire real axis while one of its Taylor series reaches only a finite interval.

One function, one expansion point

Take the geometric arctangent, normalized by \(A(0)=0\). Its derivative is \(A'(x)=1/(1+x^2)\). The first \(N\) nonzero terms of its Taylor expansion at the origin form the polynomial

\[S_N(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{(-1)^k x^{2k+1}}{2k+1},\qquad S_0(x)=0.\]

The function and its Taylor polynomials are separate computations. The question is where these polynomials approach the function as the number of terms grows.

Theorem — The exact interval of convergence

For every real \(x\) with \(|x|\le1\), the Taylor polynomials converge to \(A(x)=\arctan x\), including both endpoints. For every real \(x\) with \(|x|>1\), the sequence of Taylor polynomials does not converge.

\[\lim_{N\to\infty}S_N(x)=\arctan x\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad |x|\le1.\]

Inside this interval the remainder satisfies

\[|A(x)-S_N(x)|\le\frac{|x|^{2N+1}}{2N+1}.\]

The Lean development checks the convergence/divergence classification for every rational input, with convergence to the independently constructed geometric arctangent. The displayed classical statement includes irrational inputs; the extension of this classifier to arbitrary represented inputs remains to be supplied. The DLMF records the arctangent expansion.

A finite identity starts the proof

No interchange of infinite sums and integrals is necessary. Finite division gives

\[\frac1{1+t^2}=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(-1)^k t^{2k}+\frac{(-1)^N t^{2N}}{1+t^2}.\]

Integrating this finite identity from \(0\) to \(x\) explains both the coefficients and the error:

\[A(x)-S_N(x)=(-1)^N\int_0^x\frac{t^{2N}}{1+t^2}\,dt.\]

For \(|x|\le r<1\), the bound decays at least geometrically in the number of terms. The same bound also works at the endpoints, where it decays much more slowly. Our existing finite rectangle comparisons identify the series evaluator with the geometric arctangent.

The endpoints deserve their own argument

At \(x=1\), the series becomes

\[1-\frac13+\frac15-\cdots=\arctan1=\frac\pi4.\]

At \(x=-1\), oddness changes the sign. These endpoint series converge conditionally. Being on a circle of convergence alone does not settle what happens there; the remainder estimate does.

Choose the endpoint preset and increase the term count. The alternating sums keep enclosing the value, but the error decreases only on the scale of \(1/N\). Compare that with a point strictly inside the interval. The boundary series is also the Leibniz series.

Outside, more terms eventually make things worse

The difference between successive Taylor polynomials is the next term:

\[S_{N+1}(x)-S_N(x)=\frac{(-1)^N x^{2N+1}}{2N+1}.\]

Write \(r=|x|>1\). Bernoulli’s finite inequality gives, for every \(N\ge1\),

\[\left|S_{N+1}(x)-S_N(x)\right|\ge\frac{r(r^2-1)}3>0.\]

Consequently, the partial sums fail the Cauchy condition and cannot converge to any represented real value. This is the new checked divergence argument. Close to the boundary, a few terms can still look persuasive: a good finite approximation does not establish convergence.

The obstruction is off the real axis

The real derivative is defined everywhere, but its complex expression has two poles:

\[A'(z)=\frac1{1+z^2}=\frac1{(z-i)(z+i)}.\]

Both are distance \(1\) from the expansion point. The arctangent germ has logarithmic branch points there; these are not poles of arctangent itself. The logarithmic representation exhibits these branch points. Its Taylor series at the origin therefore has radius \(1\), even though the real function continues smoothly past both real endpoints.

This is what the Cauchy–Taylor foundation should ultimately explain: local coefficient estimates and continuation describe the reach of an expansion. For this particular series, finite remainder estimates and the term obstruction already prove the real convergence boundary. The general complex singularity argument is explanatory context, not a completed theorem in the current formalization.

The function continues beyond this Taylor series

For positive \(x\), the addition formula gives

\[\arctan x=\frac\pi4+\arctan\!\left(\frac{x-1}{x+1}\right).\]

The transformed argument lies strictly between \(-1\) and \(1\). For example, \(x=2\) becomes \(1/3\). Thus a convergent local expansion still computes the function where the original expansion diverges. This is an elementary instance of moving between local descriptions of the same function.

What is checked

The Lean proofs connect the literal polynomial partial sums to the existing alternating interval evaluator, then to geometric arctangent. They prove that a represented limit exists exactly when the rational input lies in the closed unit interval, and give a positive consecutive-term obstruction outside it. Equality of computations respects the representation of the target value.

The displayed sharp remainder identity, conditional-versus-absolute endpoint distinction, global complex branch description, and extension to arbitrary represented inputs are not all asserted as new checked theorems by this audit. The audit states precisely which declarations were checked.
