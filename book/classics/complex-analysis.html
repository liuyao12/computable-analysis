Cauchy’s theory, by finite computation

Local complex-linear approximations cancel around small triangles. Arctangent determines what remains around a pole.

Setup: a function, a polygon, a local model

For the checked contour theorem, take a finite oriented collection of rational triangles and a complex function whose values are valid rational-box computations. At stage \(n\), subdivide each triangle to depth \(d_n\ge n\), sharing every edge sample. The selected function boxes have both widths at most \(1/(n+1)\). On every small triangle, suppose the sampled function at every rational point of the filled triangle admits a complex-affine approximation \(A+Bz\) with error at most

\[\frac{M}{n+1}\,2^{-d_n},\qquad M\ge0.\]

The coefficients of the local approximation may vary between triangles and stages. The mesh and evaluation schedules are executable data. These are local approximation hypotheses; no contour identity is assumed.

Theorem — Computed polygonal Cauchy cancellation

For every function and finite triangle collection satisfying this setup, the contour computation is valid and represents zero. If \(P\) is the sum of the original triangle perimeters in the coordinate-sum norm and \(S_n\) is the oriented midpoint sum, then

\[\lVert S_n\rVert_1\le\frac{MP}{n+1},\qquad \oint_{\partial\mathcal T}f(z)\,dz\simeq0.\]

Shared interior edges cancel. Thus only the boundary of the triangle collection contributes, with the reverse orientation on boundaries of holes. No approximation is required inside an omitted hole.

This theorem is checked from the displayed effective local data. Deriving those data automatically from a general represented complex derivative, and proving agreement with arbitrary tagged partitions, remain separate obligations.

The example alongside this page is Bessel’s equation of order zero:

\[y''+p(z)y'+q(z)y=0,\qquad p(z)=\frac1z,\quad q(z)=1.\]

Its only finite coefficient singularity is the origin. Multiplying the coefficients by the appropriate powers gives \(zp(z)=1\) and \(z^2q(z)=z^2\), both regular there. We will follow this one equation while constructing tools for general complex functions and systems of any finite dimension.

For a real-variable showcase of this function theory, see where the Taylor series of arctangent converges—and where it stops.

Continuity at a point and on a domain

For a valid represented real point \(a\) in the domain \(D\), continuity supplies a positive rational radius \(\delta\) for every positive rational tolerance \(\varepsilon\), such that every valid represented domain point \(y\) satisfies

\[|y-a|\le\delta\quad\Longrightarrow\quad|f(y)-f(a)|\le\varepsilon.\]

These are arithmetic and order statements about represented values. The output bound means \(-\varepsilon\le f(y)-f(a)\le\varepsilon\). For a complex function we use the same law with \(\|u\|_\infty=\max(|\Re u|,|\Im u|)\).

Continuity on \(D\) supplies this witness at every valid point of \(D\). The radius can depend on the point; a common radius for the whole domain is additional uniform continuity data. Equivalent input representations and equivalent function evaluators preserve the law.

Interval computations justify the inequalities underneath. A common output stage is not part of the definition. A derivative estimate at just one point now proves continuity there; holomorphicity supplies those witnesses throughout its domain.

The derivative fills the missing quotient value

At a point \(a\), compute a divided difference \(Q_a\) satisfying

\[f(y)-f(a)\simeq(y-a)Q_a(y),\qquad Q_a(a)\simeq f'(a).\]

The quotient is continuous at \(a\). Away from \(a\), the factorization characterizes the ordinary difference quotient. At \(a\), it gives the derivative without dividing by zero or testing equality of represented numbers. We use the same definition for real and complex functions. Differentiability throughout a domain means this construction at each point; continuity of the derivative itself is a separate property.

For the square function, the quotient is particularly simple:

\[Q_a(y)=y+a,\qquad Q_a(a)=2a.\]

Constants and identity have constant quotients. The proved sum, product, and chain rules construct quotients for finite formulas:

\[Q^{fg}_a(y)=Q^f_a(y)g(y)+f(a)Q^g_a(y),\qquad Q^{g\circ f}_a(y)=Q^g_{f(a)}(f(y))Q^f_a(y).\]

These rules establish every finite polynomial and each successive derivative at arbitrary represented inputs, with arbitrary represented coefficients. Real restrictions are checked too. Equivalent interval representations and equivalent evaluators preserve the derivative construction.

Quotient continuity proves the earlier first-order remainder estimate. With a separately supplied open domain and continuous derivative, these constructions enter the holomorphic interface below. The quotient definition itself uses neither compactness nor a completed number system. The early convex FTC retains its secant-bracket proof.

The construction from every arbitrary remainder witness back to a computed quotient remains open. Reciprocal, exponential, sine, cosine, logarithm, and root quotient adapters still need their function-specific proofs. Value algorithms and formal derivative formulas are useful inputs to those proofs.

Holomorphic functions: the working definition

We supply a function on an open domain together with a continuous complex derivative. Both are computations by shrinking rational rectangles. Their values and the domain must be independent of the chosen valid representation of an input.

At each point \(a\), and for every positive rational error \(\varepsilon\), supply a positive rational radius \(\delta\). Whenever a domain point \(z\) and a positive rational bound \(H\) satisfy

\[\|z-a\|_\infty\le H\le\delta,\]

the first-order error must satisfy

\[\|f(z)-f(a)-d(a)(z-a)\|_\infty\le\varepsilon H.\]

Here \(\|u\|_\infty=\max(|\Re u|,|\Im u|)\): a bound means that both coordinates lie in a rational interval. No square root or imported real-number foundation is needed. The estimate must hold in every complex direction, at arbitrary represented inputs.

We also supply continuity data for the derivative: a positive rational radius \(\eta\) for which

\[\|z-a\|_\infty\le\eta\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\|d(z)-d(a)\|_\infty\le\varepsilon.\]

This asks for more explicit data than the classical pointwise definition. We choose these useful sufficient hypotheses directly; equivalence with the weakest classical definition is a separate theorem. Computability of a function alone supplies neither a derivative nor a continuity proof for that derivative.

Two constructed examples

Affine functions. For \(f(z)=cz+b\), with any valid represented complex coefficients, the derivative is \(c\). The first-order remainder and derivative variation are exactly zero.

The square function. For \(f(z)=z^2\), the derivative is \(2z\), and the finite identity

\[f(z)-f(a)-2a(z-a)=(z-a)^2\]

gives a remainder bound \(2H^2\). Choosing \(\delta=\varepsilon/2\) proves the derivative estimate. The same radius controls the variation of the derivative. These are checked constructions at represented inputs, including irrational ones.

Calculus rules we can now reuse

From supplied holomorphic functions, we now construct holomorphic sums, products, and compositions, with actual derivative estimates and continuity moduli:

\[(f+g)'=f'+g',\qquad (fg)'=f'g+fg',\qquad (g\circ f)'=(g'\circ f)f'.\]

Sums and products are defined on the intersection of the two domains. A composition is defined exactly where the inner function is defined and its value belongs to the outer domain:

\[D_{g\circ f}=\{z\in D_f:f(z)\in D_g\}.\]

The chain rule computes a neighborhood whose image stays inside the outer domain. A bound read from a finite derivative box and the first-order estimate also prove continuity of the original function. These are rational computations with validity proofs; no compactness argument is used.

Polynomials, repeated derivatives, and equations

Every finite polynomial with arbitrary represented complex coefficients now has a holomorphic witness. Its formal derivative agrees with its actual analytic derivative, and this remains true at every successive order. Horner evaluation respects changes in both coefficient and input representations.

For example, the checked computation

\[p(z)=1-2z^2+z^3\]

at \(z=1+i\) gives

\[p(1+i)=-1-2i,\quad p'(1+i)=-4+2i,\quad p''(1+i)=2+6i,\quad p'''=6,\quad p^{(4)}=0.\]

For polynomial coefficients \(A,B,C\), the computed expression \(Ap''+Bp'+Cp\) is holomorphic and agrees with the analytic residual used in the differential-equation chapter. Showing that it vanishes remains the particular equation to prove. Polynomial applications no longer need separate hand constructions of their derivative maps.

What is finished, and what comes next

The basic algebraic calculus and polynomial components are complete at their stated scopes. The full function-theory program remains open. Reciprocal and represented complex-series constructors, Cauchy's formula from holomorphicity, the general identity theorem, and general Frobenius/sector continuation still need proofs.

The existing Cauchy–Taylor result starts from a supplied quantitative Cauchy representation at rational complex inputs. It does not yet reconstruct Taylor series from an arbitrary holomorphic witness. The next construction direction is direct complex series with value tails, differentiated tails, and remainder estimates, beginning with exponential and sine. That can proceed independently of the general Cauchy formula.

Checked foundation toward the monodromy theorem

The monodromy theorem starts with a germ that can be continued along every path in a simply connected domain. Simple connectedness removes dependence on the route; it does not guarantee that a germ can pass through a singularity. Uniqueness of two already existing compatible extensions needs only connectedness.

Now checked in our computable foundation: equality of represented holomorphic germs on neighborhoods; uniqueness of derivatives; gluing of supplied local holomorphic models; finite chart chains with whole-segment coverage; and invariance of locally coherent transport under finite geometric homotopies. Convexity supplies finite contractions, and affine germs instantiate the construction for arbitrary represented coefficients and base points.

\[T_{bc}(T_{ab}(s))\simeq T_{ac}(s)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad T_p(s)\simeq T_q(s)\]

The implication is proved by induction on a supplied finite filling. Global path independence is not assumed in a record. The analytic transport also comes with actual holomorphic chart chains and respects equality of the initial germs.

The full analytic monodromy theorem remains open. We still need the general identity theorem, comparison and local stability of arbitrary chart chains, a subdivision bridge from continuous homotopies to finite fillings, and locally coherent chart selection for the resulting germ section. A global evaluator from supplied coherent charts is now constructed, as are reflected open holomorphic charts and overlap comparisons. The finite geometric presentation is not silently substituted for topological simple connectedness.

Monodromy names the effect of going around a loop

A germ at a point is a local holomorphic function, identifying representatives that agree on some neighborhood of that point. If a germ can be continued uniquely around a loop, its terminal germ at the same base point is its monodromy image. For a family of germs closed under continuation along the loop and its reverse, this gives a permutation of the germs. It need not fix each germ: logarithm translates branches, and square root exchanges two branches.

For a homogeneous linear differential equation, the solution germs form a vector space, and monodromy is an invertible linear operator. Choosing a basis gives a monodromy matrix. The differential-equations chapter defines the operator, its matrix, and the monodromy group, separating these notions from the homotopy-invariance theorem.

These are the examples we should build around, in presentation order.

Arctangent: moving beyond one Taylor disk

Continue the branch with \(A(0)=0\) along the real axis to \(2\). Its original Taylor series fails there, but the function remains regular. Moving the center to \(1\) gives the classical radius \(\sqrt{2}\), set by the branch points \(\pm i\). This makes continuation useful before introducing loops or several branches. See the existing Taylor showcase and the classical branch structure.

The derivative \(1/(1+z^2)\) provides a useful companion: it has poles but returns to the same value after a loop. Arctangent can change by a constant. Tracking the normalized logarithms in

\[A(z)=\frac{\operatorname{Log}(1+iz)-\operatorname{Log}(1-iz)}{2i}\]

predicts changes of \(\pi\) and \(-\pi\) around positive loops enclosing only \(i\) or only \(-i\), respectively. Each logarithm is continued from value \(0\) at the starting point \(z=0\). This is a branch-tracking consequence of the logarithmic expression, to be proved for our chosen routes.

Logarithm: a loop records a period

Start with \(L(1)=0\). The segment from \(1\) to \(1+i\) connects continuation to the earlier computation

\[L(1+i)=\frac12\log 2+\frac{\pi i}{4}.\]

Then follow a square route once counterclockwise around the origin: the target terminal branch is \(L+2\pi i\). This connects the vertical reciprocal computation and arctangent's square-contour normalization with the logarithm's branches.

Square root: a loop switches two branches

Start with \(S(1)=1\) and \(S(z)^2=z\). The target after one loop about the origin is \(-S\); after two loops it is \(S\). Separated root enclosures should identify the branch on each neighborhood and prove agreement with the preceding one. The classical relation \(S=\exp(L/2)\) gives another route once that identity is constructed; see powers and branches.

Later: the Bessel example already alongside this page

The regular solution \(J_0\) returns to itself, while a logarithmic companion changes by a multiple of \(J_0\). This makes continuation act on a pair of solutions and reconnects the function theory with differential equations. The normalization below specifies the multiple. It should follow the elementary examples, rather than be a prerequisite for them.

Why triangles remove the regular part

A complex-affine function has zero midpoint sum around a triangle. Replace a function locally by such a model. Only the remainder contributes. Subdivision halves edge lengths and local first-order errors while quadrupling the number of triangles; their product stays controlled. Making the first-order error arbitrarily small forces the contour value to vanish.

For the common quadratic estimate, halving the diameter divides the local error by four. The total error then halves at each refinement:

\[\lVert S_n\rVert_1\le M P\,2^{-n}.\]

The Lean development proves this for arbitrary rational triangles, then for finite triangle collections. It includes an instantiated nonconstant example, \(f(z)=z^2\). Conjugation \(f(z)=\overline z\) provides a useful contrast: around the unit right triangle its integral is \(i\). A real-linear approximation is not enough; the linear term must be multiplication by one complex number.

Arctangent measures one turn around a pole

Take the counterclockwise square centered at \(a\), with positive rational half-side \(r\). Split its four sides into eight affine half-edges. Pulling back \(dz/(z-a)\), the real parts cancel in pairs and the imaginary parts add:

\[\oint_{\partial S}\frac{dz}{z-a}=8i\int_0^1\frac{du}{1+u^2}=2\pi i.\]

Here \(\pi\) is the existing geometric circle computation. The integral on the right is the existing arctangent rectangle computation. No circle contour or new definition of \(\pi\) is needed. Translation and positive dilation cancel in the rational pullback algebra.

Change the square’s size, reverse its orientation, or refine its sample mesh in the side example. The numerical sums illustrate the effect; the formal theorem supplies rational enclosures and an explicit error rate. Independently implemented midpoint and left-endpoint samplings represent the same value.

A local residue theorem

Let \(\rho\) be any valid represented complex number, including an irrational one. Suppose the regular part \(g\) has the above local approximation data on the two triangles filling the square. On its boundary, consider

\[f(z)=\frac{\rho}{z-a}+g(z).\]

Theorem — A simple pole on a rational square

The computed contour is valid and equals the residue multiplied by one positive turn:

\[\oint_{\partial S}f(z)\,dz\simeq2\pi i\rho.\]

The value is unchanged by equivalent representations of \(\rho\), the certified tag algorithm, or a different certified regular part.

This checked theorem uses a supplied simple-pole decomposition and split quadrature: the pole and regular part are integrated separately. The runtime combines actual sampled reciprocal pullbacks with actual regular-part contour computations; it does not return the proposed answer by definition. The nonconstant family \(\rho/(z-a)+z^2\) instantiates it for every rational square and every valid represented residue.

In our motivating equation, \(p(z)=1/z\) has residue \(1\), so its square contour integral is \(2\pi i\). The coefficient \(q(z)=1\) contributes zero. This explains what the contour detects in the coefficients; it does not yet prove the full behavior of the equation’s solutions.

From a local residue to Cauchy’s formula

The next bridge is to justify the regular part in

\[\frac{f(\zeta)}{\zeta-a}=\frac{f(a)}{\zeta-a}+\frac{f(\zeta)-f(a)}{\zeta-a}.\]

The divided difference must extend across the center with certified local complex-linear approximations. Once that construction is proved, the local residue theorem yields Cauchy’s value formula. Iterating the argument should provide derivative formulas and coefficient bounds.

This extension and the general Cauchy value formula are targets, not completed formalizations. Likewise, finite triangle cancellation is available, but the full geometric adapter from an arbitrary punctured polygon to finitely many small pole squares remains to be built. Higher-order principal parts and general winding numbers also remain open.

What the example asks of the foundation

Classically, one solution of our fixed equation is

\[J_0(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^k z^{2k}}{4^k(k!)^2}.\]

A second local solution, on a chosen logarithm branch, is

\[Y_*(z)=J_0(z)\log z-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^k H_k z^{2k}}{4^k(k!)^2},\qquad H_k=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac1j.\]

After one positive circuit, the logarithm changes by \(2\pi i\), so \(Y_*\) changes by \(2\pi iJ_0\). The side example follows this continuation and displays its monodromy matrix. Its floating-point series are an illustration; these complex solution branches and their monodromy are not yet certified by the Lean development. The classical formulas follow the Bessel equation and its logarithmic solution expansion.

For the formalization we use finite-dimensional systems. With \(U=(y,zy')\), this particular equation becomes

\[zU'=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-z^2&0\end{pmatrix}U.\]

The contour layer has no dependence on the order of an equation. Matrix integration acts componentwise. The second-order equation is the presentation’s running example, while continuation, coefficient estimates, and later regular-singularity results must be built for general systems.

With solution state vectors as columns, our matrix convention gives

\[\Phi=\begin{pmatrix}J_0&Y_*\\J_0'&Y_*'\end{pmatrix},\qquad \Phi^\gamma=\Phi\begin{pmatrix}1&2\pi i\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.\]

This is the monodromy matrix for one counterclockwise circuit. Reversing the circuit changes the upper-right entry to \(-2\pi i\). The identity is between local solution germs near the starting point, not merely their sampled values there.
