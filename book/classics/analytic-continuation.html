Function theory · Local knowledge and global uniqueness

Analytic continuation

Two holomorphic functions agree in a tiny neighborhood. How can that determine their agreement everywhere?

A small patch determines the whole function

For arbitrary functions, agreement on a small patch tells us little about what happens elsewhere. For holomorphic functions, it determines everything on the connected domain they share. A function known locally can therefore have at most one holomorphic extension to that domain. This rigidity is the starting point of analytic continuation.

A germ records that local function: two representatives describe the same germ when they agree on a neighborhood. The surprise is how far this modest local knowledge reaches.

Local agreement forces global agreement

Let \(f\) and \(g\) be holomorphic on the same connected open domain \(D\). If they agree on a nonempty open neighborhood in \(D\), then they agree throughout \(D\). The domain need not be simply connected. This is the identity theorem in the form that gives uniqueness of analytic extension.

But what about the logarithm?

Start near the positive real axis with the logarithm normalized by \(L(1)=0\). Travel from \(1\) to \(-1\), first along an upper semicircle, then along a lower semicircle. The continued logarithms reach different values:

\[L_{\mathrm{above}}(-1)=\pi i,\qquad L_{\mathrm{below}}(-1)=-\pi i.\]

They agreed at the start, yet disagree at the end. Where did the identity theorem stop applying?

The two continued branches are not holomorphic functions on one common connected domain containing both comparison neighborhoods. Each can be defined on a suitable slit plane, but their common domain can be disconnected: they agree on the component containing the positive real axis and differ on the component containing the negative real axis.

Two explicit domains that expose the issue

Choose one logarithm with \(-\pi/2\lt \arg z\lt 3\pi/2\), cutting along the negative imaginary ray, and the other with \(-3\pi/2\lt \arg z\lt \pi/2\), cutting along the positive imaginary ray. Their intersection is

\[\{\Re z>0\}\;\cup\;\{\Re z\lt 0\}.\]

On the right half-plane the branches agree. On the left half-plane the upper branch exceeds the lower one by \(2\pi i\). Each slit domain is connected, but their intersection is not. Thus there is no contradiction with the identity theorem. See the logarithm’s branches.

This distinguishes two questions. Uniqueness: two existing holomorphic extensions on the same connected domain must agree if they start with the same germ. Existence of one global branch: continuation along different paths might return different germs. Simple connectedness enters the monodromy theorem addressing that second question, together with the hypothesis of continuation along every path.

Why agreement spreads: a chain reaction

Suppose the functions agree near a point \(a\). All their derivatives there agree, so their Taylor series agree. Each series converges throughout every disk centered at \(a\) contained in the holomorphic domain. Agreement therefore spreads from the small starting patch to that whole disk.

Move the center inside this disk and repeat. Overlapping disks carry agreement along a route to any point of a connected open domain. In our computable foundation, the route needs a justified finite cover with rational safety margins and certified series estimates. The mathematical reason for propagation is local rigidity, not simple connectedness.

The guaranteed Taylor disk is a disk contained in the domain. The series may extend farther if the chosen domain has an artificial boundary; its exact radius is governed by genuine obstructions to that germ. We should not confuse the boundary of one displayed domain with the nearest singularity.

The usual local proof factors a nonzero Taylor expansion as

\[h(z)=(z-a)^m\bigl(c_m+(z-a)q(z)\bigr),\qquad c_m\ne0.\]

A sufficiently small neighborhood keeps the factor in parentheses away from zero. Hence a zero is isolated unless the whole local series vanishes. The set of points where the germ vanishes is then open and closed relative to the connected domain, which is another way to express the chain reaction. For our formalization, coefficient separation and tail estimates must justify the radius. See McMullen’s treatment of isolated zeros and uniqueness.

How little agreement is enough?

A line segment already determines the derivatives

We do not need an open patch to get started. Agreement on a real interval inside the domain gives agreement of all derivatives there, because the functions are holomorphic. Any straight-line interval works. For a nonzero complex direction \(v\),

\[\left.\frac{d^m}{dt^m}f(a+tv)\right|_{t=0}=v^m f^{(m)}(a).\]

Thus the one-dimensional restriction determines the complex Taylor coefficients. Its center must be an interior point of the common domain; boundary values require additional arguments.

Even a convergent sequence suffices

Let \(z_n\) be distinct points of \(D\), with \(z_n\to a\in D\), where \(f(z_n)=g(z_n)\). Set \(h=f-g\). Continuity first gives \(h(a)=0\). The difference quotients along the sequence then give \(h'(a)=0\). Once all lower Taylor coefficients vanish, the next one satisfies

\[\frac{h^{(m)}(a)}{m!}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{h(z_n)}{(z_n-a)^m}=0.\]

Discard a term equal to \(a\), if present. Inductively every coefficient vanishes, so the functions agree near \(a\); the chain reaction takes care of the rest of \(D\). This is exact determination. A certified numerical derivative algorithm additionally needs convergence and error bounds, since division by a shrinking displacement amplifies uncertainty.

The crucial example: \(\sin(1/z)\)

\[f(z)=\sin(1/z),\qquad D=\mathbb C\setminus\{0\},\qquad z_n=\frac1{n\pi}\quad(n\ge1).\]

At every \(z_n\), the function agrees with the zero function. These distinct points converge to \(0\), yet \(f\) is not identically zero: \(f(2/\pi)=1\). The missing hypothesis is precisely \(0\notin D\). The zeros have no accumulation point inside the punctured plane.

\[f\!\left(\frac1{\pi/2+2\pi n}\right)=1\]

Trivial monodromy does not make a singularity removable. For \(\sin(1/z)\), the Laurent expansion

\[\sin(1/z)=\frac1z-\frac1{3!z^3}+\frac1{5!z^5}-\cdots\]

has infinitely many negative powers. The singularity is essential, even though the function is single-valued throughout the punctured plane. Its accumulating zeros are the identity-theorem example above. The Laurent classification distinguishes this from a pole and a removable singularity.

A useful contrast is \(\sin z/z\): its apparent singularity at zero is removable, with extension value \(1\). For arctangent, the derivative \(1/(1+z^2)\) is single-valued, while the continued function gains \(\pi\) around \(i\) and loses \(\pi\) around \(-i\). The earlier examples give the normalizations and current proof pieces.

Several ways to continue the same germ

Overlapping Taylor disks

Choose a new center inside a certified convergence disk, re-expand there, and prove agreement on the overlap. Repeat along the route, keeping a positive safety margin from each disk boundary. The transported coefficients are

\[b_k=\sum_{n\ge k}\binom nk a_nh^{n-k}.\]

Finite binomial identities and explicit tails must justify this formula. A formal jet is insufficient. Arctangent continued along the real axis beyond its original Taylor disk is the first example; the function can remain regular after one particular series stops converging. See the continuation skill and Taylor showcase.

A functional equation supplies another chart

Start with Gamma’s integral on \(\Re z>0\). Its recurrence transports the known function to a larger domain:

\[\Gamma(z+1)=z\Gamma(z),\qquad \Gamma(z)=\frac{\Gamma(z+m)}{z(z+1)\cdots(z+m-1)},\quad\Re(z+m)>0.\]

The shift moves the numerator into the known half-plane. Denominator separation gives a holomorphic chart away from the nonpositive integers; repeating the recurrence proves agreement between different shifts. Those excluded points are poles, so the continuation is meromorphic on the plane. The original function must be retained: the recurrence alone also permits multiplication by one-periodic factors. These are the Gamma recurrence and continuation and poles, with a connection to our Gamma and ball-volume page. Complex Gamma charts remain to be formalized.

Schwarz reflection across a real interval

Suppose a function is holomorphic above an open real interval, extends continuously to the interval, and takes real values there. Below it, define

\[F(z)=\overline{f(\overline z)}.\]

This is the reflected chart. The Schwarz reflection principle also proves holomorphicity on the interval itself, joining the two sides. Logarithm near a positive real interval is a useful example. Boundary continuity and real values are essential hypotheses of this version; a formula below the axis alone does not establish the seam. See the reflection principle.

We now check reflection of an open holomorphic chart, its derivative and continuity radii, and its overlap comparisons. The general boundary-seam argument is still open. A particular real-coefficient series across the interval can prove both sides and the seam directly, without waiting for a general reflection theorem.

Separated roots and local differential equations

For an algebraic function, track a root in a separated box and prove local uniqueness there. That uniqueness supplies overlap agreement. For an ODE, construct a local solution and use its proved uniqueness on successive overlaps. These methods need not first derive every chart from a Taylor theorem. The holomorphic-function skill compares direct series, finite remainder estimates, real partial derivatives, integrals, reflected charts, and local equations.

An entire circle can block continuation

\[F(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}z^{2^k}=z+z^2+z^4+z^8+\cdots,\qquad |z|<1.\]

Inside any smaller disk \(|z|\le r<1\), a simple computable tail bound is

\[\left|\sum_{k=N}^{\infty}z^{2^k}\right|\le \frac{r^{2^N}}{1-r^{2^N}}.\]

Indeed, \(2^{N+j}\ge(j+1)2^N\), so a geometric tail bounds the omitted terms. Now take a dyadic root of unity \(\zeta\), with \(\zeta^{2^m}=1\). Along \(z=r\zeta\), every term after the finite prefix is positive real:

\[F(r\zeta)=\sum_{k\lt m}r^{2^k}\zeta^{2^k}+\sum_{k\ge m}r^{2^k}.\]

As \(r\) increases to \(1\), any fixed number of terms in the second sum approach that number. The prefix stays bounded, so the real part is unbounded. A holomorphic extension through \(\zeta\) would be locally bounded, which is impossible. Dyadic roots are dense on the unit circle; any open arc contains one. Thus no boundary point admits direct holomorphic extension: the circle is a natural boundary. This series is also discussed in research on power series and Dirichlet series.

The functional equation \(F(z)=z+F(z^2)\) does not overcome the obstruction: when \(|z|>1\), the transformed argument still lies outside the known disk. A continuation method needs a genuine overlap with a known chart. Divergence of a series at boundary points alone would not prove a natural boundary; the radial unboundedness argument above supplies the obstruction.

Does this work for differential equations?

Changing a solution chart must preserve its derivatives as well as its values. We now prove that equal holomorphic germs have equal derivative germs on a smaller neighborhood. If the derivatives are themselves supplied as holomorphic functions, the comparison iterates. Consequently the second-order expression \(Ay''+By'+Cy\) agrees between representatives, for arbitrary valid represented complex coefficient values.

The checked Fuchs application uses \(y=z^2\), solving \(z^2y''-zy'=0\). One edge uses direct squaring; the next uses \((z+1)^2-2z-1\). Their computations are connected by a neighborhood identity, and derivative-germ comparison transfers the equation. Exact comparisons also identify its scaled jet with the solution used by the existing ray-growth theorem.

This is a concrete application of the continuation interfaces. It does not make arbitrary Frobenius coefficient streams into holomorphic solutions. General complex-series reconstruction, solution uniqueness, uniform ray estimates, and the Bessel example's branch continuation remain separate obligations.

Checked foundations and what remains

Checked: represented germs; derivative uniqueness; local holomorphic gluing; actual finite chart chains; finite homotopy invariance from local transport laws; and the affine analytic client. We now also construct a global evaluator from supplied locally coherent charts, prove its representation invariance and holomorphicity, and reflect holomorphic charts and their overlap comparisons. These are exact represented-input results.

The main uniqueness target: prove the identity theorem on a connected open domain for arbitrary represented holomorphic functions. Simple connectedness is not a hypothesis of this theorem.

Still needed for the separate general monodromy theorem: the identity theorem for arbitrary represented holomorphic maps, comparison and local stability of arbitrary continuation chains, and a quantitative subdivision of continuous homotopies. The realization theorem now handles supplied coherent chart selection; obtaining that selection from general pathwise continuation remains part of the analytic work.

The monodromy theorem assumes the starting germ can be continued along every path in the domain. Simple connectedness then removes route dependence. The examples and method explanations on this page do not claim new Lean proofs of logarithm or square-root monodromy, complex Gamma continuation, the identity theorem, or the natural-boundary example.
