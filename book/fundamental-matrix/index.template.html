Differential equations / successive approximation

Fundamental Matrix

Second-order equations, step by step.

How can a differential equation produce its own solution, without our first guessing the answer?

We will start with position and acceleration. Repeated integration will produce two familiar functions, then two less familiar ones. Only after we have used those solutions will we put them into a matrix.

The aim is to solve equations of the form

\[y''+p(t)y'+q(t)y=g(t),\]

using one construction for the coefficients, the initial conditions and the forcing. A nonlinear example will show which part of the construction does not depend on linearity.

1. Turn acceleration into position 2. Let cosine and sine emerge 3. Try the same idea without linearity 4. Let the coefficient vary 5. Put two solutions into a matrix 6. Add a forcing term 7. Why the iteration converges

1 / The basic instruction

Turn acceleration into position.

For the moment, suppose the equation gives acceleration as a function of time and position:

\[\begin{gathered}y''(t)=F(t,y(t)),\\y(0)=a,\qquad y'(0)=b.\end{gathered}\]

Integrating once gives velocity. Integrating again gives position. These two integrations can be written together:

\[\boxed{y(t)=a+bt+\int_0^t(t-s)F(s,y(s))\,ds.}\]

The term \(a+bt\) is the motion with the prescribed initial position and velocity but no acceleration. The integral is the correction supplied by the equation.

Why the factor \(t-s\)? An acceleration contribution at time \(s\) changes the velocity, and that change acts for the remaining time \(t-s\). Algebraically, differentiating \(\int_0^t(t-s)h(s)\,ds\) once gives \(\int_0^t h(s)\,ds\), and differentiating again gives \(h(t)\). Both initial values of this integral are zero.

There is still an unknown function inside the integral. Instead of solving for it at once, put in an approximate function and repeat:

\[\begin{aligned} y^{[0]}(t)&=a+bt,\\ y^{[n+1]}(t)&=a+bt\\&\quad+\int_0^t(t-s)F(s,y^{[n]}(s))\,ds. \end{aligned}\]

This is successive approximation, or Picard iteration of the integral equation. The superscript \([n]\) labels an approximation; it is not a derivative. Every approximation already has the correct initial position and velocity.

The instruction is simple: use the current approximate position to calculate an acceleration, then integrate twice to obtain the next approximate position.

2 / A familiar equation, without a guessed solution

Let cosine and sine emerge.

Take

\[y''=-y,\qquad y(0)=1,\quad y'(0)=0.\]

Start with \(y^{[0]}=1\). The equation then prescribes the constant acceleration \(-1\), so the first improved position is

\[y^{[1]}(t)=1-\int_0^t(t-s)\,ds=1-\frac{t^2}{2}.\]

Now use this new position to calculate the acceleration:

\[\begin{aligned} y^{[2]}(t) &=1-\int_0^t(t-s)\left(1-\frac{s^2}{2}\right)ds\\ &=1-\frac{t^2}{2}+\frac{t^4}{24}. \end{aligned}\]

The next step gives

\[y^{[3]}(t)=1-\frac{t^2}{2!}+\frac{t^4}{4!}-\frac{t^6}{6!}.\]

All these integrations use the same elementary identity:

\[\int_0^t(t-s)s^k\,ds=\frac{t^{k+2}}{(k+1)(k+2)}.\]

Thus each step appends the next even power, with its factorial denominator and alternating sign. The limit is

\[u(t)=1-\frac{t^2}{2!}+\frac{t^4}{4!}-\cdots=\cos t.\]

The name comes at the end of the calculation. We did not have to recognize cosine before starting.

Change only the initial motion.

Keep the equation, but start with \(y(0)=0\) and \(y'(0)=1\). This time the initial approximation is \(t\), and the same calculation gives

\[t,\qquad t-\frac{t^3}{3!},\qquad t-\frac{t^3}{3!}+\frac{t^5}{5!},\quad\ldots\]

The limit is \(v(t)=\sin t\). We now have two solutions with two useful initial conditions:

\[\begin{array}{c|cc} &\text{initial position}&\text{initial velocity}\\\hline u&1&0\\ v&0&1 \end{array}\]

For initial position \(a\) and velocity \(b\), their combination \(au+bv\) satisfies both the equation and the initial conditions. Uniqueness therefore gives

\[\boxed{y(t)=a\cos t+b\sin t.}\]

There are two ideas here: iteration constructs the solutions; linearity allows us to combine them. We will keep those ideas separate.

3 / What survives without linearity?

We can still integrate and substitute.

Replace the equation by

\[y''=y^2,\qquad y(0)=1,\quad y'(0)=0.\]

The iteration is now

\[\begin{aligned}y^{[0]}&=1,\\y^{[n+1]}(t)&=1+\int_0^t(t-s)\bigl(y^{[n]}(s)\bigr)^2\,ds.\end{aligned}\]

Its first step gives \(y^{[1]}=1+t^2/2\). To obtain the second, square that polynomial:

\[\left(1+\frac{s^2}{2}\right)^2=1+s^2+\frac{s^4}{4}.\]

Integrate its three terms using the same identity as before:

\[\boxed{y^{[2]}(t)=1+\frac{t^2}{2}+\frac{t^4}{12}+\frac{t^6}{120}.}\]

At the next step, the low-degree terms become:

\[y^{[3]}(t)=1+\frac{t^2}{2}+\frac{t^4}{12}+\frac{t^6}{72}+\cdots.\]
The complete third polynomial \[\begin{aligned} y^{[3]}(t)&=1+\frac{t^2}{2}+\frac{t^4}{12}+\frac{t^6}{72}\\ &\quad+\frac{t^8}{560}+\frac{11t^{10}}{64800}\\ &\quad+\frac{t^{12}}{95040}+\frac{t^{14}}{2620800}. \end{aligned}\]

The dots above stand for four further terms in a finite polynomial, not a Taylor-series remainder.

Notice what happened to the coefficient of \(t^6\): it changed from \(1/120\) to \(1/72\). Unlike the cosine example, a Picard step can correct an existing coefficient, not just append a new one. Picard iterates are not, in general, Taylor truncations.

Why do these particular iterates converge?

On \(0\le t\le1/2\), the iteration sends every continuous function with values in \([1,2]\) to another such function: its output lies between \(1\) and \(1+2t^2\le3/2\).

For \(r,s\in[1,2]\), we have \(|r^2-s^2|\le4|r-s|\). If \(\mathcal T\) denotes this iteration, then

\[\begin{aligned}\|\mathcal T f-\mathcal T h\|_\infty &\le4\frac{(1/2)^2}{2}\|f-h\|_\infty\\ &=\frac12\|f-h\|_\infty.\end{aligned}\]

Successive differences therefore decrease geometrically. The iterates converge uniformly to a function satisfying the integral equation, hence the differential equation and its initial conditions. This is a local argument; it does not assert existence for all time.

But the second idea from the oscillator example has failed. If \(y''=y^2\), then \((2y)''=2y^2\), whereas \((2y)^2=4y^2\). Multiplying a solution by two no longer gives a solution.

Picard iteration does not need linearity. A fundamental matrix uses the additional fact that solutions of a homogeneous linear equation can be added and scaled.

4 / A variable coefficient

Let the equation make its own functions.

Return to a linear equation, but let its coefficient change with time:

\[y''=ty.\]

We again construct \(u\) from initial values \((1,0)\), and \(v\) from \((0,1)\). The only change in the instruction is the extra factor \(s\):

\[\begin{aligned} u^{[n+1]}(t)&=1+\int_0^t(t-s)s\,u^{[n]}(s)\,ds,\\ v^{[n+1]}(t)&=t+\int_0^t(t-s)s\,v^{[n]}(s)\,ds. \end{aligned}\]

Starting from \(u^{[0]}=1\) and \(v^{[0]}=t\), the first steps are

\[\begin{aligned} u^{[1]}&=1+\frac{t^3}{6},\\ u^{[2]}&=1+\frac{t^3}{6}+\frac{t^6}{180},\\ v^{[1]}&=t+\frac{t^4}{12},\\ v^{[2]}&=t+\frac{t^4}{12}+\frac{t^7}{504}. \end{aligned}\]

For example, the new term in \(u^{[2]}\) is obtained by integrating \(s(s^3/6)\) twice:

\[\int_0^t(t-s)\frac{s^4}{6}\,ds=\frac{t^6}{180}.\]

Continuing gives

\[\begin{aligned} u(t)&=1+\frac{t^3}{6}+\frac{t^6}{180}+\frac{t^9}{12960}+\cdots,\\ v(t)&=t+\frac{t^4}{12}+\frac{t^7}{504}+\frac{t^{10}}{45360}+\cdots. \end{aligned}\]

This is Airy’s equation. Its usual named solutions, \(\operatorname{Ai}\) and \(\operatorname{Bi}\), are different linear combinations of these two normalized solutions.[1] We do not need those names or their prior definitions to carry out the construction.

Just as for the oscillator, the solution with initial values \((a,b)\) is \(au+bv\). What changed was the pair of functions—not the method or the way initial conditions enter.

5 / The reason for the title

Put two solutions into a matrix.

Now consider the homogeneous equation

\[y''+p(t)y'+q(t)y=0.\]

Assume \(p\) and \(q\) are continuous on an interval containing \(0\). As before, let \(u\) and \(v\) be the solutions with initial values \((1,0)\) and \((0,1)\). Arrange their positions in the first row and their velocities in the second:

\[X(t)=\begin{pmatrix}u(t)&v(t)\\u'(t)&v'(t)\end{pmatrix}.\]

Our chosen initial conditions say that \(X(0)=I_2\), the identity matrix.

Then one multiplication gives both position and velocity:

\[\begin{pmatrix}y(t)\\y'(t)\end{pmatrix} =X(t)\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\end{pmatrix}.\]

This is the normalized fundamental matrix. Its first column answers “what happens to unit initial position?” Its second answers “what happens to unit initial velocity?” There are four entries, but only two independent solutions.

For the oscillator, it is simply

\[X(t)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos t&\sin t\\-\sin t&\cos t\end{pmatrix}.\]

The same integration idea constructs the whole matrix.

To include the \(y'\) term without introducing another solving trick, keep position and velocity together. Write

\[x=\begin{pmatrix}y\\y'\end{pmatrix},\qquad A(t)=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-q(t)&-p(t)\end{pmatrix}.\]

The second-order equation is exactly \(x'=A(t)x\). Integrate once and substitute repeatedly, now for both columns at once:

\[\begin{aligned}X_0(t)&=I_2,\\X_{n+1}(t)&=I_2+\int_0^t A(s)X_n(s)\,ds.\end{aligned}\]

The first two steps are

\[\begin{aligned} X_1(t)&=I_2+\int_0^t A(s)\,ds,\\ X_2(t)&=I_2+\int_0^t A(s)\,ds\\ &\quad+\int_0^t A(s)\int_0^s A(r)\,dr\,ds. \end{aligned}\]

Continuing gives the Peano–Baker series.[2] It is just the result of repeatedly substituting the matrix integral equation into itself. Matrix products stay in their displayed order: for variable coefficients, matrices evaluated at different times need not commute.

This matrix iteration and the twice-integrated scalar iteration construct the same solutions, but their finite stages need not agree. One round here updates position and velocity together; one round earlier substituted directly into a formula for position.

Why do the two columns remain independent?

The determinant is the Wronskian \(W=uv'-u'v\). Using the differential equation gives

\[W'=uv''-u''v=-pW,\qquad W(0)=1.\]

Direct differentiation checks the solution

\[W(t)=\exp\!\left(-\int_0^t p(r)\,dr\right)\ne0.\]

Thus \(X(t)\) is invertible throughout the coefficient interval. In particular, when \(p=0\), as in both of our linear examples, \(W=1\).

6 / The same equation, with an input

Add a forcing term.

Suppose now that

\[\begin{gathered}y''+p(t)y'+q(t)y=g(t),\\y(0)=a,\qquad y'(0)=b.\end{gathered}\]

Nothing prevents us from putting the forcing into Picard iteration from the beginning. With the position-and-velocity notation of Section 5 and \(e_2=(0,1)^{\mathsf T}\), it is just

\[\begin{aligned}x_{n+1}(t)&=x(0)+\int_0^t A(s)x_n(s)\,ds\\&\quad+e_2\int_0^t g(s)\,ds,\end{aligned}\]

starting with the constant vector \(x_0(t)=x(0)=(a,b)^{\mathsf T}\). Adding the forcing changes only what is placed under the integral. When \(u\) and \(v\) have already been constructed, we can also organize the answer so as to reuse them.

First ask what a unit change in velocity does.

Let \(k(t,s)\) be the homogeneous solution which starts at time \(s\) with zero position and unit velocity:

\[k(s,s)=0,\qquad \partial_t k(s,s)=1.\]

These two conditions determine its linear combination of \(u\) and \(v\):

\[k(t,s)=\frac{u(s)v(t)-v(s)u(t)}{W(s)},\]

where \(W(s)=u(s)v'(s)-u'(s)v(s)\) is the Wronskian from Section 5.

Check the conditions: at \(t=s\) the numerator is zero; its derivative in \(t\) is \(W(s)\). For a fixed departure time \(s\), the function solves the homogeneous equation because it is a linear combination of \(u(t)\) and \(v(t)\).

A forcing contribution \(g(s)\,ds\) changes the velocity at time \(s\). Its later contribution to position is \(k(t,s)g(s)\,ds\). Add the contributions from all departure times:

The solution formula

For continuous \(p,q,g\) on an interval containing \(0\), the unique solution is

\[\begin{aligned}y(t)&=a\,u(t)+b\,v(t)\\&\quad+\int_0^t k(t,s)g(s)\,ds.\end{aligned}\]

The first two terms account for the initial motion. The integral accounts for the forcing. The same \(u,v\) serve every choice of \(a,b,g\).

The interpretation suggests the answer, but it is also easy to verify. Put \(w(t)=\int_0^t k(t,s)g(s)\,ds\). Since \(k(t,t)=0\), differentiation gives

\[\begin{aligned} w'(t)&=\int_0^t \partial_t k(t,s)g(s)\,ds,\\ w''(t)&=g(t)+\int_0^t \partial_t^2 k(t,s)g(s)\,ds. \end{aligned}\]

It follows that \(w''+pw'+qw=g\), with \(w(0)=w'(0)=0\). This proves the forcing part of the formula directly; there is no need to introduce unknown variable coefficients in a proposed solution.[3]

Return to the oscillator.

Here \(u=\cos t\), \(v=\sin t\), and \(W=1\). The response to unit velocity at time \(s\) is

\[\begin{aligned}k(t,s)&=\cos s\sin t-\sin s\cos t\\&=\sin(t-s).\end{aligned}\]

Thus

\[\begin{aligned}y(t)&=a\cos t+b\sin t\\&\quad+\int_0^t\sin(t-s)g(s)\,ds.\end{aligned}\]

For zero initial data and constant forcing, \(g=1\), the integral is

\[y(t)=\int_0^t\sin(t-s)\,ds=1-\cos t.\]

For \(g(s)=\cos s\), use the product-to-sum identity inside the same integral:

\[\begin{aligned} y(t)&=\frac12\int_0^t\bigl[\sin t+\sin(t-2s)\bigr]ds\\ &=\frac{t}{2}\sin t. \end{aligned}\]

The second sine term integrates to zero. This is resonance, but no exceptional rule was needed: the factor \(t\) emerged from the integral.

Return to the variable coefficient.

For \(y''=ty+1\) with zero initial data, we can either use the \(u,v\) of Section 4 in the forcing formula, or carry out the original iteration with the forcing already included:

\[\begin{aligned}w^{[0]}&=0,\\w^{[n+1]}(t)&=\int_0^t(t-s)\bigl(s\,w^{[n]}(s)+1\bigr)\,ds.\end{aligned}\]

The first three steps give

\[\begin{aligned} w^{[1]}&=\frac{t^2}{2},\\ w^{[2]}&=\frac{t^2}{2}+\frac{t^5}{40},\\ w^{[3]}&=\frac{t^2}{2}+\frac{t^5}{40}+\frac{t^8}{2240}. \end{aligned}\]

The forcing formula is a way to organize and reuse this construction, not a separate method for a different class of equations.

Where is the fundamental matrix in the forcing formula?

The matrix taking position and velocity from time \(s\) to time \(t\) is

\[U(t,s)=X(t)X(s)^{-1}.\]

Its upper-right entry is exactly \(k(t,s)\): it gives the position resulting from initial state \((0,1)\) at time \(s\). With \(e_2=(0,1)^{\mathsf T}\), the position-and-velocity formula is

\[x(t)=X(t)x(0)+\int_0^t U(t,s)e_2\,g(s)\,ds.\]

Alternatively, construct \(U(t,s)\) by the same matrix iteration starting at \(s\); this requires no matrix inversion. For variable coefficients, the departure time matters: generally \(U(t,s)\ne X(t-s)\).

7 / From approximations to a solution

Repeated integration supplies the factorials.

For the oscillator, we saw factorial denominators appear explicitly. The same mechanism controls the general linear equation, even when the integrals do not simplify.

Write the successive matrix terms as

\[\begin{aligned}P_0&=I_2,\\P_{n+1}(t)&=\int_0^t A(s)P_n(s)\,ds.\end{aligned}\]

The matrix iterate \(X_N\) is \(P_0+\cdots+P_N\). On a compact interval containing \(0\), choose \(M\) with \(\|A(t)\|\le M\), using a submultiplicative matrix norm normalized by \(\|I_2\|=1\). Induction gives

\[\|P_n(t)\|\le\frac{M^n|t|^n}{n!}.\]

For example, integrating the bound \(M^n s^n/n!\) and multiplying by \(M\) produces exactly the next bound \(M^{n+1}t^{n+1}/(n+1)!\) for \(t\ge0\). Reversing the integration direction gives the same estimate with \(|t|\).

The exponential majorant shows that the matrix series converges uniformly on every such compact interval. Substituting its sum into the integral equation gives \(X'=AX\) and \(X(0)=I_2\). The iterates therefore construct actual solutions, not merely formal power series.[2]

Why is the solution unique?

The difference \(d\) of two state-vector solutions with the same initial value satisfies \(d(t)=\int_0^t A(s)d(s)\,ds\). On a compact interval with \(|t|\le T\), let \(D=\sup\|d(t)\|\). Repeated substitution gives

\[\|d(t)\|\le D\frac{(MT)^n}{n!}\quad\text{for every }n.\]

The right-hand side tends to zero, so \(d=0\). This also proves uniqueness for the forced equation, since the forcing cancels when the two solutions are subtracted.

For a nonlinear equation, one instead needs control of how \(F(t,y)\) changes with \(y\), and of where the iterates stay. That is why the nonlinear example included an explicit interval and a Lipschitz estimate. The linear theorem has no finite-time blow-up inside an interval on which its coefficients remain continuous.

We placed the initial time at \(0\) only to keep notation light. Any initial time works, with the integration limits changed accordingly. An equation with a non-unit leading coefficient can first be divided by it on an interval where it is nonzero. Singular points and boundary-value problems require separate consideration.

What to remember. Integrate the equation, substitute the previous approximation, and repeat. For a linear second-order equation, two starting motions give two reusable solutions. Their positions and velocities form the fundamental matrix; their response to a unit velocity change supplies the forcing integral.
