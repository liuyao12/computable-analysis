Exponential and logarithm

Compute growth, accumulated change, and logarithmic ratios with rational error bounds.

We begin with two computations that are already useful on their own: factorial sums for the exponential and reciprocal rectangles for a logarithm. We then connect them to differentiation, integration and finite substitutions. A numerical example uses the actual Lean evaluator; a theorem explains why its enclosures are valid.

A factorial computation for the exponential

For a rational input \(x\), compute

\[E_N(x)=\sum_{j=0}^N\frac{x^j}{j!}.\]

Choose a rational \(R\ge |x|\) and a degree with \(r=R/(N+2)<1\). Every ratio after the first omitted absolute term is at most \(r\), so a geometric tail encloses the answer:

\[E(x)\in\left[E_N(x)-\frac{R^{N+1}}{(N+1)!(1-r)},\ E_N(x)+\frac{R^{N+1}}{(N+1)!(1-r)}\right].\]

The evaluator computes its degree and tail from the rational input and stage. Its validity theorem covers every rational input. For calculus on a whole interval, we also use a common-prefix version: one degree controls every sample in a fixed bounded interval. That uniform estimate is what makes simultaneous sample refinement legitimate.

Numerical tables use exact Lean computations with decimal endpoints rounded outwards. A stage is not a promised number of decimal digits; the width in the returned interval is the accuracy certificate.

Growth and the derivative rule

Finite polynomial algebra gives an increment estimate for each factorial prefix. A common tail bound controls the omitted terms, so the divided-difference error tends to zero. On the current checked chart \([-1,1]\), the common-prefix exponential satisfies

\[E'=E,\qquad E(0)\simeq1.\]

The derivative statement and the evaluator's identification with the original factorial-series program are distinct. The solution certificate supplies the basic local model of growth; it does not silently include a uniqueness theorem. Extending the calculus rules to arbitrary bounded intervals, and to scaled equations such as \(y'=ay\), is the next reusable step.

TheoremSeries and repeated multiplication compute the same e

The factorial-series computation \(E(1)\) agrees with the computation that returns

\[\left[\left(1+\frac1m\right)^m,\ \left(1+\frac1m\right)^{m+1}\right],\qquad m=1,2,\ldots.\]

The finite comparison uses binomial coefficients and factorial tails. It identifies independently executed programs, rather than defining one by the other.

Repeated multiplication explains the growth interpretation, while factorial terms usually give much narrower intervals for the same small number of stages. This difference is visible without invoking a floating-point exponential routine.

Accumulating exponential growth

A rectangle computation samples the exponential at rational mesh points. There are two choices: the domain mesh and the precision of each sample. The existing construction supplies a joint plan, finite error bounds and a proof that the enclosures shrink. The derivative rule then gives an endpoint evaluation.

TheoremThe exponential integral
\[\boxed{\int_0^1 E(x)\,dx\simeq E(1)-1.}\]

The left side is the specified exponential quadrature, with a separate validity certificate. The right side uses the factorial value at one and rational subtraction.

For this implementation, prefix stabilization uses endpoint-width information to choose a conservative enlargement. The proof map must expose that actual construction. It must not claim that the earlier cosine program, a new generic quadrature, or an unproved arbitrary-interval formula was used instead.

A logarithm from reciprocal rectangles

On \([1,2]\), the function \(1/x\) is positive and decreasing. Its values at rational inputs are rational, and the endpoint rectangle gap is explicit. The implemented conservative dyadic enclosure has width \(2/2^k\). No derivative theorem is required to construct it.

There is a second computation: adjacent partial sums of the alternating harmonic series. If

\[A_m=\sum_{j=1}^m\frac{(-1)^{j+1}}j,\]

then \([A_{2n},A_{2n+1}]\) has width \(1/(2n+1)\). A finite identity connects the two constructions:

\[A_{2n}=H_{2n}-H_n=\sum_{j=n+1}^{2n}\frac1j.\]

The last sum is exactly the right rectangle sum for \(1/x\) on \([1,2]\) with \(n\) equal cells. Thus the equivalence rests on rational finite sums and shrinking gaps, not on importing a preexisting real logarithm.

TheoremTwo computations of log 2
\[\boxed{\log2\simeq\int_1^2\frac{dx}{x}\simeq1-\frac12+\frac13-\frac14+\cdots.}\]

Here \(\log2\) names the common computed number; the series and integral retain their distinct instructions.

The tables deliberately display the existing conservative algorithms. Their slow convergence is a reason to develop range reduction and faster logarithm series, not to replace the displayed bounds with values from an unverified library call.

A useful finite substitution

Set \(t=u^2\). On a finite mesh, its increments satisfy

\[(u+h)^2-u^2=2uh+h^2.\]

Multiplying these increments by reciprocal-kernel samples compares a nonuniform mesh with the uniform-mesh quadrature of \(2u/(1+u^2)\). The extra \(h^2\) terms are bounded and summed. They are not dropped by treating differentials as exact finite increments.

TheoremA logarithmic rational integral
\[\boxed{\int_0^1\frac{2u}{1+u^2}\,du\simeq\int_1^2\frac{dx}{x}\simeq\log2.}\]

The two quadratures are independently defined. The proof transports finite sums through the square substitution and controls their refinement error.

This is a useful pattern for later substitutions: choose the transformed partition, retain the finite correction, and prove agreement of the resulting computations. It also connects naturally to the rational circle kernels in the preceding chapters.

The next completed block

The next priority is a logarithm evaluator on every supplied positively separated rational interval, together with its derivative and scaling laws. From there we can prove \(L(E(x))\simeq x\), \(E(L(y))\simeq y\), and the usual real-power rules on explicit domains. These are current development targets, not results supplied by the declarations above.

That block leads directly to growth and decay, doubling times, real powers, and elementary first-order differential equations. Taylor remainder bounds and range reduction should be developed alongside it, so the functions are useful numerically as well as formally.
