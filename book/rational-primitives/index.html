Elementary primitives

Rational functions, repeated poles, and the half-angle route from trigonometry to rational algebra.

The general analytic theorem remains open. The checked results now include complete local elementary primitives for rationally split denominators, alongside computed partial fractions and formal integration from rational linear/quadratic factorizations and a domain-preserving rational circle substitution. They use the project’s own foundation, with no Mathlib dependency.

The theorem we want

On an interval with an explicit positive lower bound for its denominator, every rational function should have an elementary primitive:

\[F'(x)=\frac{p(x)}{q(x)},\qquad |q(x)|\geq\delta>0.\]

The answer may use real algebraic constants, rational operations, logarithms of absolute values, and arctangents. Algebraic constants matter: even \(1/(x^2-2)\) naturally introduces \(\sqrt2\). Higher-degree algebraic roots need not have radical expressions.

A primitive belongs to a pole-free interval. A complex version additionally needs logarithm branches; it does not promise a single-valued primitive throughout a punctured plane.

Factorization can be supplied

The fundamental theorem of algebra is needed to obtain factors for an arbitrary denominator. Once the factorization is supplied and checked, the integration algorithm does not need FTA:

\[q(x)=c\prod_i(x-a_i)^{m_i}\prod_j\bigl((x-u_j)^2+v_j\bigr)^{n_j},\qquad c\ne0,\quad v_j>0.\]

RationalFactoredPrimitives.Factorization now accepts rational factor data, arbitrary multiplicities, a nonzero leading coefficient, the factorization identity, and a decidable check that distinct blocks do not collide. It computes the partial fractions and checks the elementary formula’s formal derivative. Neither the partial fractions nor an antiderivative are assumed.

Quadratic coefficients are computed by division modulo the quadratic. For a residual denominator \(v_1y+v_0\) modulo \(y^2+b\), the inverse uses the positive rational norm \(v_0^2+bv_1^2\). This step needs no square-root evaluator.

General FTA factors may have irrational algebraic coefficients: \(x^4+1=(x^2+\sqrt2\,x+1)(x^2-\sqrt2\,x+1)\). Extending the coefficient representation and proving the quadratic analytic evaluators are still required for the unrestricted theorem.

What repeated poles contribute

After polynomial division and real algebraic factorization, partial fractions reduce the problem to a polynomial and terms of these forms:

\[\frac{c}{(x-a)^m},\qquad \frac{A(x-a)+B}{((x-a)^2+b)^m},\qquad b>0.\]

Linear poles integrate to logarithms or reciprocal powers. For positive quadratics, put \(u=x-a\), \(Q=u^2+b\), and \(I_m=\int Q^{-m}\,dx\). One arctangent starts a terminating recurrence:

\[I_1=\frac1{\sqrt b}\arctan\frac{u}{\sqrt b},\qquad I_{m+1}=\frac{u}{2mbQ^m}+\frac{2m-1}{2mb}I_m.\]

The linear part of the numerator gives \((A/2)\log Q\) at a simple pole and \(-A/(2mQ^m)\) at higher orders. All remaining steps are finite rational arithmetic.

quadraticPrimitive_correct proves the recurrence’s formal derivative identity for every multiplicity. Decomposition.primitive_correct proves the corresponding identity for every supplied rational partial-fraction decomposition. The computed formula retains its pointwise domain.

Actual analytic primitives

For every finite rational coefficient list, RationalPrimitivePolynomial.hasDerivative proves that the coefficient primitive has the original polynomial as its two-sided interval derivative, on any rational interval:

\[P(x)=\sum_{j=0}^d c_jx^j,\qquad F(x)=\sum_{j=0}^d\frac{c_j}{j+1}x^{j+1},\qquad F'=P.\]

The logarithm series is also a valid interval computation. Its quadratic remainder estimate gives the exact reciprocal derivative of \(\log(1+x)\) on \([-1/2,1/2]\). Rational affine changes of variable support both positive and negative slopes.

For any rational pole \(a\) and rational center \(z\ne a\), the algorithm chooses \(r=|z-a|/2>0\) and constructs

\[F_{a,z}(x)=\log\!\left(\frac{x-a}{z-a}\right),\qquad F'_{a,z}(x)=\frac1{x-a},\qquad z-r\le x\le z+r.\]

simplePole_hasDerivative is an actual HasDerivativeOnInterval certificate. Its only hypothesis is that the center avoids the pole. Runtime precision depends on the step, so division by an arbitrarily small step does not amplify an unaccounted box error. Both sides of every rational pole are covered.

HasDerivativeOnInterval.add also combines independently scheduled derivative certificates on a common interval. A checked polynomial-plus-logarithm example exercises this rule; no matching-schedule assumptions are required.

From factorization to an actual primitive

For every rational numerator and every denominator given as distinct rational linear factors with arbitrary multiplicities, the complete local construction is checked. Synthetic division computes the partial-fraction coefficients; no partial-fraction identity is assumed.

\[q(x)=\prod_{i=1}^k(x-a_i)^{m_i},\qquad q(z)\ne0,\qquad r=\min(1,|z-a_1|/2,\ldots,|z-a_k|/2)>0.\]

RationalPrimitiveAssembly.splitPrimitive returns a valid elementary evaluator on \([z-r,z+r]\) and proves that its interval derivative is \(p/q\). Its elementary witness describes the evaluator itself: polynomials, reciprocal powers, normalized logarithms, rational scaling, finite sums, and restriction.

The radius is computed and proved positive; the whole interval avoids every denominator pole. A companion theorem handles any checked rational linear factorization, including its leading coefficient.

For example, the algorithm computes

\[\frac{1+2x}{x^2(x-1)^3}=-\frac1{x^2}-\frac5x+\frac3{(x-1)^3}-\frac4{(x-1)^2}+\frac5{x-1}.\]

It then assembles an elementary evaluator with this derivative on \([3/2,5/2]\). Repeated reciprocal powers are proved by finite secant estimates, including negative coefficients and intervals on either side of a pole.

Why sine and cosine reduce to the same problem

The tangent-half-angle substitution \(t=\tan(\theta/2)\) gives

\[\sin\theta=\frac{2t}{1+t^2},\qquad \cos\theta=\frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2},\qquad d\theta=\frac{2}{1+t^2}\,dt.\]

Every rational expression in the two coordinates therefore becomes a rational integrand, including the change-of-variable factor. Lean checks that finite statement for every expression and every rational chart input.

An antipodal chart uses \(u=-\cot(\theta/2)\), negates both circle coordinates, and has the same positive Jacobian. Two charts cover every rational point of the circle. Turning this into the analytic trigonometric corollary still requires the represented angle identities, a chain rule, and compatible constants on chart overlaps.

Poles survive the compiler

Algebraic simplification must not silently enlarge a function’s domain. The expression \(1/(1/x)\) remains undefined at zero, even though its value elsewhere is \(x\). So does \(0\cdot(1/x)\).

The compiler inverts \(p/q\) as \(q^2/(pq)\), retaining the original denominator as a guard. compile_correct proves equality of partial evaluations, and pullback_defined_iff proves that the circle substitution neither removes nor adds an integrand pole on the chart.

Checked examples

\[\int\frac{dx}{x(x-1)}=\log|x-1|-\log|x|+C.\]
\[\int\frac{dx}{(1+x^2)^2}=\frac{x}{2(1+x^2)}+\frac12\arctan x+C.\]

These examples are checked at the formal-derivative level. Further regressions preserve undefined reciprocals and verify that the half-angle pullback of \(1/(1+\cos\theta)\) is one on the first chart.

What remains to prove

  1. Construct partial fractions for arbitrary rational denominators over represented real algebraic constants. The current normal forms use rational constants only.
  2. Construct the remaining quadratic elementary evaluators and extend coefficients to represented algebraic constants.
  3. Extend the completed rational linear-pole assembly to quadratic and algebraic terms.
  4. Connect the two circle charts to the existing represented sine and cosine functions, then prove derivative transport and overlap gluing.

The existing general ComputableFTA is a target proposition, not a proved factorization theorem available to close the first step. This page does not claim that either requested general analytic theorem has been formalized.
