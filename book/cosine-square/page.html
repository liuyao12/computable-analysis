The cosine-square integral

Cosine-squared and sine-squared, integrated from zero to any real endpoint. The quarter-area result is a corollary.

\[\boxed{\begin{aligned} \int_0^x\cos^2(\pi t)\,dt&=\frac{x}{2}+\frac{\sin(2\pi x)}{4\pi},\\ \int_0^x\sin^2(\pi t)\,dt&=\frac{x}{2}-\frac{\sin(2\pi x)}{4\pi}. \end{aligned}}\]

These identities hold for every real \(x\), with the usual signed orientation when \(x<0\). The formal statements quantify over every valid represented real, including irrational endpoints. Both integrands are defined everywhere, so there are no singularities to exclude.

From the circle to the integrand

The rational circle point is \[P(u)=\left(\frac{1-u^2}{1+u^2},\frac{2u}{1+u^2}\right).\] Polygon areas define the angle clock and geometric \(\pi\). A closed rational search inverts that clock; the first coordinate supplies \(C(x)=\cos(\pi x)\). Thus the function to integrate is \(f(x)=C(x)^2\), decreasing from \(1\) to \(0\) on \([0,1/2]\).

The coordinate identities come from this construction. In particular, reflection exchanges the cosine and sine coordinates, and their squares add to \(1\). The geometric construction develops these ingredients before this example.

From local rectangles to arbitrary endpoints

First compute the integral on a variable part of a quarter-turn chart. With the internal coordinate \(u=2t\), the local endpoint formula is \[P(u)=\frac{u}{2}+\frac{\sin(\pi u/2)\cos(\pi u/2)}{\pi},\qquad 0\le u\le1.\] The local integral is independently defined by scaled rectangle sums. On successive charts, the squared cosine alternates between squared cosine and squared sine; the circle identity complements each rectangle. A proved equality of complete-cell integrals permits their finite sum to be compressed. Signed complete cells plus the last partial cell give the integral at every rational endpoint.

For a represented real endpoint, the program evaluates these rational-endpoint integrals at successive rational approximations. A proved uniform bound controls the change in value when the endpoint changes. The closed-form expression is evaluated separately from the geometric circle coordinates. Both extensions have shrinking rational enclosures, agree with their rational-input values, and respect equality of represented inputs. No caller supplies a partition, a period index, or an internal precision schedule.

Only rational approximations are assigned a period index. The construction does not require deciding which side of a period boundary an arbitrary real lies on; the formulas are proved to agree at those boundaries.

Half-integer values are corollaries

Substitute \(x=n/2\), where \(n\in\mathbb Z\). The oscillating term vanishes, giving \[\int_0^{n/2}\cos^2(\pi t)\,dt=\int_0^{n/2}\sin^2(\pi t)\,dt=\frac n4.\] In particular, both integrals from \(0\) to \(1/2\) equal \(1/4\). A geometric proof of the half-interval corollary

Reflect the graph in the vertical line \(x=1/4\). The reflected height is \[ f\!\left(\frac12-x\right)=\sin^2(\pi x),\qquad f(x)+f\!\left(\frac12-x\right)=1. \] Reflection preserves area, so the two integrals agree. Their sum is the area of the rectangle of width \(1/2\) and height \(1\). Writing the original integral as \(I\),

\[2I=\frac12,\qquad I=\frac14.\]

The formal proof makes this a finite-sum argument. Reflection pairs a left sample with a right sample. The circle identity cancels their squared heights; the remaining endpoint gap and rational evaluation error have explicit bounds tending to zero. No primitive is used to evaluate this integral.

First general proof: the product rule

Use \[ F(x)=\frac{x}{2}+\frac{\sin(\pi x)\cos(\pi x)}{2\pi} =\frac{x}{2}+\frac{\sin(2\pi x)}{4\pi}. \] The product rule and the derivative formulas give \[ F'(x)=\frac12+\frac{\cos^2(\pi x)-\sin^2(\pi x)}2 =\cos^2(\pi x). \] The fundamental theorem gives the formula at every endpoint: \[ \int_0^x\cos^2(\pi t)\,dt=F(x)-F(0)=\frac{x}{2}+\frac{\sin(2\pi x)}{4\pi}. \]

In Lean, the product rule constructs a quantitative derivative certificate for this primitive. A finite FTC telescopes its increments along a fixed mesh; the certified comparison with the chosen rectangle computation then lets that mesh refine. The local argument is restricted to every variable partial cell and then assembled and extended as above. Subtracting the cosine-square rectangles from the constant-height rectangles gives the sine-square formula. This value proof does not invoke the symmetry evaluation.

Second general proof: integrate the exponential

Euler’s identity gives the power-reduction formulas \[\cos^2\theta=\frac{1+\operatorname{Re}e^{2i\theta}}2, \qquad \sin^2\theta=\frac{1-\operatorname{Re}e^{2i\theta}}2.\] The prerequisite is the exponential integration law. For a nonzero constant frequency \(\omega\), \[E'(t)=i\omega E(t)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \int_a^b E(t)\,dt=\frac{E(b)-E(a)}{i\omega}.\] Indeed, writing \(E=R+iI\) gives \(I'=\omega R\) and \(R'=-\omega I\). Thus the real and imaginary integrals are respectively the endpoint differences of \(I/\omega\) and \(-R/\omega\). Applying this with \(\omega=2\pi\) gives \[\int_0^x\operatorname{Re}e^{2\pi i t}\,dt=\frac{\sin(2\pi x)}{2\pi},\] and adding or subtracting half of this expression from \(x/2\) proves both formulas.

The checked exponential construction here is differential: the geometric circle coordinates solve the rotation equation with initial value \(1\), and a proved uniqueness theorem identifies every supplied solution with those coordinates. The squared circle point supplies the doubled angle. The proof does not assume Euler’s identity or an integral formula in a certificate, and it does not claim a new bridge to a power-series exponential.

Lean first proves the exponential integration law for supplied derivative models and justified quadrature samples, including an exact represented-value version. It then applies that law to the geometric exponential on each variable chart. The audit checks that this local value proof does not depend on the product-rule square model or its integral evaluation. The global proofs share the chart construction, continuity bounds, and representation-invariant extension.

Why the square matters

Squaring measures the size of an oscillation without cancellation. The average squared height on this quarter period is \[ \frac{1}{1/2}\int_0^{1/2}\cos^2(\pi x)\,dx=\frac12. \] The other quarters have the same squared area. A sinusoid of amplitude \(a\) therefore has mean square \(a^2/2\) and root-mean-square amplitude \(|a|/\sqrt2\). This is the normalization behind signal energy and many Fourier calculations.
