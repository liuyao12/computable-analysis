\section{Elementary primitives: the finite reduction}
\label{sec:rational-primitives}

The target theorem states that a rational function on an interval whose
denominator is uniformly apart from zero has an elementary primitive.
Arbitrary certified computable-real coefficients are allowed. The full rational-function primitive theorem remains open;
the checked results include complete local analytic primitives for rationally
split denominators, computed formal primitives with computable coefficients,
and a domain-preserving circle substitution.



\begin{theorem}[Formal integration with computable-real coefficients]
\label{thm:computable-factored-primitives}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.ComputableFactoredAlgebra.primitive_correct, ComputableAnalysis.ComputableFactoredAlgebra.FactoredRational.primitive_correct}
\leanok
Given a denominator as linear and positive quadratic blocks, with arbitrary
certified computable-real coefficients and finite interval separation
certificates, the algorithm computes partial fractions and an elementary
formula. At a certified domain point, its represented formal derivative is
equivalent to the original factored rational function. The numerator and
leading coefficient may also be computable reals. No factorization theorem
or partial-fraction identity is assumed. This is a formal-derivative result;
analytic realization for general computable coefficients remains open.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Arithmetic expressions retain the original valid interval algorithms as
parameters. Finite rational sampling proves the division and recurrence
identities, and shared samples prove overlap of the resulting shrinking
interval computations. Positivity and apartness are certified with rational
endpoint comparisons, not decisions of equality of computable reals. The
closed regression uses $r=\sqrt{2}$ in
\[
  \frac{1+rx}{(x-r)((x-r/2)^2+r)^2}.
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Rational specialization: supplied factorization]
\label{thm:factored-rational-primitive-formula}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.RationalFactoredPrimitives.Factorization.decomposition,
ComputableAnalysis.RationalFactoredPrimitives.Factorization.primitive_correct}
\leanok
Given a checked factorization with rational coefficients into linear and
irreducible quadratic blocks, the algorithm computes the partial fractions
and an elementary formula whose formal derivative is the original quotient
at every permitted rational input. Multiplicities are arbitrary.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}\leanok
Linear blocks use synthetic division. For a quadratic \(y^2+b\), the
nonzero residual denominator \(v_1y+v_0\) has inverse
\((-v_1y+v_0)/(v_0^2+bv_1^2)\) modulo that quadratic. Positivity of the
rational norm gives exact division. Remove each repeated factor in turn,
then apply the checked finite integration recurrences.
\end{proof}
This theorem assumes factorization as data, not FTA as an axiom. FTA would
supply algebraic, not necessarily rational, coefficients in general. The
quadratic conclusions here are formal identities; their represented analytic
realization remains separate.

\begin{theorem}[Supplied partial fractions]
\label{thm:rational-primitive-formula}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.RationalPrimitiveFormula.Decomposition.primitive_correct}
\leanok
Given a certified decomposition with rational coefficients into a polynomial,
linear poles and positive quadratic poles, the finite primitive algorithm
returns a formula whose formal derivative is the input rational function at
every permitted rational input.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}\leanok
For \(Q=(x-a)^2+b\), \(b>0\), repeated quadratic poles reduce by
\[
 I_1=\frac1{\sqrt b}\arctan\frac{x-a}{\sqrt b},\qquad
 I_{m+1}=\frac{x-a}{2mbQ^m}+\frac{2m-1}{2mb}I_m.
\]
Linear numerators reduce to logarithms and reciprocal powers of \(Q\).
Linear poles give logarithms or reciprocal powers of \(x-a\); polynomial
coefficients integrate termwise. Induction checks the finite identities.
This is a statement about formal differentiation, not a derivative
certificate for a represented elementary evaluator.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Analytic polynomial primitives]
\label{thm:rational-polynomial-primitive-analytic}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.RationalPrimitivePolynomial.hasDerivative}
\leanok
Every rational polynomial has its termwise coefficient primitive as a
represented two-sided primitive on every rational interval.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}\leanok
Finite addition of normalized monomial secant bounds supplies an explicit
step modulus. Exact rational boxes have zero runtime width.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Analytic local logarithm primitives]
\label{thm:rational-logarithm-primitive-analytic}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.RationalPrimitiveLogarithm.simplePole_hasDerivative,
ComputableAnalysis.RationalPrimitiveLogarithm.poleRadius_pos}
\leanok
For rational \(a,z\) with \(z\ne a\), the positive radius
\(r=|z-a|/2\) defines a chart on which
\[
 F_{a,z}(x)=\log\left(\frac{x-a}{z-a}\right),\qquad
 F'_{a,z}(x)=\frac1{x-a}.
\]
The theorem constructs \texttt{HasDerivativeOnInterval}, with no analytic
certificate supplied among its hypotheses.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}\leanok
Restrict the stabilized logarithm Taylor series to the real axis. Its finite
quadratic remainder and geometric box widths give the derivative, with a
step-dependent runtime precision. Its derivative boxes contain the exact
reciprocal. Rational affine charts, of either sign, give the displayed result.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Addition without schedule hypotheses]
\label{thm:rational-primitive-sum-analytic}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.HasDerivativeOnInterval.add}
\leanok
Two represented derivative certificates on a common interval can be added,
with no compatibility assumptions on their original precision schedules.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}\leanok
Refine both runtime stages to their maximum. Nestedness controls the error
of independently shrinking near intervals by three times the original
tolerance. Multiply the natural step precisions and spend finer tolerances
on refinement and addition.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Computed partial fractions and analytic assembly]
\label{thm:rational-split-primitive-analytic}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.RationalPartialFractions.decomposition,
ComputableAnalysis.RationalPrimitiveAssembly.splitPrimitive,
ComputableAnalysis.RationalPrimitiveAssembly.radius_pos,
ComputableAnalysis.RationalPrimitiveAssembly.denominator_near}
\leanok
Let \(q(x)=\prod_i(x-a_i)^{m_i}\), where the rational pole centers are distinct
and the positive multiplicities are arbitrary. For every rational polynomial
\(p\) and rational \(z\) with \(q(z)\ne0\), the algorithm computes an elementary
primitive of \(p/q\) on \([z-r,z+r]\), with
\[
 r=\min(1,|z-a_1|/2,\ldots,|z-a_k|/2)>0.
\]
It returns a valid interval evaluator, an elementary syntax witness tied to
that evaluator, and an actual \texttt{HasDerivativeOnInterval} certificate.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}\leanok
Repeated synthetic division computes every partial-fraction coefficient.
Finite reciprocal-coordinate secant estimates handle repeated linear poles;
normalized logarithms handle simple poles. Restrict all atoms to the common
interval and synchronize rational scaling and finite addition. The computed
partial-fraction identity identifies the derivative with the original quotient.
\end{proof}


\begin{theorem}[Concrete logarithm and arctangent derivative certificates]
\label{thm:computable-elementary-derivatives}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.ComputableLogarithmChart.Coefficient.hasDerivative,
ComputableAnalysis.ComputableLogarithmChart.Coefficient.weightedHasDerivative,
ComputableAnalysis.ComputableLogarithmChart.simplePole_derivative_equiv,
ComputableAnalysis.ComputableLogarithmChart.arctan_valueSample,
ComputableAnalysis.ComputableLogarithmChart.quadraticLog_derivative_equiv,
ComputableAnalysis.ComputableLogarithmChart.quadraticArctan_derivative_equiv}
\leanok
Local logarithm series with arbitrary computable complex slopes, and arbitrary
computable constant multiples, have actual finite secant derivative certificates.
The real-coordinate simple-pole derivative agrees with the original reciprocal
evaluator, including irrational poles. Pure imaginary slopes give the standard
arctangent Taylor candidates. For $Q(x)=(x-a)^2+b^2$ and $b\ne0$, weighted real
and imaginary coordinates have derivatives $2(x-a)/Q(x)$ and $1/Q(x)$,
respectively, on their computed rational charts, given successful reciprocal
certificates for $Q(c)$, $b$, and $Q(x)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}\leanok
Search rational interval widths to approximate each coefficient, retaining a
common sampling stage for all arithmetic expressions. On $|mt|\le1/2$, the
finite logarithm secant error is at most $2M^2|h|+8M2^{-n}$, where $M$ bounds
the slope. A finite Lipschitz estimate controls coefficient perturbations.
Stabilize the shrinking rational boxes and synchronize their widths with the
secant step. Exact rational identities identify the derivative with the original
coefficient arithmetic. Irrational pole, scale, and quadratic examples use the
existing bisection computation of $\sqrt2$.
\end{proof}
The positive constant in the formal factor API still needs to be connected to
a certified width $b$; the full repeated-factor assembly remains open.

\begin{theorem}[Rational circle pullback]
\label{thm:rational-circle-pullback}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.TrigonometricRationalization.Expr.pullback_correct,
ComputableAnalysis.TrigonometricRationalization.Expr.pullback_defined_iff}
\leanok
Every rational expression in two circle coordinates pulls back to a rational
function under
\[
 s=\frac{2t}{1+t^2},\qquad c=\frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2},
 \qquad J=\frac2{1+t^2}.
\]
The computed rational function equals the expression times \(J\), and is
defined exactly when the original expression at those coordinates is defined.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}\leanok
Structural induction on rational expressions and domain-preserving polynomial
normalization. The reciprocal of \(p/q\) is stored as \(q^2/(pq)\), so the
original poles are not filled by cancellation.
\end{proof}

Two antipodal charts cover the rational circle. Factorization existence,
the remaining elementary realizations and their analytic assembly,
and transport through the represented angle charts are still needed for the
full rational-primitive theorem and its trigonometric corollary.

\begin{theorem}[Elementary-expression primitives in sine and cosine]
\label{thm:trigonometric-primitive-formula}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.ComputableTrigonometricRationalization.RepresentedFactorization.primitive_correct,
ComputableAnalysis.ComputableTrigonometricRationalization.Expr.pullback_defined_iff,
ComputableAnalysis.ComputableTrigonometricRationalization.halfAngle_derivative,
ComputableAnalysis.TrigonometricPrimitiveExamples.derivative_is_expected}
\leanok
For every rational expression in sine and cosine with arbitrary computable-real
coefficients, a certified linear/quadratic factorization of the half-angle
pullback denominator determines an elementary formula whose represented formal
angle derivative agrees with the original expression, whenever the finite
coefficient evaluations and domain certificates succeed. The primitive formula
and all partial-fraction coefficients are computed.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}\leanok
The compiler retains all denominator guards. Set
\[
 q(t)=\frac2{1+t^2}R\left(\frac{2t}{1+t^2},\frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2}\right).
\]
Integrate this factored rational expression using the checked finite recurrence.
The circle relation and sine/cosine derivative rules give the formal factor
$t'=(1+t^2)/2$, which cancels the pullback Jacobian. Common rational samples
transport the identity to equivalence of the original computable coefficient
algorithms. Factorization identities involving algebraic roots are transported
by raw-real equivalence, without requiring their finite rational samples to
satisfy the same polynomial identities. The opposite chart negates both circle coordinates and preserves
the same Jacobian.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Finite primitive change of variables]
\label{thm:primitive-finite-change-of-variables}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.PrimitiveChangeOfVariables.FiniteSecantCompositionDerivativeOnInterval.toHasDerivativeOnInterval,
ComputableAnalysis.TrigonometricPrimitiveExamples.zero_inner_increment}
\leanok
Finite inner and outer secant models, a bound for the outer derivative,
explicit error budgets, composite-box membership, and width bounds construct
an actual two-sided interval derivative certificate for the composite.
A zero finite inner increment is permitted.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}\leanok
Split the composite error into the outer linearization residual divided by
the physical step and the outer derivative times the inner secant error.
Pay for each with half the requested error, then apply the existing
finite-model derivative theorem.
\end{proof}
The formal angle identity is proved for all expressions, but the composite
box membership and schedules have not yet been constructed for all their
primitive evaluators. Thus the full interval trigonometric primitive theorem
remains open.
