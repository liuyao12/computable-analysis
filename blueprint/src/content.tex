\chapter*{Preface}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Preface}

This book develops calculus by constructing things: rational enclosures for
numbers, polygonal areas and solid volumes, trigonometric values, rectangle
integrals, secant computations of derivatives, power-series prefixes, and
finite evolutions of linear systems.  Every infinite process comes with a
proof that its finite computations agree and become arbitrarily accurate.

The emphasis is on useful examples and reusable constructions, not on the
largest possible classes of set-theoretic functions.  Rational inputs do
not force rational outputs.  Continuity data permits evaluation on computed
inputs without making a completed real line the foundation of the subject.
An identity means equivalence of two computations, often obtained in very
different ways.

After the interval foundations, geometry comes before trigonometry.  Inner
and outer polygons compute the circle; finite cylinder stacks and conical
frusta establish the sphere--cylinder volume and surface comparisons.
These geometric exhaustions require neither angle functions nor a general
integral.  A separate chapter then uses sector area to construct angles,
sine, and cosine.  The subsequent route is
\[
 \begin{gathered}
 \text{independent rectangle integrals}
 \ \longrightarrow\ \text{convex secant derivatives and the FTC}\\
 \longrightarrow\ \text{series, elementary functions, and algebraic branches}\\
 \longrightarrow\ \text{Fourier computations, jumps, and delta inputs}\\
 \longrightarrow\ \text{fundamental matrices and an elliptic integral}.
 \end{gathered}
\]

There is a reason for this order.  A function can be continuous and
differentiable at every rational point while hiding a jump at an irrational
cut.  The fundamental theorem must not mistake its pointwise derivative
for all its increments.  Convex secant inequalities supply the missing
interval control and construct the derivative itself.  Explicit jumps
can subsequently be retained as delta inputs, rather than discarded.

The fundamental matrix then unifies accumulation and propagation.  An
ordinary forcing term is integrated against it; an impulse is a finite
state update propagated by the same matrix.  The ramp, the oscillator,
the Airy equation, and an elliptic integral give concrete instances.

All mathematical prerequisites needed for these constructions are developed
in the text from finite rational algebra, with definitions, estimates, and
proofs.  Theorems about unrestricted function or distribution spaces are
not used to supply missing computations.  Conversely, a proved finite
construction is not held back because a more general theory could also
contain it.
