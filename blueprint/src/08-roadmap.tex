\chapter{Formalization Roadmap}

\section{What counts as progress}

The project should prefer theorem statements that end in one of the following
finite conclusions:
\[
  \RealRaw.\Valid,\qquad
  \ComplexRaw.\Valid,\qquad
  x\Equiv y,\qquad
  \close_\eps(I,J).
\]
These are the computational substitutes for classical existence and equality.
Whenever a classical proof says "choose a sufficiently fine partition" or
"take a small enough neighborhood", the blueprint must replace it by an
executable rational algorithm with an explicit modulus.  No raw-real
completeness principle is available or planned as a proof shortcut.

\section{Near-term milestones}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Finish the exact order layer:
  prove the remaining order laws for \cref{def:raw-real-order}, especially
  transitivity and compatibility with raw-real arithmetic under valid
  representatives.

  \item Strengthen explicit interval constructors:
  package concrete nested rational stage algorithms together with their
  orderedness and width-shrinking moduli.  Do not introduce infimum, supremum,
  or completeness principles for bounded monotone families; applications must
  carry the finite approximation data they use.

  \item Construct one-sided convex derivatives:
  supply explicit nested rational interval algorithms, then verify
  \cref{def:convex-right-derivative} and
  \cref{def:convex-left-derivative} by their rational-secant lower/upper-bound
  and greatest/leastness laws.  These laws characterize a supplied algorithm;
  they do not construct it by infimum or supremum.

  \item Prove monotone integrability:
  use the definite-integral construction from Chapter~\ref{ch:integrals} to
  prove \cref{thm:monotone-integrable}.

  \item Close the convex FTC:
  prove \cref{thm:convex-ftc} by manufacturing the derivative-bound
  certificates of \cref{thm:effective-ftc-certificate} from convexity,
  one-sided derivatives, monotone integrability, and telescoping secant
  inequalities, then promote them to the \code{DefiniteIdentityFor}
  interface used by the pi routes.

  \item Prove the interval arithmetic formula-identification rules:
  sums, scalar multiples, products, and reciprocals should transform
  formula-identification cell controls into new cell controls.

  \item Finish geometric arctangent cell control:
  prove the finite sector-area inequalities in \cref{ex:arctan-ftc} and use
  them to construct the kernel comparison route for \(1/(1+x^2)\).

  \item Derive arctangent Taylor soundness from FTC:
  combine geometric arctangent cell control with the finite kernel remainder
  identity to prove that the geometric and power-series arctangents agree on
  \([0,1]\), giving the analytic bridge needed for pi.

  \item Geometric pi validity is complete:
  the direct area and circumference raw evaluators are certified and
  equivalent.  Further geometry work is therefore optional infrastructure rather
  than a prerequisite for calculus.

  \item Choose an FTA route:
  either develop algebraic complex numbers with isolation boxes, or develop
  the argument-principle box-search route.  The algebraic-number file now keeps
  arithmetic closure and algebraic closure as explicit target propositions, so
  there are no hidden theorem placeholders in that layer.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Calculus readiness ledger}\label{sec:calculus-readiness}

The pi integration suite below is a release-style integration suite, not a percentage
of readiness for scientific or engineering calculus.  It is useful because it
forces independently constructed finite geometry, series, integration, and
inverse-function routes to agree on a demanding constant.  It is an incomplete
proxy, however: several rows can depend on one arctangent bridge, while a
major advance such as a continuous Peano--Baker theorem would add no pi row.
We therefore judge the main objective by the following non-substitutable
gates.  A ``checked'' boundary means an end-to-end Lean theorem for the stated
claim, not merely that the relevant interface has been declared.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{p{0.23\textwidth}p{0.36\textwidth}p{0.29\textwidth}}
\textbf{Gate} & \textbf{Current checked boundary} & \textbf{Next boundary}\\
\hline
Rational interval foundation & \code{RealRaw.Valid}, overlap equivalence, and
the audit excluding completed-real completeness and Mathlib analysis & keep the
dependency audit current as the module graph grows\\
Continuity and extension & interval regularity proves literal rational
epsilon--delta continuity; scheduled square root on \([0,1]\) is a checked
non-exact example with a quadratic modulus & general
representation-respecting extension closure beyond the current certified
extension interface\\
Finite integration and FTC & certified integral constructions,
derivative-bound endpoint bridges, and concrete rectangle/Cauchy/quartic
routes; the finite rectangle identity and refinement machinery behind
\cref{def:integration-by-parts-certificate} & reusable construction from
interval regularity and standard-function derivative certificates, followed by
general bounded piecewise integration-by-parts and substitution theorems\\
Monotone inverse functions & branch-local inverse and bisection interfaces;
unit-interval square-root search for rational targets & represented targets,
then sine/arcsine and exponential/logarithm branches\\

finite-difference affine/square examples & a selected exponential raw with

scalar (E'=E, E(0)=1) uniqueness specialization identifying the selected

\end{tabular}
\end{center}

There is intentionally no aggregate percentage: these gates have distinct
dependencies, and success in one does not compensate for a missing theorem in
another.  The pi table remains valuable as secondary coverage.  Lean groups
the checked representations by their primary exercised capability (finite
geometry, geometric arctangent, finite integrals, alternating series, or a
compactified improper integral).  Add a row only when it exercises a genuinely
new foundation or end-to-end bridge; do not inflate it with presentation
variants of the same arctangent construction.  The unchecked formulae are
capability probes, not a denominator for this ledger.

\paragraph{Scientific-calculus boundary.}
The intended result is a small certificate kernel for scientific and
engineering arguments, not yet a replacement for a general-purpose analysis
library.  An LLM should be able to propose a familiar antiderivative or
calculation, and the kernel should check it after the general FTC,
substitution, and integration-by-parts bridges have been proved.  At present
those bridges are only partly instantiated: the project does not yet provide
general product, chain, quotient, integrability, or endpoint theorems for
arbitrary raw functions.  It also lacks the analytic (E'=E) certificate,
the exp/log identifications, completed trigonometric calculus, continuous
matrix Peano--Baker boxes, and the numerical/PDE/domain layers needed by many
applications.  Thus the current boundary is a research prototype for tightly
certified rational one-variable calculations, rather than a drop-in basis for
proving arbitrary real-world models.

The area evaluator is the preferred \(\pi\) representation.  Direct
circumference, arctangent series and rectangle integration, Machin's single
power-series formula, and the Cauchy and reciprocal-quartic integrals are
named, independently checkable alternatives.  The direct circumference proof
checks the finite exceptional stages and a uniform later-stage estimate; it
strengthens the Archimedes row rather than creating a duplicate one.

The completed finite integration-by-parts and square-substitution rows are
deliberately narrower than their canonical exp/log formulations.  The
unmarked rows identify the next genuine gates: canonical \(\log 2\), the
self-derivative and uniqueness of exponential, and complex rotation.  Basel
currently supplies valid series and both area- and direct-circumference
\(\pi^2/6\) right-hand sides, together with their equivalence; it does not yet
claim \(\zeta(2)=\pi^2/6\).

\section{A useful style rule}

Every major theorem should have three layers:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a readable mathematical statement in the blueprint;
  \item a Lean proposition or structure with the same quantifier shape;
  \item small finite lemmas that discharge the numerical or algebraic
  estimates.
\end{enumerate}
This keeps the project faithful to the historical mathematics while making
the formal work genuinely computable.
