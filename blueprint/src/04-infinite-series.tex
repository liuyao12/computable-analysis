\chapter{Power Series and Fourier Analysis}\label{ch:infinite-series}

The second route to the same circle quantity is the arctangent power series.
It comes after the effective FTC: finite coefficient identities and tail
bounds can now be interpreted as integrals and primitives when needed.
Fourier analysis is the later complex-valued extension of this same
finite-prefix-and-tail method, not a prerequisite for the elementary route.
An infinite object is represented by a finite prefix and a certified tail.
\begin{verbatim}
prefix -> finite identity -> tail bound -> nested enclosure
\end{verbatim}

This chapter also follows the representative rule: formalize one evaluator
for each genuinely new series or transform pattern.  Sign changes, rational
scalings, routine algebra, and finite piecewise assembly are transported from
those representatives; the blueprint does not repeat the same proof for
every syntactic variant.

\texttt{ComputableAnalysis.SeriesFoundation}.  It collects the finite
coefficient, Fourier-tail, Gaussian-prefix, and (n)-ball interfaces without
importing the benchmark catalogue or a completed function space.

Fourier analysis enters first as an exact finite transform on rational-complex
lists. Root-of-unity and cancellation facts are explicit certificates; no
infinite function space is assumed.
\begin{verbatim}
complex samples -> finite transform -> orthogonality/cancellation -> coefficients
\end{verbatim}

block repetition are checked finite statements.  The next layer is now also
formalized: a stagewise coordinate-tail certificate turns finite transforms

\section{The computable Fourier-series contract}

The project’s Fourier object is not a function space completed by an
orthogonality theorem. It is a finite coefficient stage with a coordinate tail
certificate:

\begin{verbatim}
sample table / coefficient stage -> rational-complex transform
                                -> coordinate tail radius
                                -> future-stage containment
                                -> precision witness
\end{verbatim}

The tail radius is the essential analytic input. It must be nonnegative,
shrink to zero, and contain every later real and imaginary coordinate at the
earlier stage’s budget. Once those fields are supplied, the generic
stabilizer produces a valid complex raw computation. A finite-support series
is the zero-tail regression case; a geometric quarter-turn series is the
first genuinely infinite-looking case.

This gives a precise admission rule for Fourier applications. A coefficient
formula may be new, but it must end in the same finite transform plus tail
interface. Pointwise convergence, termwise integration, or an L2-style
claim is a further theorem only after its own finite norm or tail certificate
has been supplied.

The complex exponential, sine, and cosine evaluators expose their finite
increment laws directly: increasing a prefix by one stage adds exactly one
rational-complex term.  These laws are the induction interface for later
tail, derivative, and termwise-integration proofs.

The weighted-prefix identities make the index shift explicit: the derivative
of the term at index (k+1) is (z) times the term at index (k).  Thus the
finite prefix theorem is an exact algebraic statement; no infinite
interchange is being hidden in the notation.

Geometric, arithmetic, alternating, power-sum, Fourier, and reciprocal-series
examples use one interface. Divergence is expressed as potential infinity:
every finite target is eventually reached, with an explicit stage.

The convergence claim is never a completed infinite sum.  It is a pair of
finite obligations: later prefixes stay inside the earlier enclosure, and a
chosen tail budget eventually fits inside any requested rational width.
\begin{verbatim}
N := choose_tail_budget(eps)
prefix := sum(term, 0, N)
return prefix +/- certified_tail(N)
\end{verbatim}

The reusable series contract is:

\begin{verbatim}
term evaluator + finite algebra + tail bound
                  -> valid shrinking realRaw
                  -> equivalence with another computation
\end{verbatim}

Termwise differentiation or integration is admitted only after the finite
coefficient identity and the transported tail bound have both been proved.
This is the series analogue of the effective FTC: a global identity is a
chain of finite interval containments.

At the coefficient level, integration is a concrete constructor: prescribe the
constant term and divide the n-th derivative coefficient by (n+1). The
formal derivative then recovers the input stream exactly.  This is the finite
algebraic core of termwise FTC; convergence and interval tails are separate
obligations.

\section{Pi and power series}
\label{thm:shared-rational-anchor}
\label{thm:leibniz-arctan}

The project studies the space of computations, not only the fastest one.
Crude rectangles, binomial expansions, termwise integration, and alternating
series can be compared through a shared rational anchor.
\begin{verbatim}
choose route -> compute prefix -> compare enclosures -> refine
\end{verbatim}
This is the setting for Leibniz's series and the faster power-series routes to
$\pi$: the historical lesson is methodological, not a claim about Newton's
private reasoning.

Termwise differentiation and integration are finite coefficient identities
followed by a tail transport certificate.  The effective FTC in
Chapter~\ref{ch:integrals} supplies the corresponding endpoint interpretation
when a series is used as a primitive.

The finite integration identity is already closed before any tail argument:
extending an integrated Taylor prefix adds one explicit endpoint monomial, and
folding those increments gives the complete finite prefix formula.
Thus the phrase ``integrate term by term'' has a literal rational meaning here:
the endpoint difference of the finite primitive is exactly the finite sum of
the integrated monomials.  Only the later tail comparison is potentially
infinite-looking.

The derivative side is closed at the same finite level. The derivative prefix
of the integrated coefficient stream is literally the truncated target
prefix, provided the coefficient-shift certificate is supplied. This is the
finite algebraic core of termwise FTC; no infinite derivative or interchange
theorem is being smuggled in.

\section{Basel and factorial growth}
\label{thm:basel-geometric-overlap}

Basel compares two certified computations of the same quantity: reciprocal
square prefixes and geometric area. Factorial growth supplies the tail bounds
used by Stirling-type and Gaussian estimates.
\begin{verbatim}
algorithm A -> interval A       algorithm B -> interval B
                         \        /
                         overlap
\end{verbatim}

The computable version stops at the finite certificate that the application
actually uses. For a Gaussian, that certificate is an even Taylor prefix on
a bounded interval, together with a rational allowance for the omitted terms:

\begin{verbatim}
exp(-x^2) prefix -> integrate each monomial on [-R,R]
                 -> finite prefix +/- explicit tail majorant
                 -> interval for every later finite prefix
\end{verbatim}

This proves bounded, finite-stage Gaussian estimates. It does not silently
assert an improper integral over the whole line; that next step requires a
separate pointwise domination and tail certificate. The same interface also
makes the usual separability argument transparent: a finite rectangular sum
of a product factors into the product of its one-dimensional sums.

The arbitrary-dimensional weighted form is the reusable finite
multiple-integral theorem: each cell contributes a rational width times its
factor value, the nested rectangular sum factors, and nonnegative widths and
factors give a nonnegative result. Nothing here invokes Fubini, measure, or a
completed real space.

For the Gaussian, the pointwise Taylor prefix
\(\operatorname{expPrefix}_m(-x^2)\) supplies the factors.  Its two-dimensional
weighted rectangle sum is therefore a finite tensor product, with an explicit
factorization theorem ready for later tail estimates.

Thus the Gaussian and n-ball entries are in scope as finite, separable,
precision-refinable computations. A full Gaussian identity or a general
multiple-integral theorem is admitted only after its evaluator supplies the
corresponding finite tail and transport certificates; the name of a classical
integral does not provide those certificates for free.

The next finite layer is a tail ladder. At selected rational points, the
exponential evaluator itself certifies the Gaussian bound
exp(-x^2) <= 1/x^2. Summing those certified boxes gives a rational budget
for a finite collection of tail cells:

\begin{verbatim}
point x -> exp(-x^2) interval -> compare with 1/x^2
       -> add finitely many tail cells -> rational upper budget
\end{verbatim}

This is the correct entry point for an improper Gaussian argument. To close a
full-line theorem one still needs a uniform rule that covers every later cell
and proves the remaining budget can be made arbitrarily small. The finite
ladder is evidence for that provider, not a disguised appeal to Lebesgue
measure or to an attained point at infinity.

The tail partials themselves are ordered data: for a nonnegative cutoff every
finite reciprocal-square sum is nonnegative. This elementary certificate can
be composed with later comparison bounds without introducing an infinite sum.
