\chapter{Computing Derivatives and the Fundamental Theorem}
\label{ch:effective-calculus}

The derivative should be constructed from the function.  For convex
functions, neighboring secants provide its lower and upper bounds.  The
same ordering then bounds its integral by endpoint differences.  This
chapter proves that connection without a mean value theorem.

\section{What pointwise rational differentiation misses}
At a rational $x$, saying that $g(x)$ is a derivative means that, for every
rational $\varepsilon>0$, all sufficiently small nonzero rational $h$
satisfy
\[
 \left|\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}h-g(x)\right|\le\varepsilon.
\]
The neighborhood can depend on $x$.  By itself this says nothing about
nearby pairs on opposite sides of a missing cut.  For example
\[
 H(x)=\begin{cases}0,&x^2<2,\\1,&x^2>2\end{cases}\qquad(x\in\Q)
\]
is locally constant at every rational, yet $H(2)-H(0)=1$.  Its pointwise
rational derivative is zero everywhere.  Adding $S(x)$ gives exactly the
same failure for a function with pointwise derivative $\pi C(x)$.

There is no contradiction with the constructions of the preceding chapters:
this $H$ has no interval continuity certificate across the cut.  More
importantly, a derivative computation needs a reason why its finite
approximations are enclosures, not just a collection of local guesses.
Convexity provides that reason.

\section{Convexity is a finite interval condition}
\begin{definition}
A function computation is convex on a rational interval if
\[
 f((1-t)x+ty)\le(1-t)f(x)+tf(y)
\]
for rational $x,y$ in the interval and rational $0\le t\le1$.
\end{definition}
Writing $s(u,v)=(f(v)-f(u))/(v-u)$, the defining inequality gives
\[
 s(u,v)\le s(u,w)\le s(v,w)\qquad(u<v<w).
\]
Conversely these secant inequalities imply convexity by rearranging.

On a smaller interval $[a,b]$ with outer rational points $A<a<b<B$,
\[
 s(A,a)\le s(x,y)\le s(b,B)\qquad(a\le x<y\le b).
\]
Coarse computations of the two outer slopes therefore give a rational
Lipschitz bound for $f$ on $[a,b]$.  This is a finite proof of interval
continuity, not a consequence of a real-variable convexity theorem.

Midpoint convexity is already enough on a rational interval.  To prove
Jensen's inequality for $n$ equally weighted entries, pad them with copies
of their rational average to a list of length $2^k$.  Repeated midpoint
inequalities apply to this list; cancel the padding terms.  Taking repeated
copies of two entries gives every rational weight.

\section{The derivative algorithm}
Fix a rational $x$ and a positive rational $h_0$ with $x\pm h_0$ in the
convexity interval.  Put $h_n=2^{-n}h_0$ and form
\[
 \ell_n=\frac{f(x)-f(x-h_n)}{h_n},\qquad
 r_n=\frac{f(x+h_n)-f(x)}{h_n}.
\]
Secant ordering proves
\[
 \ell_n\le\ell_{n+1}\le r_{n+1}\le r_n.
\]
These endpoints are computed quantities.  To get a rational box, compute
the three values at $x-h_n$, $x$, and $x+h_n$ to widths at most
$\eta_n=h_n2^{-n-3}$.  Denote their enclosures by
\[
 [a_-,b_-],\qquad [a_0,b_0],\qquad [a_+,b_+].
\]
Set
\[
 B_n=\left[\frac{a_0-b_-}{h_n},\frac{b_+-a_0}{h_n}\right],
 \qquad D_n=\bigcap_{j=0}^nB_j.
\]
All lower secant bounds are below all upper secant bounds, so these finite
intersections are nonempty.  Moreover
\[
 \operatorname{width}(B_n)
 \le r_n-\ell_n+2^{-n-1}.
\]

\begin{definition}[Computed derivative]\label{def:computed-derivative}
When the gap
\[
 r_n-\ell_n=\frac{f(x+h_n)-2f(x)+f(x-h_n)}{h_n}
\]
shrinks to zero, the nested rational intervals $D_n$ define $Df(x)$.
A precision request is answered by searching for a sufficiently narrow
$D_n$.  The shrinking-gap proof establishes termination.
\end{definition}

Convexity alone does not promise termination.  For $f(x)=|x|$ at zero,
the ideal brackets remain $[-1,1]$.  For $f(x)=x^2$, they are
$[2x-h_n,2x+h_n]$, and the result is $Df(x)=2x$.

\begin{proposition}[The computation is the usual derivative]
For a convex function, shrinking secant gaps give a pointwise rational
derivative equal to $Df(x)$.  Conversely any such derivative forces the
gaps to shrink.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For every rational $0<|t|\le h_n$, secant ordering brackets
$(f(x+t)-f(x))/t$ between $\ell_n$ and $r_n$.  The computed derivative
has the same bounds.  Their difference is therefore at most $r_n-\ell_n$.
Conversely the two outer difference quotients approach any given derivative,
so their difference shrinks.  This also proves that changing $h_0$ or
refinement schedules gives an equivalent derivative computation.
\end{proof}

\section{The convex FTC}
\begin{theorem}[Fundamental theorem for a computed convex derivative]
\label{thm:convex-ftc}
Let $f$ be convex on an interval containing $[a,b]$ in its interior.
Suppose its derivative computation terminates at every rational point of
$[a,b]$.  Then $Df$ has a monotone integral computation and
\[
 \boxed{\int_a^b Df(x)\,dx=f(b)-f(a).}
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Passing to arbitrarily narrow derivative brackets in the secant inequalities
gives, for rational $x<y$,
\[
 Df(x)\le\frac{f(y)-f(x)}{y-x}\le Df(y).
\]
Thus $Df$ is nondecreasing.  On every rational partition $P$,
\[
 L_P(Df)\le f(b)-f(a)\le U_P(Df),
\]
by adding the primitive increments.  The gap is at most
\[
 \mesh(P)\bigl(Df(b)-Df(a)\bigr).
\]
The monotone integral of Chapter~\ref{ch:integrals} was constructed
independently of $f$.  Its shrinking enclosures overlap the endpoint
difference, proving equivalence.  Finite value-evaluation errors are
accounted for by the additional $2\eta(b-a)$ bound from that chapter.
\end{proof}

Only finitely many derivative evaluations occur at any integration stage.
Their individual terminating precision searches suffice; no uniform
modulus of differentiability is assumed.  Endpoint variants use inner
one-sided secants and explicitly supplied finite one-sided derivatives.
One can alternatively prove the theorem on interior intervals and pass to
endpoints with bounded-derivative error estimates.

\begin{example}[An irrational corner is allowed]
The rational-valued convex function $f(x)=\max(0,x^2-2)$ has
\[
 Df(x)=\begin{cases}0,&x^2<2,\\2x,&x^2>2\end{cases}
\]
at every rational point.  The branch comparison is decidable and never
an equality.  On $[0,2]$, this derivative is monotone but not uniformly
continuous.  Nevertheless
\[
 \int_0^2 Df(x)\,dx=2,
\]
and equal $N$-cell endpoint sums have gap $8/N$.  A hidden corner in a
continuous convex primitive is harmless; a hidden jump in the primitive
is not.  The latter violates the finite secant bounds across the cut.
\end{example}

\section{Differences of convex computations}
If $f=u-v$ with two convex computations whose derivatives terminate, define
$Df=Du-Dv$.  The pointwise derivative theorem shows independence of the
chosen decomposition.  Linearity of the independent integral gives
\[
 \int_a^b Df=f(b)-f(a).
\]
Concave functions are included by negation.  Compatible finite pieces may
be added by splitting the interval and cancelling matching endpoint values.

A useful concrete certificate is that $f(x)+Kx^2$ is convex for a rational
$K\ge0$.  It must be proved by finite inequalities or remainder bounds;
a lower bound on a \emph{pointwise rational} second derivative alone does
not imply it.

\subsection{Polynomials need only the binomial identity}
For $P(x)=\sum_{n=0}^d a_nx^n$ on $[-R,R]$, with rational $R\ge1$,
the centered binomial identity bounds
\[
 |P(x+h)-2P(x)+P(x-h)|\le 2K h^2,
 \qquad K=\sum_{n=2}^d |a_n|2^{n-1}R^{n-2},
\]
when $x\pm h$ remain in the interval.  Indeed only even powers of $h$
occur, and their remaining powers are bounded by $R^{n-2}$.
Thus $P+Kx^2$ is convex, its derivative gap is at most $4K|h|$, and the
centered quotient identifies its derivative by the finite binomial formula.
The computed derivative of $P$ is therefore $\sum_{n=1}^d n a_nx^{n-1}$.
Applying the convex-difference FTC to $x^{n+1}/(n+1)$ gives
\[
 \int_a^b x^n\,dx=\frac{b^{n+1}-a^{n+1}}{n+1}.
\]
One uses a slightly larger interval for endpoint derivative bounds.  This
establishes the polynomial integration used in the later remainder proofs.

\section{The geometric sine passes the test}
For $U(x)=S(x)+8x^2$, the addition formulas give
\[
 U(x+h)-2U(x)+U(x-h)
 =16h^2-2S(x)(1-C(h)).
\]
The half-angle and chord bounds give $0\le1-C(h)\le8h^2$ for all
rational $h$.  Hence
\[
 0\le U(x+h)-2U(x)+U(x-h)\le32h^2.
\]
Midpoint convexity proves convexity; the upper bound makes its derivative
gap at most $32|h|$.  Thus $DU$ is a valid computation everywhere and
$DS=DU-16x$ is the actual derivative.

The center of its ideal bracket, after subtracting $16x$, is
\[
 \frac{S(x+h)-S(x-h)}{2h}=C(x)\frac{S(h)}h.
\]
The small-angle estimate from Chapter~\ref{ch:rational-circle-trigonometry}
identifies its value as $\pi C(x)$.  Applying the identical argument to
$C(x)+8x^2$ gives
\[
 DS=\pi C,\qquad DC=-\pi S.
\]
Therefore
\[
 \int_a^b S(x)\,dx=\frac{C(a)-C(b)}\pi,\qquad
 \int_a^b C(x)\,dx=\frac{S(b)-S(a)}\pi.
\]
In particular the independent sine-area computations give $1/\pi$ on
$[0,1/2]$.  Differentiation, its termination proof, the identification of
the derivative, and the integration theorem are four distinct arguments.

\section{Products and integration by parts}
For functions represented as differences of convex computations, with
valid derivatives on the interval, let $V_f$ be the sum of the endpoint
variations of the two monotone derivatives.  The finite FTC estimate gives
\[
 \sum_i|f(x_{i+1})-f(x_i)-Df(x_i)\Delta x_i|
       \le\mesh(P)V_f.
\]
These functions also have rational Lipschitz and absolute bounds $L_f,B_f$.
For two such functions, use the exact identity
\[
 \Delta(fg)=f(x_i)\Delta g+g(x_i)\Delta f+\Delta f\,\Delta g.
\]
Summing, the error in replacing the increments by left derivative samples
is at most
\[
 \mesh(P)\bigl(B_fV_g+B_gV_f+L_fL_g(b-a)\bigr).
\]
The product integrands are integrable by their finite cell ranges.  Hence
\[
 \boxed{\int_a^b f\,Dg=f(b)g(b)-f(a)g(a)-\int_a^b g\,Df.}
\]
The ordinary pointwise product rule follows from the same finite identity.
This proof does not use a mean-value point or assume the product is already
equipped with its own convex decomposition.

\section{Substitution and further integral identities}
Suppose $\alpha$ is increasing, is a difference of convex computations
with valid derivatives, and $g$ is interval-continuous on its image.
The Stieltjes construction of Chapter~\ref{ch:integrals} has sums
$\sum_i g(\alpha(x_i))\Delta\alpha_i$.  Replacing increments by
$D\alpha(x_i)\Delta x_i$ changes the sum by at most
$B_g\mesh(P)V_\alpha$.  Therefore
\[
 \int_{\alpha(a)}^{\alpha(b)}g(u)\,du
   =\int_a^b g(\alpha(t))D\alpha(t)\,dt.
\]
Reversing orientation and splitting finitely many monotone pieces supplies
the other versions used below.  Each substitution must bring its domain,
endpoint, and increment estimates; a merely formal chain rule is not a
substitute for them.

For the angle chart, accumulation gives
$DA(t)=1/(1+t^2)$; its decreasing derivative makes $A$ concave.  Product
and substitution identities now apply to this same geometric computation.
For example
\[
 \int_0^1\frac{t\,dt}{1+t^2}
       =\frac12\int_1^2\frac{du}{u}.
\]
This motivates the logarithm construction, rather than presupposing it.
