\chapter{Exponential and Logarithm}
\label{ch:exponential-logarithm}

This chapter develops exponential and logarithm after the integral and series
chapters.  The intended order is important: the logarithmic-integral
representation of exponential depends on Chapter~\ref{ch:integrals}, while the
power-series representation depends on Chapter~\ref{ch:infinite-series}.
The guiding sanity check is the same as for \(\pi\): independent computations
of the same constant or function should be proved equivalent as raw reals.

\section{Positive-base powers and the Euler base}

\begin{definition}[Positive base and repeated multiplication]
\label{def:positive-base-natural-powers}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.exp.PositiveRealRaw.natPow}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.exp.PositiveRealRaw.natPow_valid}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.exp.PositiveRealRaw.natPowPositive}
\uses{def:raw-real}
A positive computable real is represented by a valid raw real together with
one positive rational lower bound that holds for every interval stage.  This
is the constructive positivity condition needed to make powers meaningful;
it does not appeal to an unrepresented completed real line.

For any such base \(b\), natural powers are defined by repeated
multiplication:
\[
 b^0=1,\qquad b^{n+1}=b^n b.
\]
The raw interval multiplication is the same finite rational interval
arithmetic used throughout the foundation.  Thus this definition works for
an arbitrary positive computable real base, not merely for a rational base.
Lean now proves the resulting raw evaluator valid at every natural exponent.
More precisely, if \(\ell>0\) is the supplied lower bound and \(U\) is the
upper endpoint of the initial interval, then every stage of \(b^n\) lies in
\[
  [\ell^n,U^n].
\]
Thus repeated multiplication returns another positive computable real, via
\code{PositiveRealRaw.natPowPositive}, rather than merely a syntactic raw
algorithm.  This is the checked natural-exponent base of the later rational
root construction; it does not pretend that arbitrary roots have already
been constructed.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Rational powers]
\label{def:rational-powers}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.exp.RationalPowerExtension}
\uses{def:positive-base-natural-powers}
A rational-power extension for \(b\) supplies a valid positive raw real
\(b^q\) for every \(q\in\mathbb Q\), with a certified positive rational
lower bound at every interval stage; it agrees with repeated multiplication
at natural exponents and satisfies
\[
 b^{p+q}=b^p b^q.
\]
For \(p\in\mathbb N\) and \(q>0\), its denominator law is the certified
root equation
\[
 \left(b^{p/q}\right)^q=b^p.
\]
Negative rational exponents are then governed by the additive law.  This is
an interface for a computation and its proof obligations: constructing the
positive root algorithms and proving their validity remains explicit work,
rather than being hidden in a completeness axiom.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Continuity and extension from rational names]
\label{rem:rational-power-continuity-extension}
The Lean evaluator is presented on rational names, but this is not meant to
restrict the mathematical domain of an exponential or power function.  The
separate predicate
\code{EpsilonDeltaContinuousOn} is the literal rational
\(\varepsilon\)-\(\delta\) obligation: for every positive rational
\(\varepsilon\), it supplies a positive rational \(\delta\) and an evaluation
precision such that rational inputs within \(\delta\) have output boxes within
\(\varepsilon\) of each other, each of width at most \(\varepsilon\).  The
boxes need not overlap: nearby inputs of a nonconstant function can have
distinct exact rational output values.  A rational-power exponential must
carry this certificate on every rational interval.

This is the data from which a later completion theorem may extend a continuous
function from rational names to all computable real inputs, or, for the
complex theory, to a specified open computable domain.  Discontinuous partial
functions are not entitled to such an extension.  The extension construction
itself remains later work; no topology or completeness theorem is used in the
finite certificate stated here.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Euler base from an exponential]
\label{def:e-from-exponential-at-one}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.exp.ExponentialFunction.eAtOne}
\uses{def:rational-powers}
An exponential representation is a computational presentation at rational
names that agrees with one rational-power extension and carries its
continuity-in-exponent certificate.  Its Euler base is, by definition, its
value at one:
\[
 e_E=E(1).
\]
This single definition applies to every eventual construction of the
exponential function.  The power-series, compound-interest, and inverse-log
constructions below are therefore intended to be compared as representatives
of the same \(e_E\), not as unrelated constants.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Derivative characterizations of the Euler base]
\label{thm:e-self-derivative-and-unit-slope}
\uses{def:rational-powers,def:e-from-exponential-at-one}
There are two complementary characterizations to prove constructively:
\[
 \frac{d}{dx}e^x=e^x,
 \qquad
 \left.\frac{d}{dx}b^x\right|_{x=0}=1
 \Longleftrightarrow b=e.
\]
The first statement is represented by an interval-valued derivative
certificate on each rational interval; the second uses rational difference
quotients at zero.  Lean already proves the formal coefficient identity
\(\exp' = \exp\).  The remaining analytic theorem is to turn the certified
power-series tail bounds into the corresponding derivative certificate, whose
finite-difference and derivative boxes are within the requested tolerance
rather than required to overlap exactly, and then establish the displayed
uniqueness statement by finite rational estimates.  Its evaluator schedule is
allowed to depend on the rational basepoint and nonzero rational step as well
as on the requested output precision: otherwise a fixed interval width would
be magnified by division by an arbitrarily small step.  More generally, this is
the scalar case of the linear Picard--Lindel\"of construction target in
\cref{thm:linear-picard-lindelof}: the Peano--Baker series for the
one-dimensional coefficient \(A=1\) provides the solution and its factorial
tail, while the zero-initial homogeneous case proves uniqueness.  Lean now
checks that exact zero-initial statement for every finite sampled recurrence.
The continuous lift must still pass its bounded difference through the
factorial tail schedule described in \cref{thm:linear-picard-lindelof}.
Thus neither the existence construction nor the uniqueness argument needs
completeness of an ambient real-number type.  A general nonlinear
Picard--Lindel\"of theorem is a later extension requiring interval-certified
Lipschitz maps and a separate contraction argument.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Initial value of the two finite exponential evaluators]
\label{thm:exponential-initial-value}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.ExpProofs.expPowerSeries_zero_compute_eq}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.ExpProofs.expPowerSeries_zero_valid}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.ExpProofs.expPowerSeries_zero_equiv_one}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.ExpProofs.eulerCenter_zero}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.ExpProofs.expEuler_zero_equiv_one}
\uses{def:positive-base-natural-powers}
\leanok
The two explicit rational algorithms already have the required initial
condition.  For the power-series evaluator, every finite loop has processed
the constant term and all later terms are literally zero at input \(0\).
Thus its interval is exactly the rational point interval
\[
  \exp_{\mathrm{series}}(0)=[1,1]
\]
at every stage.  The repeated-multiplication evaluator has center
\((1+0/m)^m=1\); its explicitly displayed radius remains, but its box
overlaps the same point interval at every stage.  Lean records both facts as
raw-real equivalences to \(1\), using only finite rational arithmetic.

This is the initial-value part of the eventual theorem
\(\exp'= \exp,\ \exp(0)=1\).  It is deliberately not presented as a
derivative proof or as an identification of the series and Euler
evaluators away from zero.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Finite binomial bridge for Euler products]
\label{thm:finite-euler-binomial-bridge}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.ExpProofs.fallingFactorialRat}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.ExpProofs.eulerBinomialTerm}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.ExpProofs.eulerBinomialPrefix}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.ExpProofs.euler_binomial_prefix_nat_expansion}
\uses{def:positive-base-natural-powers}
\leanok
For a rational \(x\) and natural \(m\), Lean now proves the finite identity
\[
 (1+x)^m=
 \sum_{k=0}^{m}
   \frac{m(m-1)\cdots(m-k+1)}{k!}\,x^k.
\]
The sum is implemented as an explicitly recursive rational prefix, not
imported as an analytic binomial theorem.  In particular, substituting
\(x=1/m\) expresses the literal Euler product
\(\left(1+1/m\right)^m\) in the same factorial coordinates used by the
power-series evaluator.

Lean now also checks the finite coefficient-loss estimate
\[
  0\le\frac1{k!}-\frac{m(m-1)\cdots(m-k+1)}{m^k k!}
  \le\frac{k(k-1)}{2m\,k!},
\]
and sums it with the certified factorial tail.  At the square Euler mesh
\(m=(n+1)^2\), its total \(3/m\) error fits inside the direct Euler radius
\(8/(n+1)\).  This completes the raw-real equivalence
\(e_{\mathrm{series}}\Equiv e_{\mathrm{compound}}\) without a limit theorem
or completeness of a real-number type.
\end{theorem}

\begin{definition}[Power-series \texorpdfstring{$e$}{e}]
\label{def:e-power-series}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.ePowerSeries}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.ExpProofs.expPowerSeries_center_step_movement}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.ExpProofs.expPowerSeries_widths_shrink}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.ExpProofs.expPowerSeriesRate}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.ExpProofs.expPowerSeries_valid}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.ExpProofs.ePowerSeries_equiv_eCompoundInterest}
\uses{def:arctan-series}
The power-series evaluator is now certified at \emph{every rational input}.
For \(q\in\mathbb Q\), its stage \(n\) chooses the entirely computable prefix
length
\[
  N_n=n+8+2\,|\operatorname{num}(q)|
\]
and encloses the finite sum through degree \(N_n-1\) by the usual rational
geometric-tail radius.  Lean proves the resulting boxes nested and valid, not
merely ordered.  Their public rate certificate is
\[
  \operatorname{width}(\exp_{\mathrm{series}}(q)_n)
    \le4|t_{N_0}|\left(\frac12\right)^n,
  \qquad t_N=\frac{q^N}{N!},
\]
where \(t_{N_0}\) is itself obtained by the finite rational loop.  The proof
uses the fact that every later term ratio is at most \(1/2\); it invokes no
completed real line or completeness principle.

The same finite evaluator is now exposed as a total rational-input function
with a checked rational-interval restriction.  This makes the valid boxes
available to a derivative or ODE certificate without conflating this
representation step with the still-open analytic theorem
\(\exp'=\exp\).
On every selected interval containing (0), Lean also transports the exact
series value (1) to the function-level evaluator.  Thus its future ODE
certificate already has the required domain, validity, and initial-value
data; only the self-derivative field remains to be proved.

The first constant definition is the specialization at \(1\):
\[
  e = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k!}.
\]
The Lean raw representative is \code{ePowerSeries}.  Lean now identifies it
with the compound-interest representative by a finite rational binomial
coefficient comparison and an explicit factorial-tail budget.  This remains a
constant-level agreement at input \(1\), not a proof of the analytic theorem
\(\exp'=\exp\) for a rational-input function.
\end{definition}

The second definition uses the classical limit
\[
  e = \lim_{m\to\infty}\left(1+\frac1m\right)^m.

\[
  a_m=\left(1+\frac1m\right)^m,

The finite interval is
\[
  [a_m,b_m].
\]

\[
  b_m-a_m=\frac{a_m}{m}\ge0.
\]
\[
  a_m\le a_{m+1}\le b_{m+1}\le b_m,\qquad
  b_m\le4,\qquad a_m\le4.

exponential. It does \emph{not} construct positive rational roots or claim
continuity in the exponent; those are still the explicit obligations of
\cref{def:rational-powers,rem:rational-power-continuity-extension}.

\begin{definition}[Logarithm]
\label{def:logarithm-integral}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.exp.logFromIntegral}
\uses{def:e-log-integral,def:real-fun-raw}
The logarithm is first the positive-branch integral
\[
  \log x = \int_1^x \frac{dt}{t}.
\]
On any chosen positive branch this is a \code{FunctionOnInterval}, hence an
instance of \cref{def:real-fun-raw}: for rational \(x\) in the branch interval
and stage \(n\), the integral construction returns a rational interval, and for
fixed \(x\) these intervals form a raw real.  Its inverse branch is
exponential.  Later algebraic identities such as \(\log(xy)=\log x+\log y\)
and \(\exp(x+y)=\exp(x)\exp(y)\) should be proved from uniqueness of the
inverse and then transported between representations.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[A deliberately long ODE--exponential--logarithm--pi route]
\label{rem:ode-exp-log-pi-route}
\uses{sec:ode-construction-identification-route,thm:linear-ode-elementary-function-agreement}
The project should make one pi equivalence exercise the elementary-function
and ODE layers rather than obtain every pi row from arctangent alone.
\Cref{thm:linear-ode-elementary-function-agreement} gives the common
identification principle: Peano--Baker first constructs a solution, and its
factorial-tail uniqueness certificate then makes every representation with the
same derivative and initial data agree with it.  The natural pi specialization
is the integration-by-parts identity
\[
  \pi
   =4\int_0^1\arctan(x)\,\dd x+2\log 2.
\]
Its intended proof order is:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item apply that theorem to \(f'=f,\ f(0)=1\).  Once the power-series,
  Euler-limit, and inverse-logarithmic-integral exponentials all satisfy this
  problem, uniqueness proves that the three exponential representations
  agree;
  \item use the inverse-function and FTC certificates to identify the
  positive logarithmic integral with the inverse of that canonical
  exponential.  The concrete finite comparison at two is already checked by
  \cref{thm:log-two-series-reciprocal-integral}; transport that equality to
  the resulting canonical \(\log 2\);
  \item prove the arctangent derivative and instantiate the common
  increasing/decreasing-piece integration-by-parts certificate of
  \cref{thm:integration-by-parts-certificate}.  The rational
  identity
  \[
    \int_0^1\arctan(x)\,\dd x
      =\frac{\pi}{4}-\frac12\log 2
  \]
  follows from \((x\arctan x)'=\arctan x+x/(1+x^2)\), its finite
  corner-rectangle schedule, and the logarithmic endpoint calculation.  The
  latter is now the checked literal strip theorem
  \cref{thm:arctan-log-first-strip}, built from the finite square-substitution
  core in \cref{prop:log-two-square-substitution};
  \item package the right-hand side as a valid raw pi evaluator and prove it
  equivalent to the geometric circle-area presentation.
\end{enumerate}
Thus this one final equality deliberately depends on exp/log agreement and
therefore on linear ODE uniqueness.  It is not currently a scoreboard cell:
every arrow above must be a checked finite certificate first.  A later,
strictly longer complex corroboration is
\(\pi=-2i\log(i)\); it additionally needs extension from rational names to
represented complex inputs and the agreement of the circular and exponential
solutions of the \(2\)-dimensional rotation system.

The formal interface now records this dependency directly: the exp/log
comparison theorem has a Volterra specialization which applies the
finite-tail uniqueness provider to the power-series and inverse-logarithm
candidate certificates.  Consequently no ambient ODE uniqueness axiom is
silently used in this route.  What remains is concrete and local: construct
the provider from certified integral boxes, then supply the derivative and
initial-value certificates for each proposed exponential.
\end{remark}
