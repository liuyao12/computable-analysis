\chapter{Computable Numbers and Functions}\label{ch:foundations}

A number will be a computation, not an already available point of a
completed line.  Each stage returns two rational endpoints; a proof explains
why these pairs are compatible and their widths become arbitrarily small.
There is no additional scalar hidden between the endpoints.  Different
computations remain worth keeping: a geometric construction can be
compared with a faster series by rational overlap inequalities.

The finite arithmetic used below is ordinary integer and rational arithmetic.
Fractions are reduced by the Euclidean algorithm; finite sums, products,
polynomials, induction, and the triangle inequality require no limiting
objects.  A rational input is a numerator and a positive denominator, not an
oracle for a point on a continuum.

\section{Interval arithmetic and real numbers}

\begin{definition}[Rational interval]\label{def:qinterval}
A \emph{rational interval} is a pair
\[
 [a,b]\in\Q\times\Q,\qquad a\le b.
\]
It is a finite object, not the set of rational points between its endpoints.
Its width is $b-a$.  Two intervals $[a,b]$ and $[c,d]$ overlap when
$a\le d$ and $c\le b$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[A computed number]\label{def:raw-real}
A number computation is a terminating algorithm
\[
 n\longmapsto X_n=[a_n,b_n]
\]
whose intervals satisfy
\[
 n\le m\ \Longrightarrow\ a_n\le a_m\le b_m\le b_n,
\]
and whose widths become smaller than every positive rational tolerance.
We also call such an algorithm a raw real.  No infinitely stored table of
rational endpoints is allowed, and no operation extracts a scalar from
the algorithm.
\end{definition}

A request for precision $\varepsilon>0$ is answered by computing successive
stages until $b_n-a_n<\varepsilon$.  A proof that the widths shrink proves
that this search terminates.  An explicit rate is often useful, but need not
be part of the algorithm's input.

\begin{definition}[Equivalence]\label{def:raw-equiv}
Two valid computations are equivalent when their intervals overlap at every
stage.  We write $X\sim Y$.  An equality sign between named computations
elsewhere in the text denotes this proved compatibility, not equality of
scalar values or equality of the algorithms.
\end{definition}

Same-stage overlap implies overlap at any two stages, by passing to their
maximum.  Equivalence is transitive: if two outer computations had disjoint
intervals separated by a positive rational gap, a sufficiently narrow interval
of a middle computation could not overlap both.  This proves transitivity
using only finitely many rational comparisons.

Equivalence is a theorem about computations, not a proposed decision algorithm.
Disjoint finite outputs certify inequivalence.  Positivity of a computation
means that some output has positive lower endpoint.  Such a witness is
called a separation from zero; it is a finite rational comparison.

\begin{definition}[Interval arithmetic]\label{def:raw-real-arithmetic}
Addition and negation are computed by
\[
 [a,b]+[c,d]=[a+c,b+d],\qquad -[a,b]=[-b,-a].
\]
For multiplication take the least rational interval containing
$ac,ad,bc,bd$.  If an interval $[a,b]$ avoids zero, its reciprocal enclosure
is $[1/b,1/a]$.
\end{definition}

These operations preserve inclusion.  If the input pairs have absolute
endpoint bounds $B_X,B_Y$, the product width is at most
\[
 B_X\operatorname{width}(Y_n)+B_Y\operatorname{width}(X_n).
\]
The bounds can be read from any initial outputs.  If later pairs stay at
rational distance at least $c>0$ from zero, reciprocal widths are at most
$c^{-2}\operatorname{width}(X_n)$.  These estimates prove validity
and compatibility with equivalence.  In particular, division is available
once a denominator output avoids zero, not by deciding the equality of an
unspecified scalar with zero.

Here are the literal endpoint predicates behind the abbreviated order
notation.  For $X_n=[X_n^-,X_n^+]$ and $Y_m=[Y_m^-,Y_m^+]$,
\[
 X\le Y\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 X_n^-\le Y_m^+\quad\text{for all }n,m.
\]
A displayed bound $|X-Y|\le r$, for rational $r\ge0$, means
\[
 X_n^-\le Y_m^++r,\qquad Y_m^-\le X_n^++r
 \quad\text{for all }n,m.
\]
These are predicates on algorithms, not comparisons of extracted numbers.
A fixed pair $[a,b]$ bounds a computation when it overlaps every output.
This does \emph{not} mean every finite output is contained in $[a,b]$.
One can obtain containment in $[a-\eta,b+\eta]$ by requesting sufficiently
small output width, for any positive rational $\eta$.

\begin{proposition}[Finite-prefix stabilization]\label{prop:raw-prefix-stabilization}
Suppose rational numbers $q_n$ and positive rational errors $e_n\to0$
are computed, and
\[
 |q_m-q_n|\le e_n\qquad(m\ge n).
\]
Then
\[
 X_n=\bigcap_{k=0}^n[q_k-e_k,q_k+e_k]
\]
is a valid number computation.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The finite intersection contains $q_n$.  It is nested, and its width is at
most $2e_n$.  All its endpoints are finite maxima and minima of rationals.
\end{proof}

More generally, a sequence of pairwise overlapping rational intervals with
shrinking widths can be stabilized in this way: pairwise overlap of a
\emph{finite} family of intervals implies a nonempty common intersection.
We shall repeatedly prove compatibility by common refinements or by bounds
on every finite future correction.  An independently existing limit is
never needed as an anchor.

\begin{definition}[Stage schedules]\label{def:stage-schedule}
A stage schedule is an increasing, unbounded map $\sigma:\N\to\N$.
Replacing $X_n$ by $X_{\sigma(n)}$ gives an equivalent computation.
\end{definition}

Thus two algorithms can be compared at different speeds.  A useful named
number can carry several computations connected by proved equivalences;
transitivity supplies comparisons along a finite chain.  There is no reason
to discard a slow geometric construction after discovering a faster series.

\section{Examples of real numbers}

\subsection{Square roots and finite geometry}
\label{rem:algebraic-numbers-sqrt-two}
For a rational $q\ge0$, start with rational $0\le a\le b$ satisfying
$a^2\le q\le b^2$.  Bisect and retain the half whose squared endpoints
still bracket $q$.  If the midpoint square equals $q$, return the exact
constant interval thereafter.  At stage $n$ the width is at most
$(b-a)2^{-n}$, and finite interval multiplication proves that the resulting
squared-output computation overlaps $[q,q]$ at every stage.

For $\sqrt2$, the rational secant--tangent refinement gives another
construction:
\begin{verbatim}
def sqrt2(n):
    a = 1
    b = 2
    for _ in range(n):
        a = (2 + a*b) / (a + b)
        b = (b + 2/b) / 2
    return [a, b]
\end{verbatim}
All divisions here are exact rational divisions.  For one step, writing
$a',b'$ for the new endpoints,
\[
 (a')^2-2=\frac{(a^2-2)(b^2-2)}{(a+b)^2},\qquad
 (b')^2-2=\frac{(b^2-2)^2}{4b^2}.
\]
Also $a\le a'\le b'\le b$, and
\[
 b'-a'=\frac{(b-a)(b^2-2)}{2b(a+b)}\le\frac{b-a}{2}.
\]
These identities prove validity.  The bisection and secant--tangent
outputs overlap across all stages: disjoint nonnegative intervals cannot
both have their squared endpoints bracket $2$.  Monotonicity of rational
squaring proves this directly.  There is no need to select a common root
before proving the computations equivalent.

\end{figure}

There is no rational whose square is $2$: in a reduced fraction $p/q$, the
identity $p^2=2q^2$ would make both $p$ and $q$ even.  Thus no constant
rational-pair computation $[r,r]$ is equivalent to this construction.
This is the sense in which the construction is nonrational; its two
outputs at every finite stage are rational.

Square roots also compute lengths of rational vectors.  The finite identity
\[
 (a^2+b^2)(c^2+d^2)-(ac+bd)^2=(ad-bc)^2
\]
proves the coordinate Cauchy--Schwarz inequality and hence the broken-line
inequality.  Shoelace determinants compute polygonal areas exactly.  These
are the geometric tools needed for the circle, rather than a separate
axiomatization of Euclidean space.

\subsection{Other computations of numbers}
\label{rem:sources-of-raw-reals}
The geometric sum illustrates a finite identity with a tail:
\[
 (1-r)\sum_{k=0}^{N}r^k=1-r^{N+1}.
\]
For rational $|r|<1$, the error $|r|^{N+1}/(1-|r|)$ gives a number
computation.  Bounded monotonicity by itself does not supply a shrinking
upper--lower gap; an error estimate or a terminating precision search is
still necessary.

Later we shall construct, and prove equivalent, the computations suggested
by
\[
 \frac\pi4=1-\frac13+\frac15-\cdots
   =\int_0^1\frac{dt}{1+t^2},\qquad
 \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}{6}.
\]
At this point these are destinations, not definitions silently imported from
ordinary calculus.  The circle will define $\pi$ geometrically; rectangles,
series, and Fourier sums will then supply different computations of it.

\begin{remark}[Calculus as equivalence]\label{rem:calculus-as-equivalence}
The FTC compares an integral algorithm $J$ with an endpoint-difference
algorithm $E$.  For a rational-input evaluator $f$, the latter returns
\[
 E_m=[f_m^-(b)-f_m^+(a),\ f_m^+(b)-f_m^-(a)].
\]
The theorem concludes $J_n^-\le E_m^+$ and $E_m^-\le J_n^+$ for all
$n,m$, with validity of both algorithms.  This is the endpoint meaning
of the familiar fundamental formula.  Its hypotheses must explain why
local derivative information controls a whole rational interval.
\end{remark}

\section{Complex numbers}

Let $\Q(i)=\{r+is:r,s\in\Q\}$.  A rational complex box is a pair of
rational coordinate intervals.  A complex computation gives nested boxes
whose two widths shrink to zero; equivalence is coordinatewise overlap.
Addition and multiplication are rational box operations, using
\[
 (r+is)(u+iv)=(ru-sv)+i(rv+su).
\]
For a denominator separated from zero use
$z^{-1}=\overline z/( (\Re z)^2+(\Im z)^2)$ and the real reciprocal
construction.  Here the formula specifies operations on box algorithms,
not evaluations at an uncomputed complex scalar.  Finite rational
coordinate bounds control all errors.

The same convention applies to vectors and matrices: finitely many number
computations, with finite sums and products.  No additional completeness
principle appears when the number of coordinates increases.

\section{Algorithms with a variable}
\label{rem:parameterized-constructions}

\begin{definition}[Function computation]\label{def:real-fun-raw}
A function computation has a declared domain of rational inputs and a
terminating algorithm
\[
 (x,n)\longmapsto f_n(x)=[f_n^-(x),f_n^+(x)]\in\Q\times\Q,
\]
with ordered, nested, arbitrarily narrow outputs for each permitted $x$.
\end{definition}

\textbf{Both outputs are rational.}  Fixing $x$ leaves the program
$n\mapsto f_n(x)$; it does not produce an additional scalar $f(x)$.
In particular the trigonometric evaluator will return $S_n(x)$, a rational
pair, not a nonrational point value.  Familiar unindexed function formulas
later in the text are mnemonics for relations between these evaluators.
A foundational statement must give the stage-indexed endpoint relation;
suppressing the stage index cannot supply an object for a proof.
An algebraic, dyadic, or rational geometric chart may be a more natural
first domain than all rationals; passing between charts requires an
explicit comparison of outputs at every pair of stages.

\begin{definition}[Interval continuity]\label{def:interval-regular}
On a rational interval $[a,b]$, a continuity certificate supplies, for every
positive rational $\varepsilon$, a positive rational $\mu(\varepsilon)$
such that $|x-y|\le\mu(\varepsilon)$ implies
\[
 f_n^-(x)\le f_m^+(y)+\varepsilon,\qquad
 f_m^-(y)\le f_n^+(x)+\varepsilon
 \quad\text{for every }n,m.
\]
Here $x,y$ are rational inputs in $[a,b]$.  A different interval may have
a different certificate.  This definition contains no scalar evaluations.
\end{definition}

A narrow output at a rational sample, enlarged by the continuity tolerance,
then overlaps every output at every input in a sufficiently small cell.
These rational cell ranges are distinguished from containing every coarse
output pair.  Refining the sample output and reducing the continuity
tolerance produces arbitrarily narrow ranges.  Conversely such certified
cell ranges supply continuity by removing rational width errors.  A finite
grid and coarse output pairs also give an absolute endpoint bound in the
sense of the displayed order predicates.

This is stronger than continuity at each rational point.  We do not infer it
from compactness.  For square roots, factoring differences of rational
squares proves the endpoint relation abbreviated by
\[
 |\sqrt{x}-\sqrt{y}|\le\sqrt{|x-y|}\qquad(x,y\ge0),
\]
so $\mu(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon^2$ works.  For a polynomial, expand
$y^k-x^k=(y-x)\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}y^{k-1-j}x^j$ and bound the finite sum on
the interval.  Separated rational functions follow by the reciprocal bound.

\begin{proposition}[Computed inputs]\label{prop:computed-inputs}
An interval-continuous rational-input evaluator and an input-pair program
certified in its domain determine a new output-pair program.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Intersect an input output with $[a,b]$ and choose a rational sample in that
finite interval.  Request enough input precision for the continuity
tolerance, then evaluate the sample to the remaining output-width budget.
Enlarge by the continuity tolerance.  Nesting of the input pairs and the
endpoint continuity inequalities prove pairwise compatibility of the
resulting outputs.  Finite-prefix intersections make them nested, and
the budgets prove shrinking widths.  Equivalent input programs give
cross-stage overlap of the output programs by the same rational estimates.
\end{proof}

This constructs an operation on algorithms, not evaluation at a scalar
input obtained by completing the rational line.

\section{Inverting a monotone computation}
\label{thm:constructive-inverse-function}
Suppose $f$ is increasing and interval-continuous on $[a,b]$, with a
positive separation bound $\sigma(d)$: for rational $y-x\ge d>0$,
\[
 f_n^+(y)-f_m^-(x)\ge\sigma(d)\quad\text{for all }n,m.
\]
A target program $Z_n=[Z_n^-,Z_n^+]$ is in the endpoint range when
\[
 f_n^-(a)\le Z_m^+,\qquad Z_m^-\le f_n^+(b)\quad\text{for all }n,m.
\]
Use the two trisection points $u,v$ of the current rational input bracket.
Refine until either $Z_n^->f_n^+(u)$ or $Z_n^+<f_n^-(v)$ is certified.
The separation bound and shrinking widths prove that at least one of
these finite tests succeeds.  This does not require deciding equality
between the target program and either endpoint program.  Keep $[u,b]$
in the first case and $[a,v]$ in the second.

Each step preserves the endpoint-range inequalities and shrinks the input
pair by a factor $2/3$.  The computed-input proposition constructs the
corresponding output program; continuity proves that it overlaps the
target at every pair of stages.  Separation proves uniqueness up to
compatibility.  Squaring on $[0,1]$ has $\sigma(d)=d^2$; the later
angle chart has a linear separation bound.

\section{Domains and equivalent descriptions}
Functions agree only where both computations are defined.  A geometric
chart, a series disk, and a positive algebraic branch may describe the same
function on overlapping domains without being interchangeable everywhere.
Finite chains of proved comparisons retain these domain restrictions.

The rest of the book uses only the constructions just described: finite
rational operations, endpoint pairs, terminating refinement, and
cross-stage comparisons.  When a new infinite process occurs, its finite
future errors must be bounded explicitly.  Removing a stage index in a
familiar formula does not create a scalar on which a proof may rely.
