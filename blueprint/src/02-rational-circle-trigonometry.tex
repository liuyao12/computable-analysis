\section{Archimedes' Method of Exhaustion}

Archimedes' method of exhaustion computes the area of the unit disk by
trapping it between an inscribed polygon and a circumscribed polygon, then
refining the polygons until the two areas are forced close together.  The
classical presentation starts with a hexagon and repeatedly doubles the number
of sides, producing regular \(6\cdot2^n\)-gons.  That version is geometrically
natural, but its vertices generally have coordinates involving square roots
and other non-rational quantities.  It is therefore not the most transparent
first algorithm for a blueprint whose basic data are rational intervals.

We instead use rational parametrizations of the circle.  The stereographic
parameter \(u\in\Q\) gives a rational point \(P(u)\) on the unit circle, and
bisection of rational parameter intervals keeps all data rational.  This is
the normalized version of the classical parametrization of Pythagorean triples:
Euclid already knew that triples of the form
\[
  (r^2-s^2,\,2rs,\,r^2+s^2)
\]
satisfy the Pythagorean equation, and dividing by \(r^2+s^2\) gives a rational
point on the unit circle.  Polygonal area is then especially convenient:
determinants of rational coordinates are rational, so no square-root
subroutine is needed.  Thus the first computation of \(\pi\) in this
development is an area computation, not a circumference computation.

\begin{definition}[Circle-area exhaustion algorithm]\label{def:circle-area-stage-algorithm}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.piCircleArea}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.circleAreaIncrement}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.circleAreaDecrement}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.refineAreaBounds}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.PiProofs.AreaLoopValidity.areaValid}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.PiProofs.four_arctanGeom_one_compute_eq_piCircleArea_compute}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.PiProofs.fourArctanGeomOneValid}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.PiProofs.four_arctanGeom_one_equiv_piCircleArea}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.PiProofs.piCircleArea_equiv_four_arctanGeom_one}
\uses{rem:pseudocode-convention}
The loop form of the stage algorithm keeps a current lower bound \(a\), a
current upper bound \(b\), and the list of rational parameter intervals still
being refined.  On one interval \([p,r]\), let \(q=(p+r)/2\).  The lower-bound
increment is the area of the new chord triangle, and the upper-bound decrement
is the tangent triangle removed from the old outer cap:
\[
  \Delta(p,q,r)=
    \frac{2(r-p)(q-p)(r-q)}
      {(1+p^2)(1+q^2)(1+r^2)},
\]
\[
  \nabla(p,q,r)=
    \frac{(r-p)(q-p)(r-q)}
      {(1+pr)(1+pq)(1+qr)}.
\]
The case \(p=0,r=1\) is the quarter circle.  The initial quarter-sector bounds
are \(1/2\le\pi/4\le1\), and the final answer is multiplied by \(4\).
\begin{verbatim}
def piCircleArea(n):
    a, b = 1/2, 1
    intervals = [(0, 1)]

    def increment(p, q, r):
        return (2*(r-p)*(q-p)*(r-q) /
                ((1+p*p)*(1+q*q)*(1+r*r)))

    def decrement(p, q, r):
        return ((r-p)*(q-p)*(r-q) /
                ((1+p*r)*(1+p*q)*(1+q*r)))

    for _ in range(n):
        next_intervals = []
        for p, r in intervals:
            q = (p + r) / 2
            a = a + increment(p, q, r)
            b = b - decrement(p, q, r)
            next_intervals.append((p, q))
            next_intervals.append((q, r))
        intervals = next_intervals

    return [4*a, 4*b]
\end{verbatim}
Every operation in this pseudocode is a rational operation.  The returned pair
for \code{piCircleArea} is meant to be the first raw interval algorithm for
\(\pi\): the lower endpoint is the area of the refined inscribed rational
polygon, and the upper endpoint is the area of the corresponding refined outer
tangent polygon.  In Lean this increment/decrement loop is the single public
\code{piCircleArea} definition.
\end{definition}

\section{Overview}

The purpose of this chapter is to construct trigonometric functions as
algorithms from the geometry of the unit circle, and then prove their
identities from that construction.  The Archimedean area algorithm above is
the first such construction: it gives \(\pi\) from rational polygonal areas
before any appeal to analytic trigonometric functions.

The main later algorithmic object is a raw function that sends a normalized
quarter-turn input to a certified box around the corresponding point on the
oriented unit circle:
\[
  t \longmapsto C(t)=(X(t),Y(t)),\qquad t\in\Q\hbox{ represents }t\pi/2.
\]
Cosine and sine are then not separate primary algorithms.  They are the two
coordinate projections:
\[
  \cos(t\pi/2)=X(t),\qquad \sin(t\pi/2)=Y(t).
\]
This organization is intentional.  It keeps the finite geometric proof in one
place, and it makes the trigonometric identities consequences of the circle
law rather than independent analytic facts.

As the construction becomes sharper, the Lean development may need
refactoring.  The blueprint should therefore track stable interfaces rather
than overcommit to the first internal implementation of the bisection search,
period reduction, or quadrant bookkeeping.  Chapter~\ref{ch:rational-circle-trigonometry}
now owns the geometric route to trigonometry: first define \(\pi\) by rational
polygonal area, then observe that the same refinement pattern computes
\(\arctan(x)\), then construct the monotone inverse of arctangent.  For
clarity, the arctangent object is not factored through the \(\pi\) object: it
uses the same increment and decrement formulas, but is packaged as its own raw
function.  Sine and cosine are deliberately not functions of radian inputs in
their first interface.  Their input is a rational number \(t\), interpreted as
``the angle \(t\pi/2\)''.  This makes \(t=1\) the point at ninety degrees and keeps the
external input rational even though the corresponding radian value is generally
not rational.

The stable interface is:
\[
  \hbox{rational area algorithms for }\pi\hbox{ and }\arctan
  \quad\leadsto\quad
  \hbox{monotone inverse arctangent on normalized quarter-turns}
  \quad\leadsto\quad
  \hbox{sine and cosine raw functions}
  \quad\leadsto\quad
  \hbox{identity package}.
\]
The sanity check for later chapters is always equivalence of \(\pi\)
representatives.  Chapter~\ref{ch:rational-circle-trigonometry} should settle
the geometric area/circumference comparison and the geometric identity
\(\pi=4\arctan(1)\).  Comparisons with the Leibniz and Machin algorithms pass
through the power-series arctangent and belong after the series estimates are
available.


\section{Arctangent, its inverse, and normalized quarter-turn angles}

\begin{definition}[Geometric arctangent]
\label{def:geometric-arctan}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.ArctanGeometry.arctanGeom}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.ArctanGeometry.arctanGeom_one_compute_eq}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.ArctanGeometry.PiAreaCompatibility}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.ArctanGeometry.piAreaCompatibility}
\uses{def:circle-area-stage-algorithm,rem:rational-circle-point}
For a rational slope \(x\), the point \(P(x)\) lies on the unit circle at twice
the angle whose tangent is \(x\).  The geometric arctangent algorithm returns
the sector area between \(P(0)=(1,0)\) and \(P(x)\), with oddness used for
negative slopes.  Thus it is an area algorithm for \(\arctan(x)\), separate
from the power-series algorithm of Chapter~\ref{ch:infinite-series}.  The
special case \(x=1\) is the quarter circle, so the comparison
\(\pi=4\arctan(1)\) is geometric before any series comparison enters.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Geometric arctangent gives the area definition of pi]
\label{thm:geometric-arctan-one-area-pi}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.PiProofs.fourArctanGeomOneValid}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.PiProofs.four_arctanGeom_one_equiv_piCircleArea}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.PiProofs.piCircleArea_equiv_four_arctanGeom_one}
\uses{def:circle-area-stage-algorithm,def:geometric-arctan}
The geometric sector-area arctangent satisfies
\[
  4\arctan_{\mathrm{geom}}(1)\sim \pi_{\mathrm{area}}.
\]
Lean proves this by transporting the verified \code{piCircleArea} interval
algorithm across the same-stage equality with \code{4 * arctanGeom(1)}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{definition}[Normalized quarter-turns as angles]
\label{def:quarter-turn-angle}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.RationalCircle.GeometricTrig.QuarterTurn}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.RationalCircle.GeometricTrig.quarterTurnRaw}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.RationalCircle.GeometricTrig.unitIntervalBranch}
\uses{def:circle-area-stage-algorithm}
An input angle for the first sine and cosine algorithms is a rational number
\(t\in\Q\), interpreted as \(t\pi/2\).  The raw real represented by this input is
not the rational number \(t\); it is the product of \(t/2\) with whichever
certified \(\pi\)-algorithm is being used.  Lean records this convention by the
abbreviation \code{QuarterTurn} and by the raw representative
\code{quarterTurnRaw}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Monotone inverse arctangent interface]
\label{def:inverse-arctan-interface}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.RationalCircle.GeometricTrig.ArctanInverseConstruction}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.RationalCircle.GeometricTrig.unitIntervalBranch_zero}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.RationalCircle.GeometricTrig.unitIntervalBranch_one}
\uses{def:geometric-arctan,def:quarter-turn-angle,def:qbox}
The inverse-arctangent step is the bisection/search layer which, for a
normalized rational quarter-turn input \(t\), finds the corresponding
tangent-slope information on the unit branch \(0\le t\le1\) and supplies the
branch-reduction data needed to recover the
point on the oriented unit circle.  The blueprint does not require the first
implementation to be final.  The stable Lean-facing contract asks for:
\begin{enumerate}
\item a raw tangent/inverse-arctangent function on quarter-turn inputs,
\item validity of that raw function on its stated branch,
\item the left and right inverse laws against geometric arctangent, and
\item compatibility with the chosen \(\pi\)-representative.
\end{enumerate}
This is the bridge between the geometric area definition of \(\pi\) and the
coordinate algorithms for sine and cosine.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Sine and cosine from a circle-point algorithm]
\label{def:sin-cos-from-circle-point}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.RationalCircle.GeometricTrig.FunctionRawConstruction}
\uses{def:inverse-arctan-interface}
After inverse arctangent has located the point on the oriented unit circle, the
trigonometric functions are projections:
\[
  \cos(t\pi/2)=X(t),\qquad \sin(t\pi/2)=Y(t).
\]
Lean packages this as a complex-valued point algorithm plus real-coordinate
projection functions \code{cosFunctionRawOfPoint} and
\code{sinFunctionRawOfPoint}.  The first identity theorem for these functions
should be the unit-circle law; all addition, parity, double-angle, reciprocal,
and tangent identities are then consequences of the circle law and point
multiplication.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Sine and cosine are instances of the function definition]
\label{rem:sin-cos-function-instance}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.RationalCircle.GeometricTrig.cosFunctionRawOfPoint}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.RationalCircle.GeometricTrig.sinFunctionRawOfPoint}
\uses{def:real-fun-raw,def:sin-cos-from-circle-point}
For a chosen circle-point algorithm \(C\), the functions \(\sin\) and \(\cos\)
are instances of \cref{def:real-fun-raw}: their rational domain is the set of
normalized quarter-turn inputs where \(C\) is defined, and their stage
algorithms are
\[
  (t,n)\longmapsto \operatorname{re}(C(t,n)),
  \qquad
  (t,n)\longmapsto \operatorname{im}(C(t,n)).
\]
For fixed rational \(t\) in the domain, validity of the point algorithm implies
that each projected sequence in \(n\) is a raw real.
\end{remark}

\section{Rational Circle}

\begin{remark}[Rational circle parametrization]
\label{rem:rational-circle-point}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.RationalCircle.Stage.point}
The rational points used below come from the usual stereographic chart on the
unit circle.  In the pseudocode convention of this blueprint, this is:
\begin{verbatim}
def circle_point(u):
    d = 1 + u*u
    return Point((1 - u*u) / d, (2*u) / d)
\end{verbatim}
In formulas this is the point
\[
  P(u)=
  \left(
    \frac{1-u^2}{1+u^2},
    \frac{2u}{1+u^2}
  \right),
\]
with the missing point of the chart handled separately when needed.  This
parametrization is background for the algorithms, rather than a separate
object of study in the chapter.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Rational circle equation]
\label{rem:rational-circle-equation}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.RationalCircle.Stage.point_normSq}
\uses{rem:rational-circle-point}
For every \(u\in\Q\),
\[
  P(u)_x^2+P(u)_y^2=1.
\]
This is a rational identity after clearing the denominator \(1+u^2\).  Lean
records it as \code{ComputableAnalysis.RationalCircle.Stage.point\_normSq}.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Dyadic subdivision of a rational interval]
\label{rem:dyadic-rational-circle-stages}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.RationalCircle.dyadicStage}
\uses{rem:rational-circle-point,rem:pseudocode-convention}
Start with a rational interval \([x,y]\).  Bisect every current interval
\(n\) times, and then map the resulting rational parameters to the circle by
the rational parametrization \(P\).  Equivalently, the rational parameters
after \(n\) bisections are
\[
  x+\frac{k}{2^n}(y-x),
  \qquad 0\le k\le 2^n.
\]
\begin{verbatim}
def dyadic_subdivision(x, y, n):
    s = [x, y]
    for _ in range(n):
        t = []
        for i in range(len(s) - 1):
            a = s[i]
            b = s[i + 1]
            t.append(a)
            t.append((a + b) / 2)
        t.append(s[-1])
        s = t
    return s

def circle_subdivision(x, y, n):
    return [circle_point(u) for u in dyadic_subdivision(x, y, n)]

def circle_stage(n):
    return circle_subdivision(0, 1, n)
\end{verbatim}
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[What bisection refinement means]
\label{rem:dyadic-rational-circle-refinement}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.RationalCircle.Trigonometry.dyadic_point_refineIndex}
\uses{rem:dyadic-rational-circle-stages}
There is no separate abstract definition of refinement being used here.  The
concrete fact is just that one more bisection keeps every old parameter and
inserts one midpoint between adjacent old parameters.  Thus, after mapping to
the circle, the even-indexed points in the next list are the old points:
\[
  C_{n+1,2k}=C_{n,k}.
\]
The odd indices are the new rational circle points coming from the inserted
midpoints.  Lean records this concrete bookkeeping statement as
\code{ComputableAnalysis.RationalCircle.Trigonometry.dyadic\_point\_refineIndex}.
\end{remark}

\section{Finite Area and Circumference Certificates}

\begin{definition}[Rational triangle area]\label{def:rational-triangle-area}
\uses{rem:pseudocode-convention}
A rational triangle is a triangle whose vertices have rational coordinates.
Its area is rational, because it is computed by the determinant formula:
\begin{verbatim}
def sub(p, q):
    return Point(p.x - q.x, p.y - q.y)

def cross(p, q):
    return p.x*q.y - p.y*q.x

def triangle_area(p, q, r):
    return abs(cross(sub(q, p), sub(r, p)) / 2)
\end{verbatim}
Thus every line in the algorithm is a rational operation, followed only by
the absolute value on \(\Q\).  This determinant calculation is the finite
geometric fact packaged by the incremental area corrections in
\cref{def:circle-area-stage-algorithm}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Circumference stage algorithm]\label{def:circle-circumference-stage-algorithm}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.piCircumference}
\uses{def:circle-area-stage-algorithm}
The circumference algorithm uses the same inner and outer rational boundaries,
but replaces polygonal area by path length.  The square-root subroutine is
therefore part of the proof interface: its stage choices must leave enough
room for the finite chord and tangent inequalities to close.  In this
definition, the function named \code{pi} is the circumference algorithm for
pi.
\begin{verbatim}
def pi(n):
    inner, outer = circle_boundaries(n)
    inner_len = path_length_interval(inner, 2**n)
    outer_len = path_length_interval(outer, 2**n)
    return [(4*inner_len.lo)/2, (4*outer_len.hi)/2]
\end{verbatim}
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Finite Archimedes comparison]\label{thm:finite-archimedes}
\uses{def:circle-area-stage-algorithm,def:circle-circumference-stage-algorithm,def:raw-equiv}
At each rational sample stage, the interval for pi obtained from unit-disk
area overlaps the interval for pi obtained from half-circumference.  After
validity is established for both algorithms, this gives equivalence of the
two raw reals.
The proof reduces to finite inequalities comparing inscribed chords and
outer tangent segments.  The current Lean proof target is
\code{ComputableAnalysis.PiProofs.archimedesTheorem}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Geometric pi validity]\label{thm:geometric-pi-validity}
\uses{thm:finite-archimedes,def:raw-real}
The area and circumference algorithms for pi are real numbers: their intervals
are ordered, nested under dyadic refinement, and have widths tending to zero.
This is the exhaustion principle in the raw-real setting, and it is the
starting point for the later calculus constructions.  The remaining work is
to finish the Lean proof, not to settle a mathematical uncertainty.
\end{theorem}

\section{Arctangent by the Same Exhaustion}

\begin{definition}[Arctangent area stage algorithm]
\label{def:arctan-area-stage-algorithm}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.ArctanGeometry.arctanGeom}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.ArctanGeometry.arctanAreaIncrement}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.ArctanGeometry.arctanAreaDecrement}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.ArctanGeometry.arctanGeom_nonneg_compute_eq}
\uses{def:circle-area-stage-algorithm,rem:pseudocode-convention}
For \(0\le x\le1\), the same rational polygon computation gives an interval
for the sector area from \(P(0)=(1,0)\) to \(P(x)\).  Since that sector has
angle \(2\arctan(x)\), its area is \(\arctan(x)\).  The arctangent version
uses the same rational increment and decrement formulas, but it is packaged as
its own raw algorithm.  The initial lower bound is the chord-triangle area
\(x/(1+x^2)\), and the initial upper bound is the tangent-triangle area \(x\).
\begin{verbatim}
def increment(p, q, r):
    return (2*(r-p)*(q-p)*(r-q) /
            ((1+p*p)*(1+q*q)*(1+r*r)))

def decrement(p, q, r):
    return ((r-p)*(q-p)*(r-q) /
            ((1+p*r)*(1+p*q)*(1+q*r)))

def refine(a, b, intervals):
    new_intervals = []
    for p, r in intervals:
        q = (p + r) / 2
        a = a + increment(p, q, r)
        b = b - decrement(p, q, r)
        new_intervals.append((p, q))
        new_intervals.append((q, r))
    return a, b, new_intervals

def arctan(x, n):
    a = x / (1 + x*x)
    b = x
    intervals = [(0, x)]
    for _ in range(n):
        a, b, intervals = refine(a, b, intervals)
    return [a, b]
\end{verbatim}
The restriction \(0\le x\le1\) is the only range needed for the first
trigonometric construction.  The complementary ranges are handled later by
symmetry, reciprocal identities, and period reduction.  The theorem relating
the arctangent algorithm at \(x=1\) to \code{piCircleArea} is proved as a
separate stagewise comparison, not made true by definitional identification.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Arctangent validity and monotonicity]
\label{thm:arctan-area-valid-monotone}
\uses{def:arctan-area-stage-algorithm,def:raw-real-order}
For each rational \(x\in[0,1]\), \code{arctan(x,n)} is a valid raw real.
Moreover, the resulting function is monotone: if \(0\le x\le y\le1\), then
\[
  \arctan(x)\le \arctan(y).
\]
The finite proof compares nested rational sectors.  Strict monotonicity on
apart rational inputs follows from the positive area of the additional sector
between \(P(x)\) and \(P(y)\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{definition}[Inverse arctangent search]
\label{def:inverse-arctan-search}
\uses{def:arctan-area-stage-algorithm,thm:arctan-area-valid-monotone}
For an angle parameter \(t\) interpreted as \(t\pi/2\) and known on the
principal first-octant branch \(0\le t\le 1/2\), define its
tangent parameter \(u\in[0,1]\)
by bisection against the monotone arctangent algorithm.
\begin{verbatim}
def inverse_arctan(t, n):
    lo, hi = 0, 1
    for i in range(n):
        m = (lo+hi)/2
        am = arctan(m, n+i)
        if am.hi < t*pi/2:
            lo = m
        elif t*pi/2 < am.lo:
            hi = m
        else:
            lo, hi = lo, hi
    return [lo, hi]
\end{verbatim}
The stage used for \code{arctan(m, n+i)} is intentionally allowed to increase
inside the search: the comparison must be refined until it certifies which
side of \(m\) contains the inverse, or else safely keeps the current bracket.
The output is a rational interval for \(u=\tan(t\pi/2)\).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Sine and cosine from inverse arctangent]
\label{def:sin-cos-from-inverse-arctan}
\uses{def:inverse-arctan-search,rem:rational-circle-point}
On the first octant, compute an interval for \(u=\tan(t\pi/2)\) by
\cref{def:inverse-arctan-search} and then evaluate the rational circle
coordinates
\[
  \cos(t\pi/2)=\frac{1-u^2}{1+u^2},
  \qquad
  \sin(t\pi/2)=\frac{2u}{1+u^2}.
\]
Interval evaluation of these rational formulas gives boxes for sine and
cosine without ever computing a square root.  Quadrant, sign, and reciprocal
reductions extend the construction to the full normalized interval \([0,1]\)
once \(\pi/4=\arctan(1)\) and the endpoint at \(t=1\) have been identified.
\end{definition}

\section{Trigonometric Functions}

\begin{definition}[Rational-circle coordinates]
\label{def:rational-circle-sin-cos}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.RationalCircle.Trigonometry.cos}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.RationalCircle.Trigonometry.sin}
\uses{rem:rational-circle-point}
For a rational slope coordinate \(u\), define the exact rational coordinates
\[
  c(u)=P(u)_x,\qquad s(u)=P(u)_y.
\]
Equivalently,
\[
  c(u)=\frac{1-u^2}{1+u^2},
  \qquad
  s(u)=\frac{2u}{1+u^2}.
\]
These are finite-stage coordinates, not yet the full angle-input
trigonometric functions.

\begin{theorem}[Law of cosines and the equilateral value]
\label{thm:law-of-cosines-equilateral}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.RationalCircle.Stage.segmentNormSq_law_of_cosines}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.RationalCircle.Stage.dot_eq_half_of_unit_equilateral}
\uses{rem:rational-circle-point}
\leanok
For rational coordinate points \(p,q\), the squared chord length satisfies the
coordinate law of cosines
\[
  \|q-p\|^2=\|p\|^2+\|q\|^2-2(p\cdot q).
\]
Consequently, if \(p\) and \(q\) are unit-circle points and the chord between
them also has squared length \(1\), then \(p\cdot q=1/2\).  This is the
formalized equilateral-triangle core behind the familiar
\(\cos(60^\circ)=1/2\), hence \(\sin(30^\circ)=1/2\).  Turning it into the
actual normalized-angle statement for \(\sin_C(1/3)\) still requires the
angle-to-circle-point construction to identify the one-third input with the
equilateral chord.
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}[Gauss's 17-gon]
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.GaussSeventeen.powerSeriesCheck_default}

small algebraic computations.  A much more striking example is Gauss's
constructibility theorem for the regular \(17\)-gon.  In our normalized angle

that \(\cos(2\pi/17)\) and \(\sin(2\pi/17)\) lie in a tower of quadratic
extensions of \(\Q\), hence are given by nested square roots and are
ruler-and-compass constructible.  For instance, one possible explicit
certificate is

\cos(2\pi/17)=
\frac{-1+\sqrt{17}+\sqrt{34-2\sqrt{17}}

nested radical by rational interval arithmetic, and checks that the resulting
cosine and sine intervals overlap at the chosen stages.  This is only a
precision check, not a formal proof of Gauss's theorem.

The rational-circle coordinates satisfy:
\[
  t(u)c(u)=s(u)\quad(c(u)\ne0),
\]
\[
  \operatorname{ct}(u)s(u)=c(u)\quad(s(u)\ne0),
\]
\[
  \operatorname{sc}(u)c(u)=1\quad(c(u)\ne0),
  \qquad
  \operatorname{cs}(u)s(u)=1\quad(s(u)\ne0),
\]
\[
  1+t(u)^2=\operatorname{sc}(u)^2\quad(c(u)\ne0),
\]

  1+\operatorname{ct}(u)^2=\operatorname{cs}(u)^2\quad(s(u)\ne0).

\[
  \operatorname{sc}(u)^2-t(u)^2=1\quad(c(u)\ne0),
  \qquad
  \operatorname{cs}(u)^2-\operatorname{ct}(u)^2=1\quad(s(u)\ne0).

\[

\section{The Geometric Point Algorithm}

\begin{definition}[Angle-to-circle-point raw function]
\label{def:geometric-point-functionraw}
\uses{def:sin-cos-from-inverse-arctan}
The central construction target is a raw function
\[
  C : \Q \to \hbox{boxes in }\Q^2
\]
whose value at a rational input \(t\), interpreted as \(t\pi/2\), encloses the
corresponding oriented unit-circle point.  The construction is now routed
through the monotone inverse of the arctangent area algorithm, not through a
square-root normalization.
\begin{verbatim}
def geometric_point_stage(t, n):
    t0, transform = reduce_period_quadrant_and_sign(t)
    u = inverse_arctan(t0, n)
    box = interval_eval(lambda t: Point((1-t*t)/(1+t*t), 2*t/(1+t*t)), u)
    return apply_octant_transform(transform, box)
\end{verbatim}
Here each helper is a finite rational procedure: period and octant reduction
uses the area definition of \(\pi\), inverse search uses monotonicity of
\(\arctan\), interval evaluation uses rational formulas, and the final
transform only changes signs and swaps coordinates.  The algorithm may be
refactored internally, but its public output should be a single point raw
function.
\end{definition}

\section{Identity Proof Strategy}

\begin{theorem}[Circle group law]
\label{thm:rational-circle-group-law}
\uses{rem:rational-circle-point}
Let
\[
  P(u)\cdot P(v)
  =
  (P(u)_xP(v)_x-P(u)_yP(v)_y,\,
   P(u)_xP(v)_y+P(u)_yP(v)_x)
\]
be multiplication of unit-circle points.  In the stereographic chart, this
has a rational chart-change formula away from the missing point.  The missing
point is handled by the same quadrant or projective branch data used by the
geometric point algorithm.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Projected addition formulas]
\label{thm:geometric-addition-formulas}
\uses{def:geometric-trig-functionraw-construction,thm:rational-circle-group-law}
Once the point raw function is proved compatible with circle multiplication,
the projected functions satisfy
\[
  \cos_C(a+b)=\cos_C(a)\cos_C(b)-\sin_C(a)\sin_C(b),
\]
\[
  \sin_C(a+b)=\sin_C(a)\cos_C(b)+\cos_C(a)\sin_C(b).
\]
The proof should first be finite and rational on circle boxes, then lifted to
the raw functions by validity and equivalence of the coordinate projections.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Standard geometric trigonometric identities]
\label{thm:geometric-trig-identities}
\uses{thm:geometric-addition-formulas,thm:rational-circle-basic-identities}
The final identity package for this chapter contains all the usual
trigonometric identities: the Pythagorean identities
\[
  \sin_C^2 x+\cos_C^2 x=1,\quad
  1+\tan_C^2 x=\sec_C^2 x,\quad
  1+\cot_C^2 x=\csc_C^2 x,
\]
addition and subtraction formulas, parity formulas, double-angle formulas,
half-angle formulas, cofunction formulas, reciprocal and quotient formulas,
and period formulas.  Each identity should be proved from the circle point
construction and its algebra, not by importing another trigonometric
representation.
\end{theorem}

\section{Formalization Order}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Formalize the rational parametrization of the circle, including its
  Pythagorean-triple origin, dyadic refinement, determinant area formulas, and
  the incremental inner and outer corrections.

  \item Prove validity of the area-first \(\pi\) algorithm and then formalize
  the analogous \(\arctan(x)\) algorithm on \([0,1]\): ordered bounds,
  nesting, convergence, and monotonicity.

  \item Invert \(\arctan\) by certified bisection.  This produces the tangent
  parameter \(u\) for angles in the first octant using only rational interval
  comparisons.

  \item Define sine and cosine by evaluating the rational parametrization at
  the inverse-arctangent parameter.  Extend by octant, quadrant, and period
  transformations, still avoiding square roots.

  \item Define the remaining trigonometric functions as partial quotient or
  reciprocal raw functions with explicit apartness domains.

  \item Prove all usual trigonometric identities for the projected algorithms.
  The main proof inputs are the unit-circle law, circle multiplication, the
  arctangent inverse specification, and the correctness of period and quadrant
  reduction.

  \item Put comparison with any other representation in a later comparison
  chapter or file.  That later stage can import the other definitions it
  needs; this chapter should not depend on them.
\end{enumerate}

The next chapter abstracts the same finite pattern.  Instead of circle-sector
fans, it starts with a monotone rational function on a rational interval,
forms dyadic lower and upper sums, and proves that these sums define the
corresponding definite integral as a raw real.  The arctangent computation in
this chapter is the motivating example: it is the integral of the monotone
kernel \(1/(1+x^2)\), but here it has first been presented in geometric
polygonal form.
