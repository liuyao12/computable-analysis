\begin{verbatim}
coordinates -> rational identity -> squared-length certificate
                                      -> square-root bracket when needed
\end{verbatim}
Pythagoras, Heron, the cosine law, Ptolemy, Pick, and orientation share this
pattern. Lengths are recovered from certified brackets; no completed plane is
needed for the finite statements.

The geometric API keeps three layers separate:

\begin{verbatim}
exact rational coordinates -> squared identities -> interval lengths
                         -> area/order certificate
\end{verbatim}

The last step is always a finite comparison between rational boxes.  Geometry
is used to supply the inequalities; the abstract `real` layer only packages
the resulting nested computations.

\section{Angles and rotations}
\label{thm:finite-quarter-turn}

The blueprint writes sine in one way: \(\sin(\pi x)\).  Inside the circle
algorithm we use the normalized quarter-turn coordinate \(t=2x\) on this
half-period; it is only an implementation parameter.

\begin{verbatim}
area bracket -> angle bracket -> rational circle -> finite identities
\end{verbatim}
Quarter-turns and de Moivre belong here as finite rotation certificates;
unrestricted angle semantics remain a separate bridge.
The common-prefix complex rotation evaluator exposes stable real and imaginary
coordinates; their shrinking boxes provide the shared continuity modulus.
The derivative layer identifies the two coordinate derivatives with the
opposite coordinate, closing the finite rotation calculus at the public stable
evaluator level:

\begin{verbatim}
complex-prefix quotient -> coordinate quotient -> target coordinate box
sin derivative = cos       cos derivative = -sin
\end{verbatim}

The proof is a transport of finite interval certificates, not an appeal to a
completed real-valued sine function.

\lean{ComputableAnalysis.RotationSeries.stableUniformRotationSinFunction_hasDerivativeOnInterval}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.RotationSeries.stableUniformRotationCosFunction_hasDerivativeOnInterval}
\leanok

The half-angle algorithm is the central example:

\begin{verbatim}
parent circle box -> (1 +/- cosine)/2 -> rational sqrt box
                  -> child sine/cosine boxes -> repeat
\end{verbatim}

Each stage records range, circle-equation, and width invariants.  Nested
radicals are therefore not an appeal to an already completed square root; they
are executable interval refinements.

The table is also representation-stable: changing the requested internal
precision produces overlapping sine and cosine boxes.  Thus a later proof may
switch evaluator precision without introducing a new notion of angle or
assuming extensional equality of interval algorithms.

\section{The input to integration}

This chapter supplies the function values; Chapter~\ref{ch:integrals} supplies
the area theorem.  The first target is
\(\int_0^{1/2}\sin(\pi x)\,dx\), whose samples come from the arctangent
inverse and the rational circle.  Its equal-dyadic rectangle computation and
the effective FTC are developed in the next chapter, so the same integral
interface can be reused for other special functions.

\begin{verbatim}
circle geometry -> rational sine/cosine boxes -> function evaluator
                                          -> integral chapter
\end{verbatim}

The endpoint facts \(\cos(0)=1\) and \(\cos(\pi/2)=0\) are the anchors used
later to identify the endpoint difference with reciprocal pi.

The inverse-arctangent route is a finite search followed by the rational circle
map.  The geometric area loop and its rectangle comparison are certified by
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.ArctanGeometry.arctanAreaLoopState_succ}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.ArctanGeometry.arctanIntegralRectangleCompute_nested}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.ArctanGeometry.arctanGeom_valid_on_unit}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.ArctanGeometry.arctanGeom_one_compute_eq}.
This is the bridge used by the integral and FTC chapters; no classical
real-valued angle function is assumed.

The bisection branch need not use the geometric evaluator's native stage
schedule.  A faster or more regular scheduled evaluator is admissible when
its point values are proved equivalent to the geometric `realRaw`; the
abstract inverse package records that equivalence rather than requiring
definitional equality.
