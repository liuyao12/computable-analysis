\chapter{Complex Paths and Analytic Continuation}
\label{ch:complex-paths}

Complex analysis can begin with polynomial identities, finite paths, and
the series charts just constructed.  The contour, its subdivision, and
the local bounds are supplied data.  We prove the integral identities
actually used, rather than first postulating an arbitrary holomorphic
function on an arbitrary open set.

\section{Continuity of represented values}
At a valid represented real point $a\in D$, continuity supplies a positive
rational radius $\delta$ for every positive rational tolerance $\varepsilon$:
\[
 y\in D,\quad |y-a|\le\delta
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad |f(y)-f(a)|\le\varepsilon.
\]
The output inequality means $-\varepsilon\le f(y)-f(a)\le\varepsilon$,
using represented arithmetic and exact order. For complex functions, use
the coordinate maximum norm. Continuity throughout $D$ means this same law
at every valid domain point; the radius can depend on the base point.
The pointwise and whole-domain witnesses convert in both directions.
Changing valid input names or replacing an evaluator by an equivalent one
preserves the law and reuses the radius computation. A common output stage
is not part of this definition. Finite interval estimates remain proof tools.
Real constants and identity have constructed witnesses. A derivative estimate
at a single complex point proves continuity there without requiring continuity
of the derivative or openness of the domain.

\section{Derivatives as continuous divided differences}
For a real or complex represented function, supply a valid,
representation-invariant computation $Q_a$ satisfying
\[
 f(y)-f(a)\simeq(y-a)Q_a(y),\qquad Q_a(a)\simeq d,
\]
and continuous at $a$. This characterizes the quotient away from the center
and gives its value at the center without division or an equality decision.
Differentiability throughout a domain does not require continuity of the
derivative function. For $f(y)=y^2$, take $Q_a(y)=y+a$.

Pointwise continuity laws prove the sum, product, and chain constructions:
\[
 Q^{fg}_a(y)=Q^f_a(y)g(y)+f(a)Q^g_a(y),\qquad
 Q^{g\circ f}_a(y)=Q^g_{f(a)}(f(y))Q^f_a(y).
\]
Constants, identity, every finite represented-coefficient polynomial, and all
iterated polynomial derivatives have actual quotient extensions. Restricting
to real inputs and taking real parts supplies real derivative witnesses.
Equivalent centers, derivative names, and evaluators preserve the construction.
Uniqueness on supplied open complex domains is proved, not assumed.

Subtracting $d(y-a)$ leaves $(y-a)(Q_a(y)-d)$, so quotient continuity proves
the existing first-order remainder law. Additional open-domain and
derivative-continuity evidence enters the holomorphic interface below.
A general reverse constructor from arbitrary remainder witnesses has not
been proved. Reciprocal and elementary-series quotient adapters remain open;
formal derivative formulas and value evaluators alone do not provide them.

\section{A supplied continuous complex derivative}
Our working holomorphic functions have an open domain, represented value and
derivative computations, and explicit local moduli. For every positive
rational $\varepsilon$, a positive rational $\delta$ controls the remainder:
\[
 \|z-a\|_\infty\le H\le\delta
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 \|f(z)-f(a)-d(a)(z-a)\|_\infty\le\varepsilon H.
\]
Here $H$ is positive rational and
$\|u\|_\infty=\max(|\Re u|,|\Im u|)$. A separate local modulus controls
$\|d(z)-d(a)\|_\infty$. All inputs are valid represented complex values;
domain membership and values respect representation equivalence.

This deliberately supplies more data than bare pointwise differentiability.
It assumes no contour or Taylor identity. The checked constructors
\texttt{FunctionTheory.affine\_holomorphic} and
\texttt{FunctionTheory.square\_holomorphic} cover $cz+b$ with arbitrary
represented coefficients and $z^2$, respectively. For the latter, the
remainder is $(z-a)^2$, bounded by $2H^2$; both the derivative-error and
derivative-continuity radii can be $\varepsilon/2$. Transport of the derivative
estimate between equivalent base-point and derivative representations is
proved. Uniform rectangle estimates and the later Cauchy bridges remain
separate obligations.

\subsection{Reusable differential calculus and polynomial applications}
The sum, product, and chain rules now construct full holomorphic witnesses:
\[
 (f+g)'=f'+g',\qquad (fg)'=f'g+fg',\qquad
 (g\circ f)'=(g'\circ f)f'.
\]
Sums and products use intersection domains; compositions use
$\{z\in D_f:f(z)\in D_g\}$. The chain rule constructs local image control
from the inner derivative estimate. A finite input box bounds the derivative,
and its unit-error radius supplies a local linear bound from the base point. This proves
continuity of the function without compactness. Derivative continuity is
proved by the corresponding continuity calculus.

Polynomials with arbitrary represented complex coefficients are evaluated by
Horner's rule. Formal differentiation is proved to agree with actual analytic
derivatives, and every successive polynomial derivative is holomorphic.
For polynomial coefficients $A,B,C$, the polynomial expression
$Ap''+Bp'+Cp$ agrees with the existing second-order analytic residual.
Vanishing remains an equation to prove. The general reciprocal and complex
series adapters, Cauchy's formula from holomorphicity, and the general
identity theorem remain open. Direct represented series with differentiated
tails are the next construction direction.

\section{Integrating on a segment}
Subdivide the actual oriented segment into finite chunks with endpoints
$z_j,z_{j+1}$. For each chunk, certify a rational rectangle $B_j$
containing the function's value rectangles throughout that chunk. Form
\[
 I_P=\sum_j B_j(z_{j+1}-z_j).
\]
Refine the chunks and their value enclosures, proving that the integral
rectangles are nested (or have justified finite intersections) and that
both coordinate widths tend to zero. This defines the integral of the
particular function on this segment. No parametrization is needed.

For a vertical upward chunk the displacement is $ih$ with $h\ge0$.
Rotation preserves rectangular shape, but exchanges the bounds:
\[
 i\bigl([a,b]+i[c,d]\bigr)=[-d,-c]+i[a,b].
\]
Coordinate monotonicity can identify each bound from endpoint rectangles;
otherwise a proved whole-chunk interval estimate supplies it. Polygonal
paths concatenate these finite sums. Parametrized formulas are subsequent
comparisons between explicit computations, not the initial definition.

The native box certificate requires a sound range evaluator and starts
with one chunk, not an empty partition. Constant open paths are checked.
The later midpoint-based Cauchy computation has its own proved convergence
and cancellation; its agreement with this direct enclosure construction
is a separate formalization obligation.

In a single series disk, the primitive
\[
 P(c+w)=\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{a_n}{n+1}w^{n+1}
\]
has a geometric tail on every smaller disk.  Its finite polynomial
identities and their tails prove
\[
 \int_{[p,q]}F=P(q)-P(p)
\]
whenever the segment stays in such a disk.  This also holds for a
specified continuously differentiable parametrized arc in the disk: the
finite polynomial chain rule gives the result, and its error is bounded
by a supplied arc-length bound.  Circle arcs from the earlier geometric
construction are one such family.

\section{A finite form of the contour theorem}
\label{thm:finite-contour}
Suppose a rational triangulated polygon is supplied, and each closed
triangle lies in a smaller certified series disk of $F$.  The descriptions
are proved to agree on their overlaps.  The integral around each triangle
is zero, by its local primitive.  Add these finitely many identities:
interior edges cancel with their opposite orientations.  Therefore
\[
 \int_{\partial\Omega}F(z)\,dz=0.
\]
A polygon with holes is treated by the same cancellation, giving the
outer boundary integral as the sum of the positively oriented inner
boundary integrals.  A finite chain of these subdivisions proves equality
of two path integrals.  No existence of a finite subcover is assumed:
the finite subdivision and the certified disk margins are exhibited.

This is also a computational form of contour deformation.  It is
important to retain the overlap comparisons.  Merely evaluating the
same printed formula on two sides of a branch cut is not a proof that
one has continued the same function.

\section{Coefficient extraction on a circle}
For $0<r<R$, parametrize the circle by
\[
 z(t)=c+rE(2\pi it),\qquad 0\le t\le1.
\]
This is computed at rational $t$ by trigonometry, not by assuming circle
points are rational.  Its derivative and length bound have already been
proved.  Finite trigonometric orthogonality, or the trigonometric FTC,
gives for every integer $j$
\[
 \int_0^1 E(2\pi ijt)\,dt=\begin{cases}1,&j=0,\\0,&j\ne0.\end{cases}
\]
Integrate a finite polynomial and use its uniform series tail.  The result is
\[
 \boxed{a_k=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{|z-c|=r}
                  \frac{F(z)}{(z-c)^{k+1}}\,dz.}
\]
If the value boxes on this circle certify $|F|\le B$, this formula gives
$|a_k|\le B r^{-k}$.  Thus an independent boundary estimate can produce
better coefficient bounds, rather than just use the original majorant.

For a computed $w$ with $|w-c|<r$, expand
$(z-w)^{-1}=\sum_{j\ge0}(w-c)^j/(z-c)^{j+1}$ on the circle.  Its tail
is bounded by a geometric series with ratio $|w-c|/r$.  Finite integration
and the coefficient formula give the local Cauchy identity
\[
 \boxed{F(w)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{|z-c|=r}
                         \frac{F(z)}{z-w}\,dz.}
\]
Both sides are now specified computations with independent truncation and
quadrature errors.  We have proved the identity for the supplied chart,
not invoked it to manufacture a chart from unverified pointwise data.

\section{Rectangles as contour primitives}
For the residue route it suffices to begin with rational rectangles.
On each oriented side, subdivide the segment geometrically and enclose
the real and imaginary value coordinates throughout each chunk. Supply
finite monotonicity partitions or rational range bounds, then multiply
by each chunk displacement and add the resulting rectangles.
On a monotone piece of length $L$, range width $V$, mesh size at most
$\delta$, and endpoint value boxes of width at most $\eta$, endpoint
rectangles have gap at most $\delta V+2L\eta$. Add these budgets over the
pieces and sides. A non-rational turn may instead have a shrinking rational
bracket and a bound on its central range. Existing piecewise and turning-point
integral constructions supply this pattern.

These data construct integrals. Complex analyticity is a separate local
certificate needed for Cauchy cancellation and residues: even $\bar z$
has monotone affine side components, while
$\oint_{\partial\Omega}\bar z\,dz=2i\,\mathrm{area}(\Omega)$.
Finite sums of certified side integrals allow regular-part subtraction
without searching anew for the monotonicity intervals of a sum. This is
the proposed rectangle interface; the checked example below is the square
pole normalization. Existing polygonal constructions are retained.

\section{Arctangent normalizes the Cauchy pole}
\label{sec:cauchy-arctan}
There is a direct rectangle route to the same normalization. Split the
counterclockwise boundary of $[-1,1]^2$ into eight affine half-edges.
On the right side, $z=1+iu$ gives
\[
 \frac{dz}{z}=\frac{u+i}{1+u^2}\,du.
\]
Pair opposite parameter signs and rotate through the four sides. The real
parts cancel exactly, leaving $8i/(1+u^2)$ on $[0,1]$. Our rational boxes
enclose every tagged sum on the common partition and have imaginary width
at most $16/2^n$. Thus the checked square computation satisfies
\[
 \oint_{\partial[-1,1]^2}\frac{dz}{z}=8iA(1)=2\pi i,
\]
with the original geometric $\pi$. This is
\texttt{PDE.CauchyContour.raw\_equiv\_twoPiI}; translation and rational
nonzero dilation preserve its pole pullback.

PNT+ formalizes the corresponding rectangle argument in Mathlib:
arctangent and logarithm side primitives normalize the pole, while
Mathlib's rectangle Cauchy theorem removes the regular part. Its checked
finite residue theorem concerns simple poles with no boundary poles.
Applying that theorem to
\[
 \frac{F(z)}{z-p}=
 \frac{F(z)-F(p)}{z-p}+\frac{F(p)}{z-p}
\]
gives the Cauchy value formula once the divided difference is extended
holomorphically across $p$. PNT+'s explicitly named derivative formula
uses a separate Mathlib circle-integral theorem. These are external
formal references, not imports into our foundation.

The native square normalization is checked. General native contour
transport, regular-part removal and the Cauchy integral formula still
require their explicit subdivision, local chart and convergence bridges.
The adjacent general contour descriptions give the mathematical program,
not additional checked declarations supplied by this normalization.
See the source ledger \texttt{docs/PNT\_CAUCHY\_ARCTAN.md}.

\section{Known poles and finite residues}
Suppose near a specified point $s$ the computation has a proved description
\[
 F(z)=\sum_{j=1}^m b_{-j}(z-s)^{-j}+H(z),
\]
where $H$ has a series chart.  On a sufficiently small certified circle,
finite coefficient extraction gives
\[
 \int F(z)\,dz=2\pi i b_{-1}.
\]
The coefficient $b_{-1}$ is called the residue.  The finite contour
cancellation above transports this result to larger supplied polygonal
contours when the region between them is covered by certified charts.
For circular boundary components, approximate the arcs by the geometric
polygonal construction inside the annular chart cover.  Uniform continuity
and path-length bounds control the integral errors; radial joining edges
cancel.  This supplies the same transport for those boundaries.

As a direct example, on $|z|=2$,
\[
 \frac1{z(z-1)}=\sum_{n\ge0}z^{-n-2},
\]
so its integral is zero.  Around $0$ and $1$ separately the residues are
$-1$ and $1$.  The two ways of computing the contour integral agree.
The poles were supplied and their expansions proved; no unsupported
search for every pole or zero has taken place.

\section{Logarithm along a specified path}
Start near $1$ with
\[
 L(1+w)=\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}n w^n,\qquad |w|<1.
\]
Its derivative is the geometric series for $1/(1+w)$.  The series product
and chain rules show $E(L(1+w))=1+w$: its quotient by $1+w$ has all
nonconstant derivative coefficients zero and constant value $1$.
On the positive real interval it agrees with the previously constructed
logarithm by its primitive identity and the value at $1$.

Suppose a value $L(c)$ has been continued to a nonzero computed point $c$.
Choose a certified $d<|c|$ and set
\[
 L(c+w)=L(c)+\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}n(w/c)^n,
 \qquad |w|\le d.
\]
This is a fresh chart, whose radius comes from separation from zero.
The value at the next center is computed from the old chart, and the two
charts have derivative $1/z$ on their overlap.  Their local primitive
identities and matching value prove agreement on the overlap, which is
convex.  A finite chain of such steps computes continuation along a
supplied path avoiding zero.  A rational positive distance from zero on
each path piece gives a finite step schedule.

The end value can depend on the path.  On the unit circle,
\[
 \int\frac{dz}{z}=\int_0^1 2\pi i\,dt=2\pi i.
\]
Thus continuation from $1$ once counterclockwise changes the logarithm
value by $2\pi i$.  Starting with value $0$, the computation $E(L/2)$
returns to $-1$, not $1$.  This constructs the sign change of the square
root after a circuit.  A path and a starting branch are part of the input;
there is no single globally chosen square-root computation on the
punctured plane.

\section{When does a chain of charts count as continuation?}
A continuation certificate consists of finitely many local charts, steps
lying strictly within their certified disks, and proved comparisons on
each successive overlap.  Comparisons can be coefficient identities,
local primitive identities, or the uniqueness estimates for an explicitly
constructed differential or integral equation.  Equality of all Taylor
coefficients at a common center implies equality on their common smaller
disk by the finite tail estimates.  Matching only a finite jet is not
enough unless an equation proves that it determines the later coefficients.

A path change is justified by a finite subdivision whose cells stay in
such common charts.  It does not follow from drawing apparently similar
paths.  This requirement exposes the separation and branch information
that an appeal to unrestricted analytic continuation would hide.

The next chapters use two different ways to replenish local bounds:
parameter integrals supply new majorants in right half-planes, while
linear equations supply new evolution bounds away from their coefficient
singularities.  Both can be connected to these local contour computations.


\section{Exact series equality from certified Cauchy data}
The checked reconstruction theorem is conditional on a certified Cauchy
representation and convergence of its boundary moments.  For a rational
center $a$, a positive rational radius $R$, and rational complex $z$ with
$|z-a|^2<R^2$, the independent series computation agrees exactly with the
supplied function computation:
\[
 F(z)\;\equiv\;\mathrm{series}(z).
\]
The Lean declaration is
\texttt{ComputableAnalysis.CauchyTaylor.Disk.eq\_series}; its real and
imaginary component declarations conclude \texttt{RealRaw.Equiv}.
The internal proof expands each finite Cauchy kernel geometrically, proves a
Euclidean disk error estimate, and stabilizes the resulting rational boxes.
It does not read the function evaluator at runtime.

This is not yet a general Taylor theorem from complex differentiability.
Constructing the Cauchy and moment certificates, identifying the moments
with derivatives divided by factorials, and extending the input theorem to
represented complex points remain open.  The certified radius need not be
maximal, and the boundary is excluded.  The reciprocal example proves exact
geometric-series equality on the full unit disk, including points outside
the smaller coordinate diamond. No general polygon integral is needed for
this checked conditional step.


\section{Checked polygonal Cauchy computations}
\label{sec:polygonal-cauchy-checked}
The new native module \texttt{ComplexAnalysis} proves finite subdivision
cancellation on arbitrary rational triangles. A local complex-affine error
of at most $M2^{-d_n}/(n+1)$ on each triangle of a depth-$d_n$ refinement,
where $d_n\ge n$, gives an oriented midpoint-sum bound $MP/(n+1)$.
The actual sample boxes must shrink too. The resulting contour program is
valid and equivalent to zero. Finite triangle collections are supported;
no estimate is required inside an omitted hole.

The arctangent square normalization also supports a local simple-pole
identity. For a positive rational square centered at $a$, arbitrary valid
represented residue $\rho$, and a regular part $g$ with the stated local
approximation data,
\[
 \oint_{\partial S}\left(\frac{\rho}{z-a}+g(z)\right)\,dz
 \simeq2\pi i\rho.
\]
The two computations are constructed separately: sampled reciprocal
pullbacks for the pole and certified triangle quadratures for the regular
part. The family $\rho/(z-a)+z^2$ is instantiated.

These results do not yet supply a general derivative-to-model adapter,
arbitrary-partition agreement, punctured-polygon residue transport,
higher-order poles, or the removable divided-difference construction needed
for Cauchy's value formula. In particular, they do not complete Fuchs's
criterion or Painlev\'e's classification. The reader follows
$y''+z^{-1}y'+y=0$ as a motivating second-order example; the contour
formalization itself is independent of the equation's order.


\section{A real-variable showcase: arctangent Taylor convergence}
For rational inputs $x$, the literal Taylor polynomials
\[
 S_N(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{(-1)^k x^{2k+1}}{2k+1}
\]
converge to the independently computed geometric arctangent exactly when
$|x|\le1$. Both endpoints are included. The partial sums are connected to the
existing alternating interval computation and its finite rectangle comparison.
For $r=|x|>1$, the finite Bernoulli inequality gives
\[
 |S_{N+1}(x)-S_N(x)|\ge r(r^2-1)/3>0\quad(N\ge1),
\]
which rules out every represented limit. The formal classifier currently has
rational inputs. Its extension to arbitrary represented inputs and the general
Cauchy-to-Taylor theorem remain open. The real-variable example motivates
function theory before the differential-equation applications.


\section{Foundations for analytic continuation}
\label{sec:continuation-foundation}

The main uniqueness target is the identity theorem: two holomorphic
functions on the same connected open domain that agree on a neighborhood
agree everywhere. Simple connectedness is not needed. Logarithm branches
continued above and below zero agree on the positive-side component of
their overlap but differ on the negative-side component; the overlap is
disconnected. The reader next explains propagation through Taylor disks,
then line and sequence agreement, ending with the excluded accumulation
point in the example $\sin(1/z)$. The monodromy theorem is a separate
path-independence statement.

A germ is represented by a holomorphic map on a neighborhood of a valid
represented point. Agreement means equality throughout some positive-radius
neighborhood, not equality at the base point alone. The checked continuation
foundation proves germ equivalence, derivative uniqueness, and local gluing:
a supplied represented map with local holomorphic models is holomorphic,
including a constructed derivative-continuity modulus.

For a finite polygonal presentation, local stationary, inverse, and filled
triangle transport laws imply invariance under finite homotopies. In symbols,
\[
 T_{bc}\circ T_{ab}\simeq T_{ac}
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T_p(s)\simeq T_q(s)
\]
when the supplied elementary moves connect the paths. Actual chart-chain
witnesses connect the transport result to holomorphic germs. Affine charts
provide a checked client for arbitrary represented coefficients.

This does not yet prove general analytic monodromy. The identity theorem for
arbitrary holomorphic maps, comparison of arbitrary continuation chains,
subdivision of continuous homotopies, and locally coherent chart selection
from the resulting germ section remain necessary bridges. Simple connectedness does not by
itself assert continuation along every path.


\subsection{Realization and reflected charts}
A total selector of locally coherent holomorphic charts now gives an actual
represented evaluator: select the chart at the input and evaluate that chart.
Local coherence proves representation invariance, and the gluing theorem
constructs its derivative and continuity radii. No quotient representative
or decision of domain membership is needed. Deriving coherent selection from
arbitrary pathwise continuation remains separate.

Reflection of both input and output is also checked:
\[
 f^*(z)=\overline{f(\overline z)},\qquad
 (f^*)'(z)=\overline{f'(\overline z)}.
\]
The reflected open chart preserves the supplied rational radii; reflection
also preserves germ agreement. The general Schwarz theorem across a real
boundary interval still needs its seam proof.

\subsection{A differential-equation application}
Equal holomorphic germs now have equal derivative germs on a smaller
neighborhood. If their derivatives are also supplied as holomorphic maps,
this comparison iterates and identifies their second-order differential
expressions. The coefficients may be arbitrary valid represented complex
values; no equation or uniqueness conclusion is assumed in the comparison.

A concrete client is
\[
 y(z)=z^2,\qquad z^2y''-zy'=0.
\]
Its two-chart continuation changes the computation from direct squaring to
$(z+1)^2-2z-1$, with a proved neighborhood overlap. The ODE transfers across
that overlap by derivative-germ comparison. The exponent-two Frobenius
factor has constant coefficient one and all higher coefficients zero.
On the positive real ray, exact comparisons identify the same scaled jet
$(y,zy')$ with the input of the existing Fuchs growth theorem. A remainder
bound $3|h|^2$ gives radius $\varepsilon/3$; the resulting coarse estimate is
$\lVert Y(a)\rVert_1\le6/a^3$ for $0<a\le1$.

This entire polynomial mode does not construct general Frobenius branches.
General complex series differentiation, uniform ray certificates, sector
bounds, and fundamental-matrix monodromy remain separate obligations.

\subsection{The identity theorem needs an interior accumulation point}
On the connected domain $D=\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}$, the function
$f(z)=\sin(1/z)$ has zeros $z_n=1/(n\pi)$ for $n\ge1$. They accumulate
at zero, which is outside $D$. Thus the example does not contradict the
identity theorem: zeros of a nonzero holomorphic function cannot accumulate
inside its connected domain. This general identity theorem remains a target
for the represented holomorphic interface. The reader develops the local
factorization argument, connectedness, and the distinct obstructions of
poles, branch points, essential singularities, and natural boundaries.

Continuation methods belong in the skills: certified Taylor recentering,
functional equations with denominator separation, Schwarz reflection,
separated algebraic roots, and local ODE uniqueness. The new chapter gives
Gamma recurrence and the natural boundary of $\sum_{k\ge0}z^{2^k}$ as
mathematical examples, not newly checked Lean identities.


\section{Zeta, Gamma, and certified zero counts}
The primary contract \texttt{HolomorphicOn} supplies quotient derivatives on an
open domain; derivative continuity is separate data for legacy clients.
The first special-function laws now prove, for integers \(p\ge2\),
\[
 1+2^{-p}\le\zeta(p)\le2,\qquad\zeta(p)\ne0,
 \qquad p\le q\Longrightarrow\zeta(q)\le\zeta(p).
\]
For positive-integer Gamma values, actual Gauss products satisfy
\[
 G_{m,n}=\frac{n!n^{m+1}}{\prod_{j=0}^{n}(m+1+j)}
 =m!\prod_{j=1}^{m+1}\frac{n}{n+j},\qquad
 G_{m,n}\le m!\le G_{m,n}+\frac{m!(m+1)^2}{n+1}.
\]
Finite intersections give a valid nested computation with exact factorial
limit. This does not yet identify an arbitrary-parameter Euler integral.

Reflection and uniqueness prove an exact critical-line implication: a supplied
zero in a reflection-stable isolating region, together with function symmetry
and uniqueness of the root there, has \(\Re\rho\simeq1/2\).
A supplied sequence of ordered, nested input boxes with shrinking widths
computes an actual represented point. If sound output ranges for every
represented input in those boxes contain zero and shrink in both coordinates,
the point has an exact zero equation. This is a proved completion step;
it assumes no root equation. Reflection and uniqueness can then be applied
to that constructed point. The function-specific range bounds and the
counting interpretation must still be established for an application.

The general argument principle, complex zeta continuation and symmetry, and
actual certified zero counts remain open. An approximate root list supplies
none of those missing conclusions. The maintained zero-counting skill records
the contour, multiplicity, isolation, and completeness obligations.
