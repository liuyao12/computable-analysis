\chapter*{How to read this foundation}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Preface}
\label{ch:preface}


Construct the objects, prove their laws, and compare different computations of the same value.


This book begins with familiar questions: how do we compute \(\sqrt2\) or \(\pi\), and why do those computations work? They lead naturally to functions, integrals, power series, and exact identities. The aim is a foundation for mathematics in which the computations and their justifications stay visible. Decimal evaluations are available when requested; the main results explain what the constructed objects are and how they behave.



\section*{A number comes with a computation}
\label{preface:objects}


A represented real starts with a sequence of rational endpoint pairs:

\[n\longmapsto I_n=[a_n,b_n],\qquad a_n,b_n\in\mathbb Q.\]

It becomes valid only after we prove that the endpoints are ordered, later intervals lie inside earlier ones, and their widths tend to zero. These are separate mathematical obligations. No prescribed rate belongs to the definition; a concrete construction can supply a rate or a procedure for reaching a requested tolerance.


The field \texttt{RealRaw.compute} is a mathematical function. Its type alone does not certify that it is executable. Our concrete constructions use finite rational algorithms, and their operations must preserve executability for executable inputs. General laws may nevertheless quantify over arbitrary valid represented inputs. “Computable” describes the construction discipline; it is not a restriction that silently removes irrational coefficients or inputs from an exact theorem.


The foundation uses its own represented reals and complexes. It does not import Mathlib’s real or complex numbers, or use an abstract completion to supply missing objects.



\section*{An exact identity compares computations}
\label{preface:equality}


Two valid representations can have different intervals and different rates while representing the same value. We write

\[x\simeq y\]

for \texttt{RealRaw.Equiv}: their intervals overlap at every common stage. Validity matters here. Nesting and shrinking make this overlap relation transitive on valid representations. Refinement schedules are proof tools, rather than an extra part of the definition of equality.


This is an exact statement about every stage, not a claim that two long decimal strings happen to agree. Immediately after the first square-root construction, for example, we prove

\[s\cdot s\simeq2.\]

The squared rational bounds contain \(2\) at every stage. This identifies the computed number mathematically, before any request for digits. A decimal approximation still has its usual precision convention: rounding to \(k\) decimal places has error at most \(\tfrac12 10^{-k}\), while truncation determines an interval of width \(10^{-k}\).



\section*{Rational-input functions are primary}
\label{preface:functions}


A square-root computation with rational parameter \(q\) constructs the function \(q\mapsto\sqrt q\); \(\sqrt2\) is one evaluation. Likewise, \(\pi=4\arctan(1)\) leads us from a constant to arctangent. The primary function takes rational inputs, rational complex inputs, or rational coordinates in an explicitly described chart. These inputs are its domain, rather than samples of a function already supplied on a completed continuum.


Evaluation at a represented irrational input is a derived add-on. Continuity gives the bridge: for each positive rational output tolerance, provide an input tolerance controlling the change in the function, and account for the evaluation errors. Shrinking input intervals then justify the output construction. Agreement under equivalent input representations also needs proof.


Endpoint values give range bounds when monotonicity has been justified. They do not define continuity for every function: for \(f(t)=t^2\), the endpoints of \([-h_n,h_n]\) both have value \(h_n^2\), so their singleton hulls fail nesting even though the function is continuous at zero. A general interval extension needs justified bounds for the whole input interval, or a common limit for all rational evaluations approaching the point. Endpoint values themselves can be represented reals, with an independent refinement index.


Different computations can use different coordinates. A rational multiple of \(\pi\), a rational radian, and a rational circle parameter are different inputs. We compare on an overlap, along a justified coordinate transformation, or after constructing extensions. Agreement on overlapping domains does not by itself give a globally transitive relation between functions with different domains. There is no need to discard one computation in favor of a single privileged definition.



\section*{Start with endpoint and secant bounds}
\label{preface:order-first}


When the function is known to be monotone, continuity has a simple construction. For a nondecreasing function, every rational value in an input bracket lies between the endpoint values:

\[a_n\leq t\leq b_n\quad\Longrightarrow\quad f(a_n)\leq f(t)\leq f(b_n).\]

Check that these bounds collapse as the bracket shrinks around the point. For a nonincreasing function, reverse the endpoint roles. If endpoint values are represented reals, refine their rational bounds too:

\[C_k=[\underline f_{m(k)}(a_{n(k)}),\overline f_{m(k)}(b_{n(k)})].\]

Prove the output widths shrink. Exact rational-valued monotone functions give nested output brackets directly; represented endpoint boxes can use the checked finite-intersection construction when nesting needs repair. The input brackets must cover the neighborhood being tested, with domain clipping at boundary points. Monotonicity supplies containment, and collapse supplies continuity.


For a convex function, use the corresponding idea with slopes. Two outer secants bound every sufficiently nearby inner secant:

\[S_f(l_n,u_n)\leq S_f(s,t)\leq S_f(v_n,r_n),\qquad l_n<u_n<s<t<v_n<r_n.\]

Check that the lower and upper slope bounds collapse. Concavity reverses the order. A corner retains a gap, so curvature alone does not supply differentiability. The checked convex bridge and square examples already use this route; concave constructions use their reversed bounds and a justified comparison or transport proof.


These are our preferred methods whenever the order information is available. The general definition below supplies their common meaning, uniqueness, and comparison laws. Internal subdivisions or precision schedules belong in the proof of the full-domain result.

\section*{Two indices and a derivative at any represented point}
\label{preface:limits-and-brackets}


The endpoint values need not be rational. For a rational-input function \(f:\mathbb Q\to\mathrm{RealRaw}\), the interval data for its secants has two indices:

\[Q_{n,k},\qquad n=\text{input-bracket stage},\quad k=\text{secant-value refinement}.\]

Every row is a valid represented value \(u_n\). The indices are independent. The covered-diagonal specification below is proved equivalent to this earlier target-box presentation. For a valid target \(x\), write its stage-\(m\) box as \([\ell_m,r_m]\). Our limit relation requires

\[\forall m\;\forall\varepsilon\in\mathbb Q_{>0}\;\exists N\;\forall n\ge N:\qquad \ell_m-\varepsilon\le u_n\le r_m+\varepsilon.\]

These are exact represented-value inequalities. The positive allowance handles one-sided and zero-width target boxes: a positive sequence approaching zero must also converge to the exact name whose every box is \([0,0]\). Uniqueness of limits and invariance under equivalent target and row representations are proved in Lean.


To define a derivative at a valid represented point \(x\), consider every valid bracket computation \(A\simeq x\). At every stage its interval must lie entirely inside the rational domain, and its width must be strictly positive:

\[[a_n,b_n]_{\mathbb Q}\subseteq\operatorname{dom}(f),\qquad a_n<b_n.\]

At least one such bracket must exist. A valid \(d\) is the derivative when every admissible bracket gives a secant sequence with limit \(d\):

\[S_n=\frac{f(b_n)-f(a_n)}{b_n-a_n}\longrightarrow d.\]

This uses only rational endpoint evaluations, even when \(x\) is irrational. On a closed nondegenerate domain, a checked clipping construction supplies admissible brackets at both endpoints as well as interior points. No finite stage has zero width. Equivalent names of the point admit the same brackets; derivative values are unique up to \texttt{RealRaw.Equiv}. Equivalent rational-input value computations also give the same derivative relation.


The convex computation now earns its derivative role through a general bridge: exact order of disjoint secants, a justified domain neighborhood, and validity of the literal outer-secant boxes imply the common-limit property. No derivative identity is assumed. The square computation is checked at every valid represented input, including closed-domain endpoints. The original centered algorithm is also checked for square and for an affine function with an arbitrary represented coefficient, so the endpoint values may be irrational.

\section*{Choose a covered valid diagonal}
\label{preface:parametrized-enclosures}


Input refinement and output evaluation can advance at different speeds. For rational interval data, the specification asks for cofinal index maps whose selected output boxes form a valid represented real:

\[C_k=Q_{n(k),m(k)},\qquad n(k)\longrightarrow\infty,\quad m(k)\longrightarrow\infty.\]

The selected boxes are ordered, nested, and arbitrarily narrow. Coverage gives them their mathematical role: for a sequence they enclose every remaining term; for continuity they enclose every rational function value throughout the selected input bracket. Selecting only favorable samples would not suffice. The alternating sequence has constant subsequences but no covered limit; a jump cannot acquire continuity from one-sided samples.


Continuity keeps rational-input functions primary. Every admissible input bracket must yield the same output value through some justified parametrization. Independent bracket constructions are proved equivalent using a rational point in the intersection of their input boxes. Identity and constants have explicit valid diagonals. Ordinary interval bounds, scheduled interval bounds, and epsilon–delta certificates construct further output names using finite prefix intersections, without assuming an irrational extension value.


The derivative definition now uses covered parametrized secant limits for every admissible bracket. Its exact compatibility theorem preserves the earlier secant-limit laws and convex examples. The older finite-difference evidence has a bridge with positive step radii and justified continuity of the derivative values. Complex local estimates supply valid coordinate diagonals, and their normalized first-order remainders converge to zero in every direction.


A supplied executable stage schedule gives an executable intersection construction. Logical existence of a schedule is stated separately. Existing FTC, integral, series, modular, and ODE results retain their stated evidence and domain restrictions.

\section*{Holomorphicity from shrinking complex enclosures}
\label{preface:complex-holomorphic}


Holomorphicity requires an open domain and shrinking rational rectangles enclosing every nearby two-point complex secant. At a center \(a\), the candidate derivative \(d\) is their common limit:

\[\begin{gathered}S_f(z,w)=\frac{f(z)-f(w)}{z-w},\qquad z\not\simeq w,\\S_f(z,w)-d\longrightarrow0\quad\text{as both }z,w\longrightarrow a.\end{gathered}\]

At resolution \(n\), cover both coordinates of every secant error for \(\|z-a\|_\infty,\|w-a\|_\infty\le r_n\le2^{-n}\). Each coordinate has a two-index rational interval family and a cofinal selected diagonal that is a valid represented zero. The quotient uses the existing terminating reciprocal algorithm, with nonzero displacement justified by unequal represented values. A favorable subsequence or a few directions cannot satisfy the coverage requirement.


\textbf{Checked equivalence.} These shrinking boxes are equivalent to the previous definition with an anchored complex derivative and continuous derivative. In one direction, continuity of the derivative and the proved whole-segment estimate control every secant. In the other, short secants based at nearby points compare their derivatives, proving first-derivative continuity. Neither function convexity nor a globally convex domain is required.


The checked division-free version bounds the two-point error by the separation of the points:

\[\begin{gathered}\|z-a\|_\infty,\|w-a\|_\infty\le\delta,\quad\|z-w\|_\infty\le H,\\\|f(z)-f(w)-d(z-w)\|_\infty\le\varepsilon H.\end{gathered}\]

A rational anchor-search bound for the displacement and its computed reciprocal proves equivalence with the actual quotient boxes, even when the two points are much closer to each other than to the center.


The weaker pointwise derivative remains available separately. It tests the actual first-order error, anchored at the base point:

\[R_{a,d}(z)=f(z)-f(a)-d(z-a).\]

At neighborhood resolution \(n\), cover both coordinates of every normalized error

\[\begin{gathered}\frac{R_{a,d}(z)}{H},\qquad 0<H\le r_n\le2^{-n},\\\|z-a\|_\infty\le H.\end{gathered}\]

The positive rational \(H\) makes normalization an exact rational scaling. Each coordinate has a two-index rational interval family, with some cofinal selected diagonal that is a valid represented zero. Coverage includes all valid domain neighbors and all such displacement bounds. Checking one path or a few axis directions would be insufficient.


\textbf{Checked equivalence.} These covered enclosures are equivalent to the first-order estimate: for each rational \(\varepsilon>0\), some rational \(\delta>0\) makes \(\|R_{a,d}(z)\|_\infty\le\varepsilon H\) for every \(0<H\le\delta\) and every domain neighbor bounded by \(H\). The public holomorphicity definition now uses the stronger two-point shrinking boxes above; its equivalence theorem recovers this derivative and proves its continuity. The pointwise enclosure derivative remains a separate weaker property.


Uniqueness, representation invariance, continuity of values, and exact product and chain rules are proved. A whole-segment variation theorem uses pointwise evidence with internally constructed rational bisections: zero derivative gives equal endpoint values, and equal derivatives plus one equal value identify two functions along the segment. These underlying pointwise laws remain available without derivative continuity. The holomorphic function constructions provide shrinking secant boxes, from which derivative continuity is derived.


The existing computations now satisfy this definition: arbitrary represented-coefficient polynomials and rational functions, the established exponential, sine and cosine at arbitrary complex frequency, normalized logarithm branches, Lambert series, and \(E_2,E_4,E_6\) on the full unit disk. Their genuine pole and branch domains and primary rational-input extension proofs are retained. Conjugation is continuous, but the checked negative test excludes every proposed complex derivative at zero.

Cauchy and Taylor statements remain consequences, rather than fields of the definition. The proof uses the derived continuity of the derivative to construct finite rational covers and uniform affine error bounds; oriented rectangle cancellation and the computed reciprocal period give Cauchy recovery.


\section*{Cauchy recovery and an actual Taylor series}
\label{preface:cauchy-taylor-enclosures}


For every positively oriented rational rectangle whose entire closed region stays in the domain, the actual boundary computation recovers every represented interior point:

\[f(a)\simeq\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial J}\frac{f(w)}{w-a}\,dw.\]

At every domain center, the proof constructs a local square, its justified contour values, a positive series radius, and valid represented coefficients. The summed series equals the original function on that neighborhood:

\[\begin{gathered}f(z)\simeq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_n(z-a)^n,\\c_n\simeq\frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}.\end{gathered}\]

The coefficients come from actual Cauchy moments. A geometric kernel remainder proves prefix convergence and exact agreement with the original evaluator; formal jets alone are not used as evidence. The construction and its value respect equivalent representations and holomorphicity witnesses.


Differentiated local series and derivative uniqueness prove that the derivative is itself holomorphic. Iteration constructs every finite higher derivative on the entire original domain, proves that each really differentiates the previous one, and gives higher Cauchy formulas and continuity. First-derivative continuity already follows from the shrinking secant condition. The existing exponential and full-disk Eisenstein computations instantiate the exact Taylor reconstruction at arbitrary represented centers.

\section*{Derive with a skill; certify with a proof}
\label{preface:derive-check}


This separation takes some getting used to. A skill can use substitution, partial fractions, or a familiar trigonometric manipulation to propose the integral of a particular function. We need not first formalize that entire technique as a general Lean theorem. Reusable checked lemmas are helpful, but the derivation can remain a guide for discovering the answer.


The proposed answer then earns its mathematical meaning through a concrete proof. For a definite integral, construct the endpoint expression, verify its derivative against the independently specified integrand on the whole segment, and use a justified FTC argument:

\[\int_a^x f(t)\,dt\simeq F(x)-F(a).\]

The derivative check is often easier than formalizing the technique that found \(F\). The subtraction fixes the base point. Pole avoidance, branch conditions, and validity must hold throughout the segment; checking only the endpoints cannot justify crossing a singularity. The derivative identity becomes an integral identity through FTC and the required integral evidence.


For example, our concrete square-root secant proof certifies

\[\int_a^b\frac{dt}{2\sqrt t}\simeq\sqrt b-\sqrt a\qquad(0<a\le b).\]

Skills guide discovery; Lean checks the proposed result. This is a procedure for proof development, not a claim that every function has a closed form or that an automatic integrator is already implemented. \href{https://liuyao12.github.io/computable-analysis/skill-real-integrals.html}{Read the integration skill}, or \href{https://liuyao12.github.io/computable-analysis/foundation-story/index.html\#ftc}{inspect the checked square-root route}.



\section*{General laws and particular constructions}
\label{preface:laws}


A law can start with supplied, justified objects and prove a new consequence. Proving that such objects exist for a particular function is a separate job. Neither a record named “integral” nor an assumed endpoint identity supplies that proof. Each function brings its own construction and evidence; we do not first insist that every integrand belong to one preselected class.


The current \texttt{Integral.HasIntegral} API uses arbitrarily tight rectangle bounds. This is a checked sufficient route, more restrictive than the intended method-independent core. Numerical validity alone does not establish an integral’s role. The preface describes the intended organization and identifies this implementation boundary explicitly.


The same discipline applies to inverses and derivatives. Convexity or concavity can suggest a useful interval update without being formalized as a general prerequisite. For square root, explicit supporting-line and secant formulas suggest an algorithm; rational inequalities prove containment, nesting, and shrinking. For the functions we actually construct, neighboring secants and their error bounds construct the derivatives we need.



\section*{What “computable” means here}
\label{preface:terminology}


There is substantial common ground with established computable analysis. In the represented-spaces approach, points have names, and computability of a map is defined through a computable transformation of those names. This framework also accommodates noncomputable points. Rational interval representations are therefore not, by themselves, an unfamiliar idea. \href{https://arxiv.org/html/1204.3763\#S3}{Pauly’s introduction} explains that framework.


The distinctive choices of this project are the primary rational-input functions, the separate extension proofs, exact comparison of retained computations, and the use of function-specific constructions beneath general laws. We are developing analysis with explicit computations as its foundation; we are not currently presenting a Turing-machine classification of which analytic problems are computable.


Bishop-style constructive mathematics places a restriction on proof reasoning, typically expressed through intuitionistic logic. Our repository permits classical reasoning: the checked theorem audits may include \texttt{Classical.choice}. We separately require the actual constructors to be executable and their intervals to be justified. Thus a classical proof of validity does not automatically extract an algorithm, and classical choice must not substitute for a missing executable construction. \href{https://researchcommons.waikato.ac.nz/items/5e0e94b5-e729-445a-b11d-56a968dac2cd}{Bridges and Reeves} discuss the constructive tradition.


These distinctions concern the objects, interfaces, and proof discipline. They do not claim that interval arithmetic is new, or that constructive analysis cannot develop completeness. A connection to an established framework would itself be a theorem to prove.
