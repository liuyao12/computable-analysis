\section{Constructive Definite Integrals}

This chapter is the comparison layer.  The trigonometric functions constructed
in Chapter~\ref{ch:rational-circle-trigonometry} remain the geometric
algorithms.  Integral formulas enter only after those algorithms exist, as
theorems saying that a Riemann-sum algorithm computes the same raw real as an
endpoint expression or an inverse branch.

\begin{definition}[Interval function as a total raw evaluator]
\label{def:function-on-interval-to-realfunraw}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.FunctionOnInterval.toRealFunRaw}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.FunctionOnInterval.toRealFunRaw_valid}
\uses{def:real-fun-raw}
\leanok
A function certified on a rational interval \([a,b]\) can be exposed as a
\code{RealFunRaw} whose domain is exactly that interval.  The total
\code{compute} field has a dummy value outside the interval, but every
mathematical statement carries the domain proof, so only interval-certified
evaluations are used.
\end{definition}

\label{def:definite-integral-identity}

For raw functions \(f\) and \(F\), a definite integral identity over
\([a,b]\) consists of:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a certified Riemann-sum construction for \(f\) on \([a,b]\);
  \item a validity proof for the endpoint-difference raw real \(F(b)-F(a)\);
  \item an equivalence proof, in the raw-real overlap sense, between the
  constructed integral and that endpoint difference.
\end{enumerate}
The interval version packages the same statement when \(f\) and \(F\) are
already certified as \code{FunctionOnInterval}s.  The
\code{DefiniteIdentityFor} variant is the same endpoint-difference interface
for newer domain-aware \code{ConstructionFor} algorithms, such as the Farey
constructions below.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[The constant-integrand algorithm]
\label{def:constant-integral-construction}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.Integral.constantFunRaw}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.Integral.linearPrimitiveFunRaw}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.Integral.constantPlan}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.Integral.riemannLeftInterval_constant_one}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.Integral.constant_algorithm_compute}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.Integral.constantConstruction}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.Integral.constantIntegral_compute}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.Integral.constantIntegral_valid}
\uses{def:definite-integral-identity}
\leanok
As a first completely checked integral construction, take the exact rational
integrand \(x\mapsto c\).  The plan uses one subinterval and no evaluation
refinement:
\[
  h=b-a,\qquad
  L_1=\sum_{k<1} h\,c=(b-a)c.
\]
Since every stage returns the exact interval
\(\bigl[(b-a)c,(b-a)c\bigr]\), the boxes are ordered, nested, and shrink
immediately.  Lean packages this as a certified
\code{Integral.Construction} for every rational \(a,b,c\).
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Constant-integrand endpoint identity]
\label{thm:constant-integral-endpoint-identity}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.Integral.endpointDifference_linearPrimitive_compute}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.Integral.constantPrimitiveEndpoint_valid}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.Integral.constant_integral_equiv_endpoint}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.Integral.constantDefiniteIdentity}
\uses{def:constant-integral-construction,def:definite-integral-identity}
\leanok
For the primitive \(F(x)=cx\), the endpoint-difference raw real computes
\[
  F(b)-F(a)=cb-ca.
\]
The constant-integrand construction computes \((b-a)c\), and Lean proves the
two exact rational intervals overlap at every stage.  Thus the first
\code{DefiniteIdentity} is fully formalized:
\[
  \int_a^b c\,\dd x \Equiv cb-ca.
\]
\end{theorem}

\section{Monotone integrands and convex FTC}

The first integral theorem should be for monotone functions on rational
intervals.  A monotone integrand has upper and lower Riemann sums whose gap is
controlled by endpoint variation times the mesh size, so the finite proof is
especially transparent.

\begin{theorem}[Monotone integral construction]
\label{thm:monotone-integral-construction}
\uses{def:constant-integral-construction,def:definite-integral-identity}
A function with certified monotonicity on \([a,b]\) has a valid rational
Riemann-sum integral.  The construction uses left and right sums, or equivalent
interval sums, and the width estimate is the finite monotone-variation bound.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Derivative-bound FTC package]
\label{thm:derivative-bound-ftc-package}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.convexFTC}
\uses{thm:monotone-integral-construction}
\leanok
Monotone integrability supplies a valid integral algorithm.  The fundamental
theorem of calculus supplies something different: an endpoint-equivalence proof
for that integral algorithm.  The current Lean theorem packages the completed
derivative-bound route: once finite local derivative bounds on rational
partition cells enclose both candidate derivative values and primitive endpoint
differences, the integral raw real is equivalent to the endpoint-difference raw
real.  This is one way an integral row in the pi scoreboard can measure genuine
calculus content, while still counting only the underlying pi computation.
\end{theorem}

Define another arctangent representative by
\[
  \arctan_{\int}(x)=\int_0^x \frac{dt}{1+t^2}.
\]
The preceding density calculation shows why this is the right kernel: it is
the differential area swept by the same rational parametrized circle sectors
used by \(\arctan_{\mathrm{geom}}\).
This gives a new computation of \(\pi\) via
\[
  \pi \Equiv 4\arctan_{\int}(1).
\]
The comparison theorem should identify this integral arctangent with both the
geometric-sector arctangent and the power-series arctangent.  Following the
function-equivalence convention, the preferred Lean target is
\code{ArctanIntegralGeomFunctionAgreement}: the integral representation and the
geometric representation agree on the overlap of their domains.  The current
integral representation is defined for \(0\le x\), while the geometric
representation is total, so their overlap is the nonnegative rational branch.
The pointwise-at-\(1\) statement needed for \(\pi\) is then a corollary.
Lean also records a structural warning for the older point-Riemann
\code{Integral.Construction} wrapper: because the kernel is evaluated by exact
point intervals, every stage of \code{arctanIntegral x c} has width zero, and
validity forces all stages to be equal.  Thus the completed refining integral
routes in the scoreboard use the domain-aware \code{ConstructionFor} schedules
below.
\end{definition}

\section{A Projective-Line Test Integral}

The Farey subdivision is meant to work uniformly on the projective line, not
only on bounded intervals.  A useful test, simpler than the Gaussian integral
but still genuinely projective, is
\[
  \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\dd x}{x^4+a x^2+1}.
\]
For \(a>-2\), the classical value is
\[
  \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\dd x}{x^4+a x^2+1}
    =\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{a+2}}.
\]
Thus when \(a=b^2-2\) with rational \(b>0\), the integral should give the
rational pi multiple \(\pi/b\).  The especially clean benchmark is \(a=-1\):
\[
  \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\dd x}{x^4-x^2+1}=\pi.
\]

\lean{ComputableAnalysis.IntegralIdentities.geometricSinOnInterval_valid}

\leanok
Once the geometric point algorithm is available on a rational interval, its
coordinate projections restrict to interval-certified sine and cosine
functions.  This is only a domain wrapper around the geometric algorithms; it
does not introduce a new definition of sine or cosine.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Geometric trigonometric integral identities]
\label{def:geometric-trig-integral-identities}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.IntegralIdentities.GeometricTrigIntegralIdentities}
\uses{def:geometric-trig-on-interval,def:definite-integral-identity}
\leanok
The integral identity target for the geometric trigonometric functions on
\([a,b]\) is the pair of definite integral statements
\[
  \int_a^b \cos(t)\dd t
  \quad\hbox{equals}\quad
  \sin(b)-\sin(a),
\]
\[
  \int_a^b \sin(t)\dd t
  \quad\hbox{equals}\quad
  -\cos(b)+\cos(a).
\]
In Lean this is a structure, not yet an existence theorem: it records the
precise derivative, integral-construction, and endpoint-equivalence data that
must be supplied by the later geometric proof.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Inverse elementary integral identities]
\label{def:inverse-elementary-integral-identities}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.IntegralIdentities.InverseElementaryIntegralIdentities}
\uses{def:definite-integral-identity}
\leanok
The inverse trigonometric and logarithmic comparison targets are:
\[
  \int_0^x \frac{\dd t}{1+t^2}
  \quad\hbox{equals the chosen arctangent branch at }x,
\]
\[
  \int_0^x \frac{\dd t}{\sqrt{1-t^2}}
  \quad\hbox{equals the chosen arcsine branch at }x,
\]
and
\[
  \int_1^x \frac{\dd t}{t}
  \quad\hbox{equals the chosen logarithm branch at }x.
\]
These are branch-local statements.  They are not imported into the geometric
definition of trigonometric functions; they are used to prove that different
constructions agree.
\end{definition}

\section{Abelian Integrals Related to Trigonometry}

\begin{definition}[Algebraic differentials and Abelian integral inverses]
\label{def:abelian-trig-integral-representations}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.AbelianIntegral.DifferentialRaw}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.AbelianIntegral.Raw}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.AbelianIntegral.InverseRepresentation}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.IntegralIdentities.AbelianTrigIntegralRepresentations}
\uses{def:inverse-elementary-integral-identities}
\leanok
An Abelian integral is represented by an algebraic branch, an algebraic
differential, a path, and a certified Riemann-sum algorithm.  The elementary
trigonometric comparison cases include:
\[
  \operatorname{arctan}(x)
  \quad\hbox{from}\quad
  \int_0^x \frac{\dd t}{1+t^2},
\]
\[
  \operatorname{arcsin}(x)
  \quad\hbox{from}\quad
  \int_0^x \frac{\dd t}{w}
  \quad\hbox{on the curve } w^2=1-t^2.
\]
The same infrastructure also records the next Abelian family beyond circular
trigonometry, for example elliptic integrals such as
\[
  \int_0^x
    \frac{\dd t}{\sqrt{(1-t^2)(1-k^2t^2)}}.
\]
This is the natural home for the comparison between circular functions,
inverse trigonometric functions, logarithmic integrals, and later elliptic
examples such as ellipse circumference.
\end{definition}

\section{Proof Order}

The intended formalization order is:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item prove the geometric trigonometric algorithms valid and prove the
  exact identities from Chapter~\ref{ch:rational-circle-trigonometry};
  \item prove the derivative certificates for geometric sine and cosine from
  the circle construction;
  \item feed those certificates into the definite-integral identity package;
  \item prove arctangent, arcsine, and logarithm integral identities on their
  chosen branches;
  \item prove the Abelian inverse representations agree with the corresponding
  geometric or power-series algorithms.
\end{enumerate}

This keeps the definitions honest: geometry first, then integrals as
comparison theorems.
