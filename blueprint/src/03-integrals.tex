\chapter{Integrals and the Fundamental Theorem}\label{ch:integrals}

The motivating integral is circle area.  Crude rectangles under the rational
arctangent kernel give finite lower and upper bounds; an integral is the
resulting \texttt{realRaw} computation.  Lebesgue measure and arbitrary
measurable functions are outside the project.

\begin{verbatim}
rational partition -> value boxes on each cell -> weighted finite sum
                   -> shrinking area box -> realRaw integral
\end{verbatim}

The scoped Lean entry point is
\texttt{ComputableAnalysis.IntegralFoundation}.  It exposes one construction
for each genuinely different computational pattern and transports routine
variants through explicit equivalence edges.

\section{The central bridge: an effective FTC}
\label{thm:convex-ftc}
\label{thm:effective-derivative-bound-ftc}

Historically, the FTC came first for the functions people could explicitly
calculate.  Its mean-value corollary was then isolated as a structural
principle and eventually used to rebuild calculus from completeness.  This
project keeps the earlier computational direction: local finite bounds prove
the endpoint identity directly.

For each cell, a derivative box encloses the primitive increment after
multiplication by the cell width.  The finite sum telescopes.  Different
stages may choose unrelated partitions; shrinking width plus endpoint overlap
is enough, because prefix stabilization constructs nested output boxes.

\section{Riemann--Stieltjes and finite identities}

A finite Stieltjes sum records increments of one rational path weighted by
samples of another.  It is the common finite language for substitution,
integration by parts, and chain rules.

The square identity retains the quadratic variation:
\[
  2\sum_i f_i\,\Delta f_i+\sum_i(\Delta f_i)^2
  =f_n^2-f_0^2.
\]
If the maximum step shrinks, the variation term receives an explicit rational
bound and the endpoint identity follows.

\section{Interface}

\begin{verbatim}
interval evaluators -> finite cell geometry -> finite sum
                    -> shrinking budget -> representation overlap
\end{verbatim}

Every theorem in this chapter follows that route.  When a new function needs a
different local computation, it contributes that evaluator and its finite
bounds; it does not duplicate the integral, FTC, or representation machinery.
