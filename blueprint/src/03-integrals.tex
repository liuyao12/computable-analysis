\section{Constructive Definite Integrals}

This chapter is the comparison layer.  The trigonometric functions constructed
in Chapter~\ref{ch:rational-circle-trigonometry} remain the geometric
algorithms.  Integral formulas enter only after those algorithms exist, as
theorems saying that a Riemann-sum algorithm computes the same raw real as an
endpoint expression or an inverse branch.

This is deliberately a \emph{certificate layer}, not a table of integrals.
The foundation must prove general substitution and integration-by-parts
theorems, with their domain, orientation, and endpoint hypotheses explicit.
After that, a user or an LLM may recall or propose a primitive, a
partial-fraction decomposition, or another familiar calculation; the kernel
checks its finite algebra and the relevant derivative theorem.  A derivative
identity alone does not establish a definite-integral formula: one still
needs a certified integral and a general FTC/substitution/integration-by-parts
bridge to its endpoint difference.  The finite integration-by-parts material
below is retained as a pi benchmark for this general interface, not as a
template for accumulating named integration recipes.

For raw functions \(f\) and \(F\), a definite integral identity over
\([a,b]\) consists of:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a certified Riemann-sum construction for \(f\) on \([a,b]\);
  \item a validity proof for the endpoint-difference raw real \(F(b)-F(a)\);
  \item an equivalence proof, in the raw-real overlap sense, between the
  constructed integral and that endpoint difference.
\end{enumerate}
The interval version packages the same statement when \(f\) and \(F\) are
already certified as \code{FunctionOnInterval}s.  The
\code{DefiniteIdentityFor} variant is the same endpoint-difference interface
for newer domain-aware \code{ConstructionFor} algorithms.  It is stable under
replacing the construction by an equivalent integral computation, via
\code{transportConstruction}, and two endpoint identities for the same
integrand and primitive have equivalent integral raw reals.  The
\code{MonotoneDefiniteIdentityFor} variant records the same endpoint identity
when the integral side is explicitly supplied by a monotone construction, and
forgets back to \code{DefiniteIdentityFor}.  The
\code{GeneralDefiniteIdentityFor} variant records the endpoint identity for
the public piecewise-monotone integral \code{generalIntegralFor}; a one-piece
monotone endpoint identity promotes to it by \code{ofMonotone}.  It can also
be transported across equivalent general constructions, forgotten back to the
ordinary endpoint-identity interface, or built from an ordinary
\code{DefiniteIdentityFor} together with an equivalent general construction.
The endpoint-additivity, linearity, scalar, and order-transfer laws are
available at the ordinary, monotone, and general-integral levels.

The default finite mesh for ordinary definite integrals is static dyadic:
stage \(n\) uses the equal subdivision of \([a,b]\) into \(2^n\) intervals.
This is deliberately different from the dynamic subdivision in
\(\arctangeom\), where the next partition is chosen by repeatedly
refining the rational circle parameters already present in the sector
algorithm.  When an FTC proof uses this default mesh, the bridge to endpoint
identities is \code{definiteIdentity\_of\_staticDyadicEffectiveFTC}.

For an exact rational density \(f\) on \([0,1]\), a nonnegative rational
constant \(L\), and the finite estimate
\(\lvert f(s)-f(t)\rvert\le L\lvert t-s\rvert\), the elementary cell
brackets are
\[
 (r-p)\bigl(f(p)-L(r-p)\bigr),\qquad
 (r-p)\bigl(f(p)+L(r-p)\bigr).
\]
Lean now proves once and for all that splitting \([p,r]\) at its rational
midpoint raises the lower bracket and lowers the upper bracket.  This
\code{lipschitzCells\_refine} theorem is finite rational algebra; concrete
dyadic-integral constructions supply only their density-specific Lipschitz
estimate and their partition bookkeeping.
\end{definition}

The same identities are checked for every supplied \code{RationalPartition}.
On an increasing piece each corner rectangle is nonnegative.  If
\(\Delta u_i\leq\delta\) on the chosen common refinement, Lean proves the
sharp finite estimate
\[
  \sum_{i<n}\Delta u_i\Delta v_i
  \leq\delta\,(v_n-v_0).
\]
It also proves the coarser endpoint-variation bound
\[
  \sum_{i<n}\Delta u_i\Delta v_i
  \leq (u_n-u_0)(v_n-v_0).
\]
Negating both paths preserves the corner sum, giving the corresponding bound
for two decreasing pieces.  Thus the needed increasing/decreasing split is
explicit finite rational data rather than a hidden global variation theorem.
There is already one complete mesh-vanishing schedule: for the unit mesh
\(u_i=v_i=i/n\), the corner sum is exactly \(1/n\).  Given a positive rational
\(\varepsilon\), the fully explicit choice
\(n=\varepsilon.\mathrm{den}+1\) makes that rational corner sum at most
\(\varepsilon\).  This illustrates the intended limit discipline without
asserting the general product theorem.
The first common-refinement interface is now checked for uniform rational
grids: the \(mn\)-piece grid contains the \(m\)-piece grid at indices
\(i\mapsto in\), and contains the \(n\)-piece grid at indices
\(j\mapsto jm\).  Its fine width is exactly the old \(m\)-mesh width divided
by \(n\).  Lean packages the two index maps as a
\code{CommonRefinement} certificate; refinement maps have checked identity
and composition operations.  This is enough for the dyadic meshes used by the
present rectangle algorithms: stage \(n+1\) keeps the old point \(k\) at
\(2k\) and halves the mesh exactly.  Synchronizing arbitrary finite rational
breakpoint lists now has a checked insertion primitive: any rational point in
a specified cell gives a refined partition and an explicit embedding of every
old breakpoint.  The next step no longer needs a caller to supply that cell:
\code{locateInsertionCell} performs a bounded left-to-right scan of the
finite rational partition and proves that the chosen cell encloses the point.
Thus \code{insertionChainOfPointList} turns any finite in-range rational list
into explicit scan-and-insert data.  A finite \code{InsertionChain} composes
such insertions, and its endpoint is a certified refinement of the initial
list.  Moreover, every fine point between embedded consecutive coarse points
is proved to stay inside that coarse cell, so local bounds transfer cell by
cell.  The general merge is now checked as well.  Lean forms
\code{breakpointList} for the right partition, inserts that finite list into
the left partition, and uses a second bounded \code{firstOccurrence} scan to
recover the monotone embedding of every right breakpoint.  The resulting
\code{commonRefinementOfPartitions} carries both index embeddings.  It is a
deterministic finite merge of the supplied ordered data; it intentionally
retains duplicate breakpoints rather than optimizing to a minimal union.  The
clamped breakpoint path is nondecreasing, and its corner sum is bounded by the
square of the endpoint variation.  The maximum-increment premise is now
connected directly to partition data.  The finite predicate
\code{MaxStepAtMost} records a nonnegative rational cell-width cap, and
\code{clampedPath\_step\_le\_of\_maxStep} extends it to every increment of
the total clamped path, including its zero tail.  Therefore
\code{clampedPath\_quadraticVariation\_le\_stepBound\_mul\_endpointDifference}
proves, for any nondecreasing second rational path, that the corner correction
is at most that cap times its endpoint variation.  Uniform partitions
instantiate the cap by their literal mesh.  On the unit mesh,
\code{unitMeshPath\_quadraticVariation\_le\_one\_div\_mul\_endpointDifference}
gives the checked finite estimate
\[
 \sum_{i<n}\Delta_i x\,\Delta_i v
 \leq \frac1n\bigl(v_n-v_0\bigr)
\]
for every nondecreasing sampled path \(v\).  This is exactly the coordinate
side of the corner estimate needed for \(x\arctan x\); the remaining
arctangent monotonicity certificate is deliberately separate.
The finite ingredients are now combined for every supplied certified
partition.  If its maximum step is at most \(\delta\) and the sampled path
\(v(P_i)\) is nondecreasing, Lean proves the literal endpoint bracket
\[
 b v(b)-a v(a)-\delta\bigl(v(b)-v(a)\bigr)
 \leq
 \sum_i P_i\Delta v_i+\sum_i v_i\Delta P_i
 \leq b v(b)-a v(a).
\]
This is
\code{coordinateIntegrationByParts\_onPartition\_endpoint\_bracket}.
It is the finite, monotone-piece integration-by-parts theorem required by the
\(x\arctan x\) route; calling either strip an integral still requires its
separate effective-FTC certificate.
For the later integral theorem, functions are supplied on explicit increasing
or decreasing pieces and the two decompositions are replaced by a common
rational refinement.  The corner correction is then bounded piece by piece
by their signed variations.  This is a finite interval estimate, not an
appeal to completeness or an implicit choice of a variation decomposition.
The product representation needed to promote the finite identity is kept
separate from the product rule.  On \([0,1]\), let \(X(x)=[x,x]\) be the
exact coordinate box and let \(A(x,n)=[A_{\mathrm{lo}}(x,n),A_{\mathrm{hi}}(x,n)]\) be the
rectangle evaluator for arctangent.  Both factors have certified endpoints
in \([0,1]\).  Hence their literal interval product has the monotone
corner form
\[
  P(x,n)=[xA_{\mathrm{lo}}(x,n),\,xA_{\mathrm{hi}}(x,n)].
\]
More generally, a pointwise product of two nonnegative rational-input
functions is valid when each value carries a positive rational upper bound;
its width is controlled by the two factor widths and those bounds.  The Lean
development instantiates
this construction as the concrete function \(P=xA\) on the unit interval,
and proves that its evaluator reduces to the displayed two-corner box.
This is an evaluator-level representation of \(x\arctan x\).  Its concrete
two-sided product derivative is now checked below.  The remaining work is the
effective FTC/logarithm endpoint certificates required before the resulting
pi formula is claimed.

The next certificate begins with an exact finite identity, rather than with
an assumed product rule.  For rational \(h\ne0\), write
\(D_hU(x)=(U(x+h)-U(x))/h\).  Then
\[
  D_h(UV)(x)=U(x)D_hV(x)+V(x+h)D_hU(x)
\]
and, equivalently,
\[
  D_h(UV)(x)=U(x)D_hV(x)+V(x)D_hU(x)
    +h\,D_hU(x)D_hV(x).
\]
Thus a constructive product-derivative certificate must provide finite
enclosures for the two difference quotients and point values, a rational
continuity budget to replace \(V(x+h)\) by \(V(x)\), and a bound making the
displayed \(h\)-remainder smaller than its allocated error.  The concrete
positive product below now supplies these data for \(x\arctan x\) and
positive steps; no general product rule is inferred from the evaluator alone.

More specifically, if \(U'\) and \(V'\) are the proposed derivatives and
\(h\ge0\), the finite identity gives the rational error allocation
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \left|D_h(UV)(x)-\bigl(U(x)V'(x)+V(x)U'(x)\bigr)\right|
 &\leq |U(x)|\,|D_hV(x)-V'(x)| \\
 &\quad+|V(x)|\,|D_hU(x)-U'(x)|
   +h\,|D_hU(x)|\,|D_hV(x)|.
\end{aligned}
\]
The three summands are respectively the two supplied derivative-error
budgets and the corner-remainder budget.  This finite inequality is the
formal hand-off from the product algebra to the interval-continuity
certificate; it does not assert that any particular elementary function has
already met those budgets.

The interval derivative interface permits signed rational steps.  The same
checked finite calculation therefore has the genuinely two-sided form with
\(\lvert h\rvert\) in the final summand; the displayed \(h\ge0\) formula is
its forward-mesh specialization.  This matters when a derivative certificate
is stated using arbitrary nearby rational inputs rather than only a right
difference quotient.

For an inexact interval evaluator, the evaluation precision in that
certificate must also be allowed to depend on the rational basepoint and
nonzero step.  A stage selected only from the requested output precision has
a fixed positive box width; after division by an arbitrarily smaller
\(\lvert h\rvert\), it cannot certify a quotient error bound.  Thus the
derivative interface uses a rational step-aware schedule
\[
  E(x,h,n)
\]
for the two function evaluations and the derivative evaluation at requested
precision \(n\).  This is finite scheduling data, not a limit operation.
Exact rational functions may take \(E\equiv0\), which is why the affine and
quadratic regression examples retain their simple proofs.  The chart
transport of \cref{prop:arctangent-tangent-chart-increment} is the first
non-exact case that requires this dependence explicitly.

The coordinate factor has now crossed the first of those obligations.  For
an exact rational affine function \(L(x)=mx+c\), the singleton interval
quotient is literally

\[
 \frac{[L(x+h),L(x+h)]-[L(x),L(x)]}{h}=[m,m]
 \qquad(h\ne0).
\]

Thus the unit coordinate \(X(x)=x\) has a checked interval-valued derivative
certificate \(X'=1\), not merely an informal differentiation rule.  Together
with the checked arctangent derivative, this is the first component certificate
used by the concrete forward product rule below.  A reusable two-sided product
closure remains a later theorem.

There is also a first non-affine regression case for the interval definition:
\[
 D_h(x^2)=2x+h.
\]
At precision \(n\), the certificate asks for
\(\lvert h\rvert\) no larger than the rational stage tolerance itself.  The
two singleton quotient and derivative boxes are then near at that precision.
This verifies a real error budget in the definition, rather than only the
zero-error affine case, while still using only rational arithmetic.
\end{theorem}

\[
  P'_+(x)=A(x)+\frac{x}{1+x^2}.
\]
The right-hand side is itself a raw interval evaluator: at stage \(N\) it is
the rectangle box for \(A(x)\) shifted by the exact rational value
\(x/(1+x^2)\).

The proof uses no unproved product rule.  For a positive step \(h\), put
\(Q=D_hA(x)\).  At the same evaluation stage the literal product quotient
has the exact endpoint identity
\[
 D_hP(x)=
 \bigl[A_{\mathrm{hi}}(x)+(x+h)Q_{\mathrm{lo}},\,
       A_{\mathrm{lo}}(x)+(x+h)Q_{\mathrm{hi}}\bigr].
\]

\(h\le1/(72(n+1))\), calls the arctangent derivative at the finer precision
\(m=8(n+1)\), and uses its step-aware rectangle stage.  The checked bounds
\[
  \varepsilon_m\le\varepsilon_n/8,
  \qquad 2h\le\varepsilon_n/4,
  \qquad 0<\frac1{1+x^2}\le1

\section{A constructive integration-by-parts certificate}

The finite rectangle theorem above is not merely motivational.  It fixes the
interface for the first function-level integration-by-parts result, and makes
clear which parts of the usual textbook proof must become finite certificates.
This avoids accidentally obtaining the theorem from an unintroduced general
FTC, a completeness argument, or an unbounded-variation assertion.

\begin{definition}[Integration-by-parts certificate on rational pieces]
\label{def:integration-by-parts-certificate}
\uses{thm:finite-geometric-integration-by-parts,def:definite-integral-identity}
Let \(a=b_0<\cdots<b_r=b\) be a supplied finite list of rational
breakpoints.  On every piece \([b_j,b_{j+1}]\), an
integration-by-parts certificate for raw functions \(U,V\) consists of the
following explicit data.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Certified integral constructions for the two ordinary integrands
  \(U V'\) and \(U' V\), together with endpoint-difference certificates for
  \(U\) and \(V\).  Here \(U'\) and \(V'\) name supplied derivative algorithms;
  they are not values chosen by a hidden limiting operation.
  \item A valid product raw function for \(UV\), with its rational bounds on
  the piece.  The first implementation may use the already checked positive
  bounded product interface; signed products are obtained later by an explicit
  finite sign split, not by changing the meaning of multiplication.
  \item A declared direction for each factor on the piece (increasing or
  decreasing), a common rational-refinement procedure, and rational moduli
  \(\omega_U,\omega_V\) such that every refined cell of mesh at most \(h\)
  has first increments bounded by the corresponding modulus.  The finite
  rectangle correction is then bounded using the signed endpoint variations
  of that particular piece.
  \item A computable schedule which refines until the sum of the product-box,
  quadrature, and corner-rectangle error budgets is below a requested positive
  rational epsilon.
\end{enumerate}
The certificate records its breakpoints and its error schedule as finite
rational data.  It does not quantify over an unspecified ``partition fine
enough'', and it does not require a global Jordan decomposition.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Integration by parts from the certificate: construction target]
\label{thm:integration-by-parts-certificate}
\uses{def:integration-by-parts-certificate}
For an integration-by-parts certificate, the intended theorem is the raw-real
equivalence
\[
 \int_a^b U(x)V'(x)\,\dd x
 \Equiv U(b)V(b)-U(a)V(a)-\int_a^b U'(x)V(x)\,\dd x.
\]
At a common rational refinement \(P\), the only new term in the finite
identity is the sum of corner rectangles.  On an increasing piece it has the
literal bound
\[
 0\leq \sum_{I\in P}\Delta_IU\,\Delta_IV
 \leq \max_{I\in P}\Delta_IU\,\bigl(V(b_{j+1})-V(b_j)\bigr),
\]
when both factors are increasing.  The corresponding decreasing or mixed-sign
formula uses the declared nonnegative signed endpoint variation on that piece.
Thus the refinement schedule makes this term
smaller than its allocated rational error budget.  The finite identities on
the pieces telescope, and the supplied endpoint/product certificates compare
the resulting boxes.  Showing overlap at every requested precision is exactly
the required \code{RealRaw.Equiv} proof.

This is a construction target, not yet a Lean theorem.  Its deliberately
small first scope is bounded functions with finitely many rational monotone
pieces; that includes the positive branch needed below and is already broad
enough for many tabulated engineering kernels.  Extensions to arbitrary
piecewise-continuous functions should be separate theorems with their own
finite regularity certificates.
\end{theorem}

\begin{definition}[Finite arctangent endpoint-order certificate]
\label{def:arctangent-sampled-monotonicity}
\uses{thm:finite-geometric-integration-by-parts}
The integration-by-parts route needs an order fact about the \emph{input} of
arctangent which is distinct from the already used fact that its reciprocal
kernel decreases as the integration variable increases.  For the rectangle
presentation \(A(x)\) of arctangent on the unit branch, the first finite
certificate is
\[
  0\leq x\leq y\leq1
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \operatorname{lo}(A(x,n))\leq\operatorname{hi}(A(y,n))
  \qquad\text{for every stage }n.
\]
This is the box-valued, same-stage form of the statement that \(A\) is
nondecreasing, and is the right statement for a rational-input
\code{FunctionOnInterval}.  It should not be obtained by first assuming the
derivative theorem and then appealing to a classical mean-value theorem.
The endpoint-order portion is now a checked theorem for the rectangle
algorithm and is packaged as a weak nondecreasing
\code{FunctionOnInterval} witness.

For the geometric rectangle algorithm the proof has a literal finite
covering shape.  The stage-\(n\) cells for \([0,x]\) cover that interval;
append the one rational cell \([x,y]\).  Positivity of
\(1/(1+t^2)\) says that this extension cannot decrease the lower rectangle
sum.  The extended cells and the stage-\(n\) cells for \([0,y]\) are two
finite covers of the same interval, so the lower sum of the former is at
most the upper sum of the latter.  Their composite is precisely the displayed
box inequality.  Thus the certificate needs only rational covers and
nonnegative rectangle areas, not a completed real line or a geometric
sector-limit argument.

This endpoint-order lemma is necessary but is intentionally not confused
with the entire corner-error input.  For a particular common partition
\(x_0<\cdots<x_m\), the integration-by-parts construction must additionally
produce compatible rational sample boxes whose widths are charged to the
global error budget, and whose selected comparison path is nondecreasing.
That \emph{monotone sampling lift} is what permits
\(\sum_i\Delta x_i\,\Delta v_i\) to use the finite corner bound.  Merely
knowing that lower and upper endpoints at adjacent samples overlap in the
correct direction does not license treating one arbitrary choice of endpoints
as an exactly nondecreasing rational path.  On a fixed unit mesh the lift is
now checked by the canonical cumulative lower envelope
\[
  v_i=\max_{0\leq j\leq i}\operatorname{lo}(A(x_j,n)).
\]
The weak box order says that every earlier lower endpoint is at most the
upper endpoint of the current box.  Consequently \(v_i\) lies in the
\(i\)-th box, while its definition makes the rational path
nondecreasing.  The exact finite unit-mesh corner estimate therefore applies
to these samples.  This construction deliberately rules out arbitrary
endpoint choices.

There are two independent finite precision knobs.  Given a positive rational
\(\varepsilon\), take
\[
  m=\varepsilon.\mathrm{den}+1,
  \qquad
  r=4(\varepsilon.\mathrm{den}+1).
\]
Here \(m\) is the number of unit-mesh cells and \(r\) is the rectangle
evaluation stage.  The canonical path starts at \(0\), remains at most
\(1\), and therefore has corner correction at most \(1/m\leq\varepsilon\).
Independently, every rectangle box at stage \(r\) has width at most
\(4/(r+1)\leq\varepsilon\), so the selected rational sample is within
\(\varepsilon\) of either endpoint of its own box.  These are checked
finite inequalities, not an appeal to a limiting partition.

The two left-endpoint strip sums are now instantiated with this particular
coordinate path and this particular arctangent sample path.  Write
\[
 S_{m,r}
 =\sum_{i<m}x_i(v_{i+1}-v_i)
   +\sum_{i<m}v_i(x_{i+1}-x_i),
 \qquad
 C_{m,r}=\sum_{i<m}(x_{i+1}-x_i)(v_{i+1}-v_i).
\]
Lean proves the exact finite identity
\[
 S_{m,r}+C_{m,r}=v_m,
\]
because \(x_0=v_0=0\) and \(x_m=1\).  Its already checked nonnegative
corner bound gives the stronger endpoint bracket
\[
 v_m-\frac1m\leq S_{m,r}\leq v_m,
\]
and the explicit choice \(m=\varepsilon.\mathrm{den}+1\) replaces
\(1/m\) by \(\varepsilon\).  Thus the concrete rectangle route now has a
full finite integration-by-parts mesh certificate, including its endpoint
and convergence-rate budget; the two summands are not yet claimed to be
definite integrals.  Promoting them separately still requires the effective
FTC comparison certificates needed by the later integration-by-parts theorem.

There is nevertheless a useful checked \emph{supplementary computation} at
this finite stage.  At stage \(n\), form the point interval at
\(S_{n+1,n}\), enlarge it by \(1/(n+1)\), and intersect the finite prefix of
these direct intervals after widening the \(k\)-th one by the public radius
\(4/(k+1)\).  The endpoint bracket supplies the first enlargement, while the
already verified rectangle arctangent width supplies the second.  Thus the
result is a valid raw representative equivalent to the rectangle computation
of \(\arctan(1)\); its direct candidate has width exactly \(2/(n+1)\).
Multiplying it by four gives a rate-certified regression evaluator for
\(\pi\).  This does \emph{not} promote either strip sum to a definite integral
and does not mark the arctangent--logarithm scoreboard row: its purpose is to
keep the actual finite integration-by-parts algebra executable while the
effective-FTC and canonical-logarithm bridges are developed.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Monotonicity and endpoint anchoring of the positive arctangent product]
\label{prop:coordinate-arctangent-monotonicity}

On the unit branch, let \(A_n(x)=[L_n(x),U_n(x)]\) be the geometric

\[
  P_n(x)=[xL_n(x),xU_n(x)].
\]

\(L_n(x)\le U_n(y)\), while both lower endpoints are nonnegative.  Hence
\[
  xL_n(x)\le yL_n(x)\le yU_n(y).
\]
Lean packages this as a nondecreasing interval-function witness for the

\[
  P_n(0)=[0,0],
  \qquad

\]
Consequently the endpoint-difference raw for this product on \([0,1]\) is a
valid presentation equivalent to the geometric value
\(\arctangeom(1)\).  This is boundary data only: it does not
differentiate the product or identify an integral of the product with that
endpoint difference.  The finite two-stage construction later in this
chapter supplies both the product comparison and its public stabilized
definite-integral packaging.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[A finite Lipschitz certificate for the arctangent kernel]
\label{prop:arctangent-kernel-lipschitz}
\uses{def:one-over-one-plus-square-kernel,def:definite-integral-identity}
\leanok
The existing geometric rectangle construction exploits that
\(1/(1+x^2)\) decreases on the unit interval.  It is also important to
record a direction-free estimate, because the generic finite
Lipschitz--Darboux constructor is the integration interface intended for
regular rational kernels more generally.  For rational \(s,t\in[0,1]\),
the required estimate follows from the exact identity
\[
 \frac{1}{1+s^2}-\frac{1}{1+t^2}
 =\frac{(t-s)(s+t)}{(1+s^2)(1+t^2)}.
\]
Here \(0\le s+t\le2\), while both denominator factors are at least one.
Consequently
\[
 \left|\frac{1}{1+s^2}-\frac{1}{1+t^2}\right|
 \le 2|t-s|.
\]
Every step is finite rational arithmetic: positivity of \(1+x^2\), the
displayed factorization, and multiplication of ordered rational bounds.
It neither asserts an arctangent derivative nor uses a mean-value theorem.

The resulting dyadic boxes use the generic lower and upper Lipschitz
rectangles with \(L=2\), so their exact width is \(4/2^n\) at stage \(n\).
The comparison with the established circle rectangle boxes is also finite.
On their common midpoint-refined mesh, the literal right-endpoint
sum
\[
 \sum_{[p,r]}(r-p)\frac{1}{1+r^2}
\]
is both the generic box's certified interior point and the lower rectangle
sum of the decreasing-kernel construction.  Lean proves that the two stage
boxes contain this same rational computation, and therefore proves their
raw-real overlap directly.  This comparison is useful evidence that the
generic integration layer agrees
with the geometric arctangent integral; it is not a further pi-scoreboard
row, and it does not replace the later derivative/FTC identity
\(\int_0^x(1+t^2)^{-1}\,\dd t=\arctan x\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Finite zero-tangent bracket for arctangent]
\label{prop:arctangent-zero-tangent-bracket}
\uses{def:one-over-one-plus-square-kernel,prop:arctangent-kernel-lipschitz}
\leanok
Let \(A_n(x)=[L_n(x),U_n(x)]\) be a finite geometric rectangle box for
the arctangent integral on \([0,x]\), with \(0\le x\le1\).  The next
derivative-facing certificate is the pair of finite bounds
\[
  \frac{x}{1+x^2}\le L_n(x)\le U_n(x)\le x.
\]
The upper bound is the familiar comparison with a rectangle of height one.
For the lower bound, every right-endpoint kernel value on an ordered cover of
\([0,x]\) is at least \(1/(1+x^2)\); summing the cell lengths gives the
displayed left inequality.  Thus the whole finite box lies in
\[
  [\,x-x^3,\;x\,],
\]
because \(x-x/(1+x^2)\le x^3\).

At \(x=0\) the rectangle box is exactly \([0,0]\).  Dividing the preceding
enclosure by a positive rational \(h\le1\) produces the literal finite
difference-quotient bracket
\[
  1-h^2\le \frac{A_n(h)-A_n(0)}{h}\le1.
\]
Its width is \(h^2\), independent of a completed-real limit.  This is the
basepoint calculation needed to identify \(\arctan'(0)=1\).  The next
one-sided certificate packages this exact statement.  It is not yet a global
\code{HasDerivativeOnInterval} certificate: transporting the bracket to a
nonzero rational basepoint requires the rational tangent-addition chart and
compatible evaluation schedules.  That later transport is exactly where the
project must account for the denominator \(1+x(x+h)\), rather than silently
invoking the mean-value theorem.
\end{proposition}

\begin{definition}[Forward finite-difference derivative certificate]
\label{def:forward-finite-difference-derivative}
\uses{def:one-over-one-plus-square-kernel}
\leanok
For an interval-valued function \(F\), a rational basepoint \(x\), and a
proposed derivative raw real \(d\), a forward certificate supplies a
step-radius schedule \(R(n)\) and an evaluation schedule \(E(h,n)\).  For
every positive rational step \(h\) that remains in the stated domain and
satisfies
\[
  0<h\le\frac1{R(n)},
\]
the literal quotient box
\[
 \frac{F_{E(h,n)}(x+h)-F_{E(h,n)}(x)}h
\]
is within the requested stage-\(n\) rational tolerance of the stage-\(E(h,n)\)
box for \(d\).  Both quotient and derivative boxes are required to have that
tolerance as a width bound.

This is a deliberately one-sided, pointwise interface.  It records a finite
right-derivative estimate without implying a two-sided derivative, continuity,
or any fundamental-theorem-of-calculus rule.  Its purpose is to make the
zero-basepoint arctangent calculation usable while leaving the separate
transport and endpoint work visible.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Tangent-chart coordinate for derivative transport]
\label{prop:arctangent-tangent-chart-increment}

\leanok

\[

\]
This is the chart increment that represents the ordinary endpoint \(x+h\):
whenever the displayed denominators are positive, the rational
tangent-addition chart satisfies
\[
  \frac{x+\tau(x,h)}{1-x\tau(x,h)}=x+h,
  \qquad
  \frac{\tau(x,h)}h=\frac1{1+x(x+h)}.
\]

finite chart-addition theorem gives
\[
  \arctangeom(x)+\arctangeom(\tau(x,h))
  =\arctangeom(x+h).
\]
This is an equality of certified rational interval reals, obtained from the
same finite rectangle transport used for tangent addition; it is not a
continuity or mean-value argument.  Lean also records the equivalent
endpoint-difference form
\[
 \arctangeom(x+h)-\arctangeom(x)
 =\arctangeom(\tau(x,h)).
\]

The scale conversion is controlled directly by rational inequalities.  On
\(0\le x\le1\), \(0<h\), and \(x+h\le1\),
\[
  0\le
  \frac1{1+x^2}-\frac1{1+x(x+h)}
  =\frac{xh}{(1+x^2)(1+x(x+h))}
  \le h.
\]
In particular \(0<\tau(x,h)\le h\le1\).  Combined with the finite
tangent box, this algebra is now part of the checked full-unit forward
derivative certificate for the rectangle arctangent.  The same formulas are
retained for the geometric transport; a full \code{HasDerivativeOnInterval}
certificate still requires a compatible evaluation schedule for that
equivalence and a separate two-sided endpoint treatment.
\end{proposition}

\section{Projective and Improper Test Integrals}

The first useful test, simpler than the Gaussian integral but still genuinely
projective, is
\[
  \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\dd x}{x^4+a x^2+1}.
\]
For \(a>-2\), the classical value is
\[
  \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\dd x}{x^4+a x^2+1}
    =\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{a+2}}.
\]
Thus when \(a=b^2-2\) with rational \(b>0\), the integral should give the
rational pi multiple \(\pi/b\).  The especially clean benchmark is \(a=-1\):
\[
  \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\dd x}{x^4-x^2+1}=\pi.
\]

\begin{definition}[Reciprocal-tail compactification]

 \frac{(y^2-x^2)\bigl(x^2+y^2+x^2y^2-2\bigr)}
 {(x^4-x^2+1)(y^4-y^2+1)},
 \qquad f(x)=\frac{1+x^2}{x^4-x^2+1},
\]
is formalized as well.  On the compact interval its factors now give the
checked rational bound
\[
  |f(x)-f(y)|\le 8|x-y|\qquad(-1\le x,y\le1).
\]
Consequently the total compact-density evaluator has a literal rational
\(\varepsilon\)-\(\delta\) certificate on \([-1,1]\), with
\(\delta=\varepsilon/8\).  More strongly, it now has the checked
interval-regular evaluator required by the general calculus interfaces: for
an input interval \(I\), evaluate \(f\) at its rational midpoint \(m\) and
return
\[
  [f(m)-8\operatorname{width}(I),\ f(m)+8\operatorname{width}(I)].
\]
This interval contains every rational point-value over \(I\), has width
\(16\operatorname{width}(I)\), and therefore meets output precision
\(1/n\) when the input width is at most \(1/(16n)\).  Thus the density is
now a \code{ContinuousFunctionOnInterval}, with no appeal to topology or
completed reals.  The generic checked bridge from interval regularity to
rational \(\varepsilon\)-\(\delta\) continuity also recovers the stated
continuity certificate.  The affine compact integral construction itself is
now concrete:
the affine chart \(x=2t-1\) turns the compact density into
\[
  g(t)=2\frac{1+(2t-1)^2}{(2t-1)^4-(2t-1)^2+1},\qquad 0\le t\le1.
\]
Lean proves directly that \(g\) is \(32\)-Lipschitz on rational unit inputs
and names its exact left sums on the ordinary \(2^n\)-cell dyadic mesh.  On
each rational cell \([p,r]\), it takes the left value \(g(p)\) and brackets
the cell contribution using the proved oscillation bound
\[
 (r-p)\bigl(g(p)-32(r-p)\bigr)
 \ \le\ \text{cell value}\ \le\
 (r-p)\bigl(g(p)+32(r-p)\bigr).
\]
The density-specific estimate is passed to the generic finite theorem
\code{Integral.lipschitzCells\_refine}, which proves that midpoint splitting
raises the lower sum and lowers the upper sum.  The resulting raw boxes are
nested and have exact width
\(64/2^n\), hence shrink to zero using rational arithmetic alone.  This is a
genuine domain-aware integral construction for the affine compact density,
rather than an unconstrained raw-real witness.  Since Lean also proves
\(g(t)=2f(2t-1)\) by rational algebra, the identical computation now packages
an actual \code{ConstructionFor} for the compact density \(f\) on
\([-1,1]\).  The finite projective/Cauchy comparison is now also checked:
the literal compact dyadic boxes and scheduled full-line Cauchy boxes lie in
an explicit shrinking rational envelope.  This proves the concrete compact
integral equivalent to the area-loop \(\pi\), without an improper-integral
limit or a completeness principle.
Thus the full finite pullback identity for \(a=-1\) is now available without
extra denominator hypotheses, under only \(x\ne0\).  Lean also identifies
\[
  \frac{1}{u^2+1}
\]
with the existing arctangent kernel \(\code{ArctanGeometry.integralKernel}(u)\),
so the pi-case quartic test now lands directly in the same rational kernel used
by the verified rectangle arctangent construction.
\end{theorem}

\section{Trigonometric Functions as Integral Identities}

\begin{definition}[Geometric sine and cosine on an interval]

Once the geometric point algorithm is available on a rational interval, its

functions.  This is only a domain wrapper around the geometric algorithms; it
does not introduce a new definition of sine or cosine.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Geometric trigonometric integral identities]
\label{def:geometric-trig-integral-identities}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.IntegralIdentities.GeometricTrigIntegralIdentities}
\uses{def:geometric-trig-on-interval,def:definite-integral-identity}
\leanok
The integral identity target for the geometric trigonometric functions on
\([a,b]\) is the pair of definite integral statements
\[
  \int_a^b \cos(t)\dd t
  \quad\hbox{equals}\quad
  \sin(b)-\sin(a),
\]
\[
  \int_a^b \sin(t)\dd t
  \quad\hbox{equals}\quad
  -\cos(b)+\cos(a).
\]
In Lean this is a structure, not yet an existence theorem: it records the
precise derivative, integral-construction, and endpoint-equivalence data that
must be supplied by the later geometric proof.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Inverse elementary integral identities]
\label{def:inverse-elementary-integral-identities}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.IntegralIdentities.InverseElementaryIntegralIdentities}
\uses{def:definite-integral-identity}
\leanok
The inverse trigonometric and logarithmic comparison targets are:
\[
  \int_0^x \frac{\dd t}{1+t^2}
  \quad\hbox{equals the chosen arctangent branch at }x,
\]
\[
  \int_0^x \frac{\dd t}{\sqrt{1-t^2}}
  \quad\hbox{equals the chosen arcsine branch at }x,
\]
and
\[
  \int_1^x \frac{\dd t}{t}
  \quad\hbox{equals the chosen logarithm branch at }x.
\]
These are branch-local statements.  They are not imported into the geometric
definition of trigonometric functions; they are used to prove that different
constructions agree.
\end{definition}

\section{Abelian Integrals Related to Trigonometry}

\begin{definition}[Algebraic differentials and Abelian integral inverses]
\label{def:abelian-trig-integral-representations}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.AbelianIntegral.DifferentialRaw}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.AbelianIntegral.Raw}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.AbelianIntegral.InverseRepresentation}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.IntegralIdentities.AbelianTrigIntegralRepresentations}
\uses{def:inverse-elementary-integral-identities}
\leanok
An Abelian integral is represented by an algebraic branch, an algebraic
differential, a path, and a certified Riemann-sum algorithm.  The elementary
trigonometric comparison cases include:
\[
  \operatorname{arctan}(x)
  \quad\hbox{from}\quad
  \int_0^x \frac{\dd t}{1+t^2},
\]
\[
  \operatorname{arcsin}(x)
  \quad\hbox{from}\quad
  \int_0^x \frac{\dd t}{w}
  \quad\hbox{on the curve } w^2=1-t^2.
\]
The same infrastructure also records the next Abelian family beyond circular
trigonometry, for example elliptic integrals such as
\[
  \int_0^x
    \frac{\dd t}{\sqrt{(1-t^2)(1-k^2t^2)}}.
\]
This is the natural home for the comparison between circular functions,
inverse trigonometric functions, logarithmic integrals, and later elliptic
examples such as ellipse circumference.
\end{definition}

\section{Proof Order}

The intended formalization order is:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item prove the geometric trigonometric algorithms valid and prove the
  exact identities from Chapter~\ref{ch:rational-circle-trigonometry};
  \item prove the derivative certificates for geometric sine and cosine from
  the circle construction;
  \item feed those certificates into the definite-integral identity package;
  \item prove arctangent, arcsine, and logarithm integral identities on their
  chosen branches;
  \item prove the Abelian inverse representations agree with the corresponding
  geometric or power-series algorithms.
\end{enumerate}

This keeps the definitions honest: geometry first, then integrals as
comparison theorems.
