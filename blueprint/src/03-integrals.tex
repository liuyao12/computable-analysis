\chapter{Integrals and the Fundamental Theorem}\label{ch:integrals}

The scoped Lean entry point for this chapter is
\texttt{ComputableAnalysis.IntegralFoundation}.  It exposes the finite
rectangle, piecewise, polynomial, arctangent, Stieltjes, and effective-FTC
certificates.  Routine variants are transported from these representatives;
the blueprint does not require a separate formalization for every similar
function.

The motivating integral is circle area: crude rectangles produce nested
rational bounds for the area under the arctangent kernel. An integral is a
program returning such bounds. Lebesgue measure is outside scope.

This chapter is where the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus belongs. It is the
first general bridge from local change to accumulated area; later chapters
only instantiate and reuse its certificate interface. We state the contract
here immediately after the first area algorithm. The next chapter packages
the derivative-bound, convexity, monotonicity, and tail certificates that
make the contract effective.

The order is intentional:

\begin{verbatim}
circle/arctan example -> rectangle integral -> FTC contract
                      -> effective certificates -> power series
                      -> exp/log -> linear ODEs
\end{verbatim}

FTC is introduced immediately after the first computable integral, then used
as the common interface for the remaining chapters.

\section{FTC as the organizing contract}
\label{sec:integrals-ftc-contract}

Once the rectangle algorithm has produced a certified area, the next question
is not another special function.  It is the general finite statement that
turns a derivative computation into an endpoint difference:

\begin{verbatim}
derivative bounds -> cell rectangles -> finite telescoping sum
                  -> endpoint difference within the same rational box
\end{verbatim}

This is the computable form of the FTC.  The theorem is introduced at this
point because it explains why integration is useful; its hypotheses are then
implemented by Lipschitz, monotone, convex, power-series, and turning-point
certificates.  Chapter~\ref{ch:effective-calculus} contains that reusable
implementation layer.  Chapters~\ref{ch:infinite-series}--\ref{ch:differential-equations}
consume the contract rather than reopening the foundations.

The first algebraic law is already finite: the rectangle fold for a pointwise
sum is the interval sum of the two rectangle folds.  No limiting argument is
needed here; convergence and representation transport are separate
certificates.

Left-endpoint rectangles are the default implementation, but the same finite
algebra applies to right-endpoint rectangles.  The convention is therefore a
choice of sample schedule, not a change of foundation.

The trapezoid schedule is the finite midpoint of those two presentations:
average the two endpoint boxes cell by cell.  Its identity with half the sum
of the left and right folds is proved before any limiting argument.

\begin{verbatim}
left  := fold (mesh * value at x_k)
right := fold (mesh * value at x_(k+1))
trap  := half (left + right)

left.width <= eps and right.width <= eps -> trap.width <= eps
sample overlap at both endpoints -> trap overlap
\end{verbatim}

These are identities between finite rational computations.  A theorem that
the three schedules converge to the same abstract integral is admitted only
after a separate nesting, shrinking, and evaluator-equivalence certificate.

\section{Rectangle algorithm}

The question is not how to choose the best subdivision. Every certified
subdivision is a computation, and the theory relates all of them.

For each cell, certify monotonicity or isolate finitely many turns.  A turn is
represented by a nested rational bracket; the left, middle, and right boxes
are then assembled by finite interval addition.  The assembled width is
proved to shrink.  Only the final comparison with the intended integral is
function-specific, and is supplied as an explicit completion certificate.
\begin{verbatim}
mesh -> cell classification -> lower/upper rectangles -> overlap
\end{verbatim}

\section{Monotone pieces and finitely many turns}

The usable generalization is piecewise, not an unrestricted measurable
function.  A monotone cell uses its endpoint range as the Darboux bracket;
finitely many turning points split a function into such cells.  The same
rectangle computation then adds the cell brackets.

\begin{verbatim}
finite breakpoints -> monotone cells -> endpoint ranges
                  -> add cell brackets -> one integral raw
\end{verbatim}

The Lean layer separates the geometric classification from the computation:
the monotone schedule supplies the dyadic ranges, while a piecewise-monotone
certificate supplies the finite assembly.  This is the route used for
functions such as \(\sin(\pi x)\): classify each cell as increasing,
decreasing, or containing one certified turn, then refine only the local
boxes needed by that classification.

The atomic refinement inequality is explicit.  With coordinatewise endpoint
order, the range rectangle on $[a,c]$ contains the sum of the two range
rectangles on $[a,b]$ and $[b,c]$:

\begin{verbatim}
coarse [a,c] >= refined [a,b] + refined [b,c]
\end{verbatim}

This is the finite geometric step used to assemble dyadic nesting; no
supremum or completed real is introduced.

\lean{ComputableAnalysis.endpointOrderedNondecreasing_splitScaledEndpointRange_contains}

There is one essential distinction.  Piecewise monotonicity describes the
integrand and makes its area computable; it does not by itself say that the
area equals the endpoint difference of that same function.  The FTC
certificate supplies a (possibly separately represented) primitive.  For
example, \(\lvert x\rvert\) on \([-1,1]\) is the first two-piece area
example, while \(x\lvert x\rvert/2\) is the corresponding primitive for an
endpoint identity.  This is why the general FTC interface is stated after
the rectangle assembly, and why each special function must provide its own
primitive/evaluator equivalence.

The public primitive-specific interface now makes this distinction explicit:
it transports the cell integrals through a primitive endpoint list and then
uses the same finite telescope as the single-function interface.  The
absolute-value construction now supplies the first completed finite-turn FTC
case: the two cell areas add to (1), and the primitive
\(P(x)=x\lvert x\rvert/2\) transports that sum to \(P(1)-P(-1)=1\).

The dyadic implementation uses the normalized angle \(2x=k/2^n\).  Its
finite stage is an explicit indexed rectangle sum:

\begin{verbatim}
for k in range(2^n):
    (sine, cosine) = nested_radical_table(n, k)
    add (1/2^(n+1)) * sine to the interval sum
\end{verbatim}

The finite sum is packaged as a raw candidate. The remaining certificate
proves that the half-angle boxes enclose the intended values and that the
resulting sums are nested and shrink. Only after that certificate is supplied
does the raw candidate become a computable integral.

The implementation keeps mesh level and evaluation precision separate:
each stage rebuilds its nested-radical paths at the current precision.  This
prevents an old, coarse parent box from being reused forever at refined
stages.

Each square-root box is then clipped to its geometric range \([0,1]\).  The
exact root remains enclosed, while the clipping makes the range invariant
inductive: lower-half cosine boxes lie in \([0,1]\), reflected boxes in
\([-1,0]\), and sine boxes in \([0,1]\). Lean proves this finite geometric
invariant directly.

\section{The first general integrability theorem}

The project does not claim that every continuous function is integrable by
default. It proves the stronger-for-computation statement for an explicit
rational Lipschitz certificate:

\begin{verbatim}
f : rational evaluator on [0,1]
L : certified Lipschitz bound
        |
        v
dyadic cells -> lower/upper Lipschitz rectangles
                 -> nested raw integral
                 -> width <= 2*L/2^stage
\end{verbatim}

This is the foundation's replacement for a completeness-based existence
theorem. The rate is visible, executable, and can be transported to a
special-function evaluator. A merely effective modulus of continuity can be
added later by supplying a corresponding cell-width schedule; it is not
silently identified with the Lipschitz case.

The general interval-regular layer now exposes the finite step that comes
before a raw integral.  Each cell is assigned the common image enclosure
provided by `IntervalRegularOn`, and a uniform partition gives the explicit
bound

\begin{verbatim}
width(sum of cell boxes) <= (b-a)/(requested precision + 1).
\end{verbatim}

This works without monotonicity.  It is intentionally only a finite
quadrature candidate: cross-stage nesting still has to be supplied before it
is promoted to a `realRaw` integral.

The first reusable part of that coherence proof is now factored out: if the
interval images nest at later evaluator stages, the weighted Darboux sum nests
on every fixed partition.  Refinement across changing partitions remains an
explicit additional certificate.

routine changes of formula do not require another induction.

\end{verbatim}

does not claim that the assembled object is an infinite integral; it only
exposes the finite budget that a later shrinking schedule must consume.

\section{Functions that turn finitely many times}

The next usable class is not “all continuous functions.” It is a function
whose interval is split into finitely many monotone pieces, with each
non-rational turning point represented by a shrinking rational bracket. At
stage \(n\), integrate the certified left and right monotone pieces and put a
range enclosure over the unresolved middle bracket:

\begin{verbatim}
turn bracket -> left monotone box + middle range box + right monotone box
             -> finite rational sum -> shrinking integral candidate
\end{verbatim}

The middle contribution needs only a bound on the function range. Its width
is therefore bounded by the range bound times the turn-bracket width; no value at
the turn is required. This is the constructive form of the practical rule
used for \(\sin(\pi x)/x\): classify each cell as increasing, decreasing, or
containing one of finitely many certified turns, and refine only the latter.
For one turn, the three finite budgets are independent: left piece, unresolved
middle bracket, and right piece.  Their widths add, so computation can be
allocated according to the difficulty of each part.

Darboux stage is nested inside a coarser one. An automatic schedule must add
that stronger endpoint-order certificate (or use a common validated anchor);
the missing proof is mathematical data, not a tactic or completeness axiom.

\section{The central bridge: an effective FTC}
\label{thm:convex-ftc}
\label{thm:effective-derivative-bound-ftc}

The historical core of calculus is the converse relation between local change
and accumulated area. Here the FTC is the chapter's central theorem: it is
proved from finite comparisons, not imported from completeness.

\begin{verbatim}
derivative bounds -> lower/upper rectangles -> endpoint difference -> overlap
\end{verbatim}

On each cell, a certified derivative bound controls the difference between
the rectangle sum and the primitive's endpoint increment.  For monotone or
convex/concave derivatives, neighboring secants provide the needed bounds;
finite turning-point certificates extend the construction piecewise.  The
result is an effective FTC: every requested output precision comes with a
finite partition and a rational error certificate.

The public contract is general:

\begin{verbatim}
candidate derivative -> cell bounds -> finite sum -> endpoint overlap
                                         -> shrinking raw integral
\end{verbatim}

The certificate does not prescribe how derivative bounds are obtained.
Monotonicity, convexity, power-series tails, and formula-specific interval
arithmetic all implement the same interface. Thus the theorem is reusable
without claiming that every function is integrable.
Its raw integral exposes an explicit denominator-based precision witness:
requested tolerance becomes a finite stage, rather than an existential appeal
to completeness.

The formal spine is now exposed as four layers:

\begin{verbatim}

EffectiveDerivativeBoundFTC  -> local derivative bounds -> EffectiveFTC

\section{Riemann--Stieltjes viewpoint}

Uneven samples are finite Riemann--Stieltjes sums: a monotone chart supplies
the increments of the integrator, while the function supplies the sampled
values.  For the sine example, use
\(x(u)=2\arctan(u)/\pi\).  Equal subdivisions in \(u\) therefore produce
unequal subdivisions in \(x\), computing
\(\int_0^{1/2}\sin(\pi x)\,dx\) through Stieltjes sums.

\begin{verbatim}
uniform u-mesh -> inverse-chart x-samples -> Stieltjes rectangles -> overlap
                         |
                         +-- substitution theorem (when needed)
\end{verbatim}

\section{Complex polygonal integrals}

The same finite viewpoint applies to complex-valued functions. A path is a
finite list of rational-complex vertices; integration is a finite sum over its
segments:

\begin{verbatim}
vertices -> segment increments -> complex differential sum
         -> endpoint primitive difference
closed path -> exact displacement = 0
\end{verbatim}

Constant and polynomial differentials are the first representatives. Their
closed-path exactness is an algebraic identity over rational complex boxes,
not a theorem about a completed complex plane. The polygonal integral itself
is already a `ComplexRaw` computation; a later analytic path theorem may add
uniform refinement and overlap certificates for a larger class of functions.

As a regression case, the left sum for a constant integrand on one rational
segment is exactly (c(b-a)) at every positive subdivision count. The finite
fold identity makes this endpoint law explicit before any path limit is taken.
The same identity assembles over any finite polygonal path and reduces a closed
path to zero by endpoint cancellation.

\section{Tails and products}

Compactify the domain, bound the tail, and combine finite bounds. Multiple
integrals are iterated finite enclosures; Gaussian and $n$-ball formulas are
the main examples.
\begin{verbatim}
finite boxes -> product bounds -> tail certificate -> nested enclosure
\end{verbatim}

The two-dimensional product layer is literal finite algebra: sampled
rectangle values are folded over two rational lists, and separable products
factor into the product of their one-dimensional folds. The same construction
is now exposed for an arbitrary finite list of coordinate axes; the Gaussian
wrapper generates one Taylor evaluator per axis, so dimension changes do not
change the proof pattern. Positivity is checked by the same finite induction.
The Gaussian layer
then integrates even exponential Taylor prefixes on a bounded interval and
keeps the improper tail as a separate finite upper-bound ladder. The
$n$-ball layer records the familiar recurrence as a rational coefficient
model, with the chosen `RealRaw` approximation to $pi$ supplied later.
None of these statements introduces Lebesgue measure or an attained infinite
domain.
When all axes use the same rule, the finite nested sum is the corresponding
one-axis sum raised to the number of axes. This is the exact finite pattern
used by separable Gaussian prefixes.
