\section{Polynomial roots as computable objects}

\begin{definition}[Rational-complex polynomial]\label{def:qcomplex-poly}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.CPoly.Coeffs}
\uses{def:complex-raw}
A rational-complex polynomial is a finite list
\[
  (a_0,\ldots,a_d),\qquad a_j\in\Qi,
\]
interpreted as
\[
  p(z)=a_0+a_1z+\cdots+a_dz^d.
\]
Positive degree means that some coefficient with index \(d\ge 1\) is
nonzero.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Computable root]\label{def:computable-root}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.IsComputableRoot}
\uses{def:qcomplex-poly,def:complex-raw,def:raw-equiv}
Let \(p\in\Qi[z]\).  A computable root of \(p\) is a complex number \(z\)
such that the interval evaluation of \(p(z)\) is equivalent to zero:
\[
  p(z)\Equiv 0.
\]
\end{definition}

two roots are explicit finite witnesses. This is an algebraic computability
certificate, not a claim of an arbitrary-degree fundamental theorem.

polynomial.  The positive search theorem

`factorizedQuarticPolynomial_root_search_some_computable`; these are
the executable finite cores used for benchmark items 37 and 46.

algebraic witness for every member of the supplied factor list.

For a rational degree-one polynomial, evaluation splits into its value at the
origin plus the linear coefficient times the input.  If the polynomial has a
rational root (r), it factors exactly as (a(x-r)).  The corresponding
quadratic factorization is also checked.  These are the finite rational base
cases of benchmark item 89.  The recursive synthetic-division theorem then
provides the corresponding linear factor/remainder statement for arbitrary
finite rational coefficient lists.  Division by higher-degree factors remains
future algebra infrastructure.  The cubic identity also provides the
rational-root reduction step for benchmark item 37, not a general cubic
solution formula.  The worked factorization and root certificate above give
one explicit rational cubic solution within that boundary.  The quartic
factorization and roots are the project's checked worked-example core for
benchmark item 46; they do not claim a general quartic formula.
For arbitrary finite rational-complex coefficient lists, the new
`FiniteFTABoundary` module performs one synthetic deflation step from a
supplied exact root.  It computes the quotient and remainder, proves the
Horner remainder identity, and packages the zero-remainder factorization.
This advances the finite root-peeling interface toward FTA while leaving root
existence and algebraic closure as separate obligations.
The coefficient sign-change counter and its zero-free quadratic example record
the finite input data for benchmark item 100; the Descartes root-counting rule
itself remains optional future work.  Zero coefficients are explicitly removed
before the second example is counted, matching the usual sign-variation input
convention.  The quadratic sign-pattern theorem generalizes this finite count
to all positive--negative--positive rational coefficient triples.
The discriminant certificate adds the corresponding executable root test:
every rational quadratic root produces a nonnegative rational discriminant,
while a negative discriminant excludes all rational roots.  This is a finite
rational strengthening of the item-100 data, without asserting the full
real-root-counting form of Descartes' rule.
The degree-two certificate
`quadratic_three_distinct_roots_impossible` also rules out three pairwise
distinct rational roots by two finite difference factorizations.  In the
positive--negative--positive pattern this supplies the exact quadratic
“at most two” boundary behind the sign-variation count.
The general finite lemma
`signChangeCountIgnoringZeros_zero_of_nonneg_coeffs` proves that a list of
nonnegative coefficients has zero sign variation after zero filtering.
The sign-symmetric lemma
`signChangeCountIgnoringZeros_zero_of_nonpos_coeffs` gives the same result for
nonpositive coefficients.
The nonnegative-coefficient evaluation lemma supplies the corresponding
zero-sign-variation root-exclusion base case on the nonnegative ray.
The nonpositive-coefficient dual is checked as well.
With a positive input and one positive coefficient, the nonnegative case is
strict, giving the corresponding positive-ray root exclusion.
The packaged nonzero conclusion is available for direct root-search use.
The strict negative dual completes the opposite coefficient-sign orientation.
The combined `one_positive_variation_certificate` packages the one sign
change and the resulting uniqueness of any positive rational root in a single
finite witness.  This is the reusable certificate-level Descartes boundary;
the unrestricted real-root counting theorem remains deferred.
\end{theorem}

\section{Two constructive routes}

\begin{proposition}[Algebraic route]\label{prop:fta-algebraic-route}
\uses{conj:algebraic-fta}
It is enough to build an algebraic closure of \(\Qi\) with computable
embeddings into complex interval boxes.  The finite proof obligations are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item factor a nonconstant polynomial into irreducible factors over \(\Qi\);
  \item adjoin one formal root of an irreducible factor by a quotient algebra;
  \item isolate a complex embedding of that root by rational boxes;
  \item prove that interval evaluation overlaps zero.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Analytic search route]\label{prop:fta-analytic-route}
\uses{conj:computable-fta,def:qbox}
An alternative proof avoids abstract algebraic closure.  For a positive-degree
polynomial \(p\), choose a rational square \(B\subset\C\) so large that
\[
  |a_d z^d| >
  |a_{d-1}z^{d-1}+\cdots+a_0|
  \qquad (z\in\partial B).
\]
Then the winding number of \(p(\partial B)\) around zero is \(d\).  Subdivide
\(B\) rationally, using interval arithmetic to discard boxes whose image
does not meet zero and to keep a box whose root count is positive.  A nested
sequence of such boxes is a computable root.
\end{proposition}

\begin{theorem}[Finite complex-box polynomial soundness]
\label{thm:finite-box-polynomial-soundness}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.QBox.evalPoly}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.QBox.evalPoly_point}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.QBox.evalPoly_contains}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.QBox.evalPoly_no_root_of_not_overlaps_zero}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.QBox.FiniteRootExclusionCertificate}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.QBox.FiniteRootExclusionCertificate.no_root_in_domain}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.QBox.OneSurvivorCertificate}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.QBox.OneSurvivorCertificate.root_mem_survivor}
\leanok
The Horner evaluator on rational complex boxes is sound: whenever a rational
point lies in the input box, the corresponding polynomial value lies in the
output box.  This is the finite interval-arithmetic certificate used by the
analytic search route to discard boxes whose image misses zero.  It is a
containment theorem for finite rational data; its companion no-root theorem
justifies discarding a box whose output does not overlap zero.  Neither result
is the global fundamental theorem of algebra.  The finite covering structure
packages the same discard step over a supplied rational domain: a finite cover
whose images all miss zero certifies that the domain has no root.
The one-survivor certificate gives the corresponding positive search step:
if all discarded children miss zero, any root in the parent must lie in the
retained child.
\end{theorem}

\lean{ComputableAnalysis.FiniteFTASubdivision.survivingChildren_ordered}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.FiniteFTASubdivision.root_mem_survivingChildren}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.FiniteFTASubdivision.survivingSubdivide}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.FiniteFTASubdivision.root_mem_survivingSubdivide}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.FiniteFTASubdivision.survivingSubdivide_nonempty_of_root}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.FiniteFTASubdivision.survivingSubdivide_nestedIn_parent}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.FiniteFTASubdivision.survivingSubdivide_ordered}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.FiniteFTASubdivision.survivingSubdivide_width_height_exact}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.FiniteFTASubdivision.dyadicSubdivide}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.FiniteFTASubdivision.dyadicSubdivide_nonempty}
\lean{ComputableAnalysis.FiniteFTASubdivision.dyadicSubdivide_nestedIn_parent}

\leanok
An ordered rational complex box can be split at its coordinatewise midpoint
into four closed rational quadrants.  The finite construction proves that the
children are ordered, nested in the parent, and cover every point of the
parent.  Combined with the polynomial-image exclusion certificate, this is
the geometric subdivision step for the constructive FTA search route.
The executable filter `survivingChildren` retains precisely the children whose
polynomial-image boxes overlap zero, and `root_mem_survivingChildren` proves
that a root in the parent cannot be discarded by this finite filter.
The retained children remain ordered, so the recursive filter can continue
to use the same box semantics at every stage.
The recursive `survivingSubdivide` schedule applies this filter at every
finite depth, and `root_mem_survivingSubdivide` proves that a supplied root
remains in at least one retained box at each such depth.
Consequently, a supplied root also gives nonemptiness of the survivor list;
the survivor schedule preserves orderedness and nesting in the original
parent at every finite depth.
The filtered schedule inherits the exact (2^{-n}) width and height law, so
pruning by polynomial-image overlap does not weaken the finite precision
budget of the surviving boxes.
The recursive `dyadicSubdivide` schedule is nonempty at every finite depth,
and every generated box remains nested in the original ordered parent.  This
is the finite potential-infinity scaffold for repeated root isolation.  The
exact width theorem gives the sharper precision law: every depth-(n) box has
width and height equal to the corresponding parent dimension divided by
(2^n).
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}
The algebraic route is closer to pre-completeness algebra.  The analytic
route is closer to the usual complex-analytic proof of FTA, but it must be
made finite by replacing open-cover and compactness arguments with explicit
box bounds and winding-number computations.
\end{remark}
