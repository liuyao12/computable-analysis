Definition-complete means a valid raw real; registry-complete means that the
corresponding raw presentation has a formal chain of \code{RealRaw.Equiv}
theorems to the preferred area presentation.  The table is a compact
release-style coverage suite, not a numerator for a calculus-completeness
percentage.  It deliberately names natural theorem families rather than
presenting a catalogue of rearranged equations for pi.  The checked registry
corollaries are formalized by \code{PiProofs.PiCoverageBridge} and its
\code{equivalent} theorem.

The suite asks whether a construction useful elsewhere in calculus agrees
with an independently constructed value of the same constant.  Thus a row is
evidence for a capability gate, rather than a claim that five successful pi
proofs make five equal-sized contributions to scientific or engineering
calculus.  In particular, a continuous Peano--Baker theorem and a proof that
the selected exponential satisfies \(e^x{}'=e^x\) are major advances even
though neither creates a new pi row.

The primary \code{PiProofs.PiPresentation} registry retains supplementary
checked evaluators as regression implementations: polygon area, the two
perimeter normalizations (available as \code{pi.circumferenceStabilized} and
\code{pi.circumferenceReboxed}), Nilakantha, and the single Machin power-series
formula.  The Machin runtime has its own certified bound
\(w_n\le20/2^n\), recorded by \code{PiProofs.piMachinRate}; it remains a
series-regression implementation rather than another coverage row.  Its direct cross-fan view is \code{pi.circumference} (also
\code{pi.circumferenceFan}).  The abstract \code{piCertified} handle additionally stores the
separately named \code{pi.curvatureFan} and
\code{pi.integrationByPartsMesh}, and \code{pi.triangleLogSeries}
representatives.  They remain useful
executable comparisons, but are not additional readiness cells: the former
sharpens finite geometry, while the latter two are variants of the checked
finite integration-by-parts bridge.  The primary named view
\code{pi.integrationByParts} is the reciprocal-log formula itself; its rate
\(w_n\le52/2^n\) is certified by
\code{Logarithm.piTriangleLogReciprocalIntegralRate}.  None of these facts
yet transports the logarithm to the canonical inverse-exponential
presentation.

The direct \code{piCircumference} evaluator remains a geometric diagnostic:
its unresolved lower-endpoint refinement is a local square-root-enclosure
problem, not a missing calculus gate.  Lean now reduces it to
\code{AdjacentChordCurvatureMarginCoversFineWidths}: an explicit rational
margin comparing the two fine curvature certificates, their bisection widths,
and the coarse squared chord.  The reduction from that margin to the original
endpoint refinement is checked; the universal margin inequality remains open.
The checked finite integration-by-parts row is deliberately narrower than the
future canonical formulation.  Its exact finite acceptance contract is stated in
\cref{def:integration-by-parts-certificate,thm:integration-by-parts-certificate}:
every monotone piece must provide its product box, common refinement,
corner-rectangle bound, and rational error schedule.  The concrete target is
then isolated in \cref{cor:arctangent-logarithm-pi-route}.  The two-sided product derivative
of the actual \(x\arctan x\) evaluator and its supplied finite FTC comparison
are checked; general monotone-piece composition remains the calculus gate.
The canonical refinement is deliberately the long elementary-function route:
its \(\log 2\) endpoint must agree with the inverse
of the canonical exponential.  The linear Peano--Baker/Picard--Lindel\"of
uniqueness theorem will make that identification only after the individual
exponential presentations have supplied their own derivative and initial-value
certificates.  Thus the prospective refinement tests a capability the checked
finite row does not.
The Euler/complex-logarithm row above is a second, longer exp/log/ODE
corroboration; it remains unmarked until its explicit complex certificates are
present.  The arcsine/Newton and Gaussian rows remain future probes for
inverse/square-root integration and the exponential/full-line layers; other
advanced routes are intentionally omitted from this progress table.
